The Fall of Lorch
When Rome was Hamstrung in 233 AD


1 — The Lion
Sand kicked up as Thana and Toria closed the distance at a dead sprint, blades already rising, the crowd's roar loud enough to drown whichever of them screamed first.
Then a second roar cut under it — deeper, guttural, rolling in off the far side of the arena — and both women broke stride at once, blades still up, eyes cutting toward the sound.
A lion came through the gate, gaunt and ravenous, claws tearing at the sand, and every eye in the arena went with it.
Servius had owned and trained more than one gladiatrix in his years running the Kynegion, but the Greek woman, Thana, was the only one who had ever really riled him. He had grown tired of her arrogance. Tired of the smirk she wore into every bout, the one that said she already knew how the other woman would die before the first blow landed. Eleven fights, eleven corpses that weren't hers, and still that same insufferable calm.
This day, Servius had decided to pair her against the Hispanic firebrand, Toria.
He had rarely matched two of his own gladiatrices against each other before, not when both women drew a crowd and both crowds paid. The sun-bronzed Iberian was fast in a way Thana wasn't, vicious in a way the Greek's cold precision had never had to answer for, and he told himself the novelty alone would cover the loss. Two gladiatrices, one blade each, same armour as any other bout. Let the smirk answer that.
What he hadn't accounted for was the third woman.
Tacita was difficult to manage in ways that had nothing to do with defiance. She never argued or resisted, and she had never given the guards a reason to raise a hand. She moved through the compound quietly, often somewhere she wasn't supposed to be. She had been there three years, and not once had Servius heard her utter a word, or even make a sound. In that time she had learned to move silently, even when clad in bronze armour. There was something uncanny about her he could never put a finger on.
While Thana and Toria were led into the tunnel that opened onto the sand, Tacita had slipped away into the low stone corridor where the compound kept its beasts.
Back in the arena, the lion paused momentarily, sizing up the two women, neither of whom had been primed for fighting a beast this round. They had both participated in paired animal kills before, and their tactics switched immediately. They needed to divide the lion's attention to gain the advantage. Without a word, and not even so much as a glance towards each other, they peeled apart and approached from two different angles. The lion turned on Thana and immediately, Toria's falcata tore into the beast's emaciated frame. It spun around, snapping its jaws closed right where Toria's bare midriff had been moments before, and Thana buried her xiphos to the hilt between the protruding ribs. It was over in under a minute — messy, yet efficient. The crowd had just witnessed two gladiatrices turn a death-match into a shared triumph, and they roared for it.
In the tunnels beneath the arena, the animal handlers were unravelling. A lion loose mid-bout was a serious failure, and they knew exactly whose neck it would land on. Word went out fast, calling down every man they could pull from elsewhere in the compound to help account for what had gone wrong. Guards who should have been at the Porta Sanavivaria were suddenly absent.
When Thana and Toria came back in off the sand, still catching their breath, they found Tacita waiting for them in the passage.
Tacita simply beckoned and they followed.

2 — Out of Byzantium
The tunnels beneath the arena were built for animals and the men who managed them, low stone and narrow turns.
Thana and Toria still carried what they'd fought with. Disarming them hadn't occurred to anyone in the scramble to explain a loose lion — and so a falcata and a xiphos came with them into the dark.
Tacita was carrying a gladius, one she had taken quietly from the beast cages.
She led them without hesitating, through turns she seemed to already know, leading out through a low service door into the cool dark beyond the compound walls.
They had perhaps a hundred paces before the cry went up behind them. A shout followed by the sound of hobnailed sandals breaking into a run on stone.
Byzantium at that time was narrow lanes and blind corners, walls close enough on both sides to touch. Tacita cut left down a street that looked like a dead end, ducked under a hanging line of drying fish, vaulted a low wall into a courtyard and on into the street beyond.
It wasn't enough. Byzantium's watch knew their own city well.
Two men came out of a side alley ahead of them, blades already drawn, shouting for them to halt — and Toria didn't slow down. The falcata came up and around in the same motion as her stride, catching the first man across the throat. She rolled her wrist and brought it backhanded across the neck of the second, opening the jugular. Neither of them would be standing watch again, that night or any other.
Behind them, a third pursuer rounded the corner at a dead run and met Thana. She didn't have Toria's speed, but she'd never needed it. She sidestepped his swing, then lunged in close, and thrust the xiphos in under his ribs.
She was moving again before he hit the stones.
More voices behind them now, closer than before, torchlight starting to bounce off the walls at the mouth of the lane. Tacita caught Toria's arm and hauled her sideways through a gap between two buildings barely wide enough for a shoulder, and Thana followed. There was only time to run and trust that the woman who'd gotten a lion loose in a guarded arena knew how to get three women out of the city.
They lost the torches in the tanners' quarter, where the smell was bad enough that even a determined watch didn't want to linger, cutting through standing water and worse.
By the time Byzantium's outer wall rose up ahead of them, the pursuit had scattered into search parties combing the city, calling to each other across distances that told the women that the immediate danger was past.
They avoided the gate, and Tacita led them to a stretch of wall where the stonework had crumbled enough for a foothold. They scaled the wall, dropping into the scrub beyond, breathing hard.
They stayed close to the wall that night, watching the gates, listening for a search party to emerge.
The night was quiet, but at first light, a search party came out from the city gate, four men, unhurried, assuming the fugitives to be miles from the city by now.
Tacita spotted them first. She watched them long enough to learn their spacing, then went back for the other two.
The previous night, Toria and Thana had killed men in the dark of a city, out of necessity. This time was calculated, there was no need for speed. Four men went into that stretch of scrub looking for three fugitives. None emerged.
It would be some time before Byzantium realised that their search party was not coming back.

3 — The Wrong Wind
Traversing the goat tracks through the Haemus mountains took almost three weeks. The deep ravines and limestone scree was ground the three women soon mastered, learning how to use the natural landscape to hide their movement from Roman patrols. Several isolated waypoints along the military road unwittingly provided sustenance, and there were many Roman sentries whose last conscious sensation was cold steel on the throat.
As they reached the northern slopes, the rock fell away, opening out onto the steppe, where the ground flattened into wide pasture cut by dry washes, with long swells of yellow grass stretching toward the Danube. There were no limestone clefts to break sightlines here, so the women needed to alter their tactics, keeping low along the folds of the land and timing their movement between the ridges.
They picked up a patrol two hours before midday - six auxiliary infantrymen and a junior officer, marching west along an unpaved border trail. Thana, Toria, and Tacita shadowed the file from a shallow gully running parallel to the track, assessing their spacing and waiting for a bend in the road to close the distance. They kept below the rim of the wash, careful of their footing and maintaining absolute silence.
The patrol was approaching the bend, and the three women were preparing for the strike, when a voice spoke from the crest of the ridge directly behind them.
"You're standing in the wrong wind. They'll smell you before they see you."
Three heads turned together, but none of them flinched. These weren't women who startled.
A woman stood on the lip of the bank, holding the lead of a bay mare. She wore a lamellar cuirass of hardened leather plates, bronze-edged and embossed with medallion motifs at the shoulders, chest, and belt, along with leather greaves and boots, banded to match the cuirass. A baldric worn across her chest supported the gorytos at her hip, which held a composite recurve bow and a quantity of heavy, fletched arrows. Her horse stood still, ears turned toward the dust on the road.
"You've been tracking us," Thana said.
"I've been tracking the patrol," the woman said, with a tilt of her head toward the distant patrol. "Or attempting to, but you've been getting in my way doing it badly. I decided I'd fix that before it got one of you killed."
"We've left a trail of dead Romans behind us, all the way from Byzantium. I think we already know how to handle these boys, wind or no wind," Toria said.
"In close quarters, no doubt," the woman said, her voice unhurried. "Out here in the open it's different. They'll catch your scent fifty paces off and meet you with javelins."
"I'm Tigratavā," she said. "The patrol has already turned at the bend. If you want them, circle west behind the rise downwind from them. I can take the ridge, and we'll easily have them."

4 — The Falx
Weeks on the steppe had taught four women to move as a unit. Tigratavā still scouted alone, vanishing for hours at a stretch and coming back with information about the richest pickings. Pillaging a pair of Roman courier stations had provided grain and three sturdy mounts, putting each woman on a horse of her own.
By midwinter the Danube had frozen solid, the ice thick enough to support the horses. Tigratavā had crossed this way many times before to reach her lands on the northern bank, and she led them now, a little further west, through into the mountains.
Dacia in winter was bleak. It had been a Roman province for a hundred and thirty years, but the imperial infrastructure hadn't altered the wild landscape.
Tigratavā sighted the woman from a rise, downwind, unseen. She watched for longer than usual, then came back to the others with her report.
"One woman, wearing a fitted leather cuirass over a long tunic. She has a heavy fur mantle over the cuirass, close-fitted trousers, and high leather boots. She's clearly a local — the falx makes that plain enough, and the fact that she's standing over a dead surveyor and isn't hiding the body." She paused, turning something over in her mind. "She's not hiding herself either. Looks like she wants to be found. By the right people."
"And are we the right people?" Thana asked, amused by her own question.
"I think," Tigratavā said, "she will know the answer to that better than we do."
They found her just as Tigratavā had described, quite unbothered by their arrival, her grip on the falx unaltered.
Toria spoke first. "That's a strange place to leave a dead Roman."
"It's not for want of caring," the woman said. "I want the next one to see him."
It was a bold enough answer that Toria's mouth twitched toward something sharper — an edge she rarely bothered to leash when a stranger tested her — and it might have gone further than words if Tacita hadn't simply looked at her. Toria exhaled through her nose and let it go.
"I'm Toria," she said instead, and if her voice was a shade cooler than it had been a moment ago, the stranger didn't seem to notice. "We're headed north. You're welcome to ride with us, if you've a mind to."
"I have a camp a half hour from here," the woman said, with a glance that took in the four tired horses. "You'll want to rest your mounts before going too much further." She turned toward a picket line half-hidden in the brush, where a horse of her own stood already saddled, and swung up onto it without waiting to see if they'd follow.
Her name was Tiata, and her camp was better kept than four fugitives expected from one woman alone — dry wood stacked, water close, the fire built low and smokeless in a hollow. She moved through her own space with the same flat certainty she'd shown standing over the body, and soon the five of them were settled around the fire.
It was Thana who broke the quiet first, because something about Tiata's self-assurance needled her.
"You kill Roman surveyors for sport, or is there a cause behind it somewhere?"
"There's a province behind it," Tiata said softly. "A hundred and thirty years of one. You'd know something about causes, I'd think, given how loudly you don't have one."
Thana's smile didn't move, but her eyes narrowed. "I have plenty of cause. I simply don't feel the need to announce it to strangers with strange swords."
"Falx," Tiata said.
"I know what it is."
"Then you'll know it's not for show either." Tiata turned the blade slightly where it lay across her knees. "You wear your skill like it's an entitlement, rather than having earned it. I've known many Roman officers like that, and they tend not to survive contact with people who earn what they carry."
Thana's jaw tightened, and her voice was thin. "You don't know anything about what I earned."
"Then tell me," Tiata said flatly.
Thana looked at the fire for a long moment before she answered, and when she did, her face had become sober.
"I wasn't born to a cage," she said. "My family had standing — not wealth, but standing, in a town on the coast that many thought too small for pirates to bother with. We were wrong about that." She turned a piece of kindling over in her hands. "I was fourteen. They didn't take the town. A dozen men came ashore at night, took what they could carry that breathed, and were gone before anyone could raise an alarm."
"The market at Thessaloniki, or somewhere smaller?" Tiata asked, not unkindly.
"Smaller. It doesn't matter. What matters is the man who bought me didn't buy me for my arms or my back. He watched me for one day and he told his overseer I'd last longer than the fighters twice my size, because I wasn't going to make the mistake of fighting fair." Something almost like the old smirk flickered back, thin and bitter now. "He was right. Eleven bouts. Eleven women who thought strength was the only thing that mattered in that sand, and eleven times I let them believe it right up until it killed them."
"And the arrogance?" Tiata asked.
"It's not arrogance," Thana said. "It's arithmetic. I already know how most fights end before they start. I've simply never found it useful to pretend otherwise."
Tiata studied her for a moment longer, then nodded once. "Good," she said. "I can use arithmetic."

5 — A Leader
Tiata was the first awake. She had the fire stoked and food prepared before the others stirred. Soon all five were moving.
"Where are you headed?" asked Tiata.
"We don't entirely know," Toria admitted. "We started out running. That's simple enough to understand — Byzantium wasn't going to let three escaped gladiatrices stay as guests."
"We've killed enough patrols. Taken enough supply. Put enough dead officers back on Roman desks that someone has to explain them," added Thana. "We're not avoiding the empire anymore. We're hurting it, but it's only small cuts. Nothing it can't absorb."
"The cuts do add up," Toria said, her passion rising. "But there are garrisons out past where anyone bothers to look — frontier postings, undermanned, half-forgotten by whoever's supposed to be minding them. Hit the right one, hard enough, in the right place, and it isn't a supply raid anymore. It's a message that travels further than we do."
"You mean to make the empire flinch," Tiata said.
"I mean to make it bleed somewhere it can't hide the wound," Toria said.
Tiata was quiet for a while after that, turning the falx over where it lay across her knees.
"That's not a plan," she said. "That's an appetite with a direction. Which isn't nothing — it's more than most people manage. But it isn't a plan yet."
She paused briefly, then stood up.
"I'm not ready to move on this morning," Tiata said, as she banked the last of the fire with the flat of her boot. "There's a matter or two I haven't finished settling in this province, and I'd rather not leave it half done. But I wouldn't mind the company while I finish it."

6 — Old Ground
"A survey team consisting of six men," Tiata told them, as they broke camp. "An escort of four and two surveyors have been working their way along the old boundary stones. They're measuring the land so it can be given away — parcelled out to men who've never set foot on it, veterans owed land for service, officials owed favours. Once the stones are moved and the record's made in Rome, it no longer belongs to the people of Dacia. That's how a province disappears. Stone by stone, until one day the name on the map is the only Dacian thing left."
They were scouting the ground an hour ahead of the survey party's expected line, when Tiata turned to Tigratavā.
"You read ground better than anyone I've fought beside," she said. "Where would you put us?"
It wasn't flattery. Tiata didn't deal in flattery. It was a genuine ceding of judgement on the one thing Tigratavā had always done better than anyone else.
She studied the tree line, the fall of the ground, the place where the old boundary stones sat half-swallowed by grass.
"There," Tigratavā said, pointing to a shallow rise choked with scrub, downwind of the stones and low enough to stay unseen. "I would open from here. Thana and I can pin the escort from two angles. Tacita takes whoever's left standing. You and Toria go straight for the stones and the men measuring them, before they think to run with the record they've already made."
Tiata looked at the ground Tigratavā had marked out for a long moment, turning the plan over, and nodded once. "That should work."
The escort broke exactly as Tigratavā had said it would. Thana continued what Tigratavā started with cold economy, and Tacita moved through the confusion leaving no one alive.
Toria and Tiata came on the surveyors from the tree line together, and the falx and the falcata did their work.
One of the surveyors — older, grey at the temple, a man who'd clearly done this work in many provinces — simply knelt over the half-driven stake he'd been setting, hands empty, and said something in Latin before Toria's blade ended it.
She stood over him afterward with a distant look in her eyes.
"You understood him," Tiata said.
"I heard enough of it." Toria didn't look up. "He said it wasn't personal. That he was only doing the measuring — someone else decides where the lines go, he just walks them." Her jaw tightened. "Someone said something very like that to me once before. Different words. Same meaning."
She paused, her breath still coming fast.
"I was small when Rome came through my village," Toria said, at last. "Small enough that nobody explained anything to me. There'd been trouble — I never learned what kind, or whether it was even trouble my own people had caused. Rome doesn't bother finding out before it burns something down. It just puts down what's in front of it and calls the job finished."
She was silent a long moment, and her voice was subdued when she continued.
"I remember smoke. I remember my mother's voice stopping in the middle of a word. I don't remember the ship, or the market, or most of the first year after. Just that I was young, and quick, and worth more living than dead, and that was the whole of the mercy anyone showed me."
"And the surveyor?" Tiata said quietly.
"He was measuring the same kind of thing that was done to me," Toria said, vehemently. "Somebody's already decided where this land goes. He's just walking the lines so Rome doesn't have to admit that's what happened. I know that walk. I've been the thing at the end of it."
Tiata was quiet for a moment, then reached down and pulled the half-driven stake free of the ground, tossing it into the brush.
"Well, we're going to make sure these stones stay exactly where they are," she said. "Whatever else changes."

7 — The Cost of Silence
They returned to Tiata's camp, and Tacita built the fire, quickly and without wasted motion. She had always been the quietest of them, and by now the others had simply accepted her as she was, but Tiata noticed it differently.
"You haven't spoken since I joined you," she said, once the fire had caught and they had all settled.
Tacita didn't look up from the fire.
"You either don't speak because you can't, or because you've decided against it," Tiata said. "You're not the first kind."
Thana shifted, about to step in, but Tiata lifted a hand.
"I don't ask to be cruel," Tiata said. "I ask because I've noticed you're the one among all of us who never once has to be corrected. Tigratavā reads ground. Thana reads fights. Toria acts before she thinks and is usually right anyway. But you—" she paused, choosing her words carefully, "—you never do anything that needs undoing. That's not nature. That's discipline. And discipline like that is built, not born, whatever quiet you started with."
After a long silence, Tacita spoke, her voice low and raw. "I was always quiet."
Tiata nodded. "I imagine you were. But quiet isn't silence. Not this kind." She paused a moment before going on, more gently. "What made the difference?"
Tacita was quiet again for a long while. She was still looking at the fire when she finally spoke again, each word costing her great effort.
"My father," she said, "owed a debt he could not pay." She paused. "He found a way to pay it that did not cost him anything he valued."
There was a long pause before she went on.
"Rome bought what he sold," she said. "That is all there is."
A stillness stole over the little group, broken only by a soft reassurance from Tiata.
"That is enough."

8 — A Silent Station
They rose before sunrise, and over their first meal for the day, Tiata outlined a plan she had begun to formulate.
"There's an outpost northwest of here," she said. "A watch station, the kind Rome scatters along the frontier. A dozen men, perhaps fewer by now, forgotten by whoever's supposed to be rotating them out. If it stops reporting, someone has to ask why, and whoever answers that question has to explain it to someone above them, and so on, up a chain that doesn't like admitting a dozen of its own simply vanished off a frontier it swore was secure."
"How many men actually man it, that you know of?" asked Tigratavā.
"I don't know precisely. That's the first thing we need to find out before anything else." Tiata glanced her way. "You'll want eyes on it before we're anywhere near close enough to be seen."
"And after?" Toria asked. "Once it's fallen?"
"Rome sends someone to ask questions they cannot answer," Tiata said. "And somewhere far south of here, in a room we will never see, someone with far too much authority and far too little information starts wondering whether the frontier he's been assured is secure actually is." She paused. "That's the plan for now."
They broke camp and rode out into country that grew wilder with every mile — the road thinning to a track, trees closing in from both sides until the sky narrowed to a strip overhead.
Tigratavā found it the following morning — a timber watchtower and a low palisade wall, manned by fewer men than even Tiata had guessed.
"Wood, not stone," she reported. "One gate. The watchtower at the northeast corner, one man in it most hours, sometimes empty. They've gone soft here."
"Good," Tiata said. "Soft is what we came for. Now, we leave no survivors."
Tacita took the tower, going up and over the palisade in the dead hour before dawn. The man on watch never called out. He simply stopped being a threat.
Tigratavā remained on watch while the other three went through the barracks door together. What followed inside was fast, brutal work, men waking to their own deaths.
It was over before the sky had properly lightened. Nine men dead, and not one of them had gotten a shout past the walls.
They left it standing — gate shut again and palisade intact, with everything in its place except the men who should have been walking it. From a distance, riding past on the frontier road, it would look like nothing was wrong at all.
"How long before someone notices?" Toria asked.
"Depends how badly Rome wants to admit it's stopped watching its own frontier," Tiata said. "Days, or possibly longer, if the men meant to check on it are as soft as the men we just met."

9 — Out of Place
They crossed the frontier and began travelling northwest, cutting between the Iazyges plains to the west and the Hasdingi tribal lands to the east.
"I believe there are more remote outposts beyond these plains to the west," said Tiata. "The Danube is lined with heavily fortified garrisons between Moesia and Pannonia, so if we cut through the plains, we will reach the northern frontier again, further up where the resources of the empire are stretched thin."
It was Gothic country now; they expected little this deep in it beyond game trails and old grudges.
Tigratavā lay along a fallen trunk for close to an hour before she came back to report her find.
"There's a woman half a mile ahead, dressing a boar over a low fire," she said. "Alone. No camp markers, no signs of kin nearby, nothing that says she's expecting company or minding a herd for someone else." She paused. "She doesn't belong here."
"Meaning what?" Tiata asked.
"Meaning I've seen Gothic dress and Gothic weapons the whole way through this stretch of forest, and hers is neither. Fair enough hair for it, but the way she's built her fire, the way she's dressed — it's not local."
"How is she dressed?"
"She's wearing layered leather, riveted and quilted, with a wolf-fur collar at the throat, a wide leather belt, dark leggings, and tall leather boots."
Tiata considered for a moment. "Do you think she's dangerous?"
"I think she's competent," Tigratavā said. "She hasn't looked up once in an hour, which either means she hasn't noticed me — unlikely — or she has, and doesn't consider one watcher in the trees worth spoiling a meal over."
"If she's been taken from somewhere and dragged this far south, there are only a few ways that happens," Tiata said. "Gothic raiding parties often push north against the tribes along the Baltic coast. That's the likeliest story — captured somewhere up past where any of us have ever been, brought south, and somewhere along the way, she escaped."
"You've seen this before," Toria said.
"I've seen enough of it," Tiata said. "Rome isn't the only power on this continent that profits from moving people." She straightened up. "Let's go and find out."
Tigratavā led them in, quiet as ever, and the woman by the fire didn't so much as glance up until they were within a dozen paces of her — at which point she looked up, entirely unsurprised, as though she'd been waiting for them.
She was tall and broad, an axe within easy reach of one hand and a boar half-dressed in front of her.
"You've been standing in my tree line for over an hour," she said, in accented but serviceable Latin, nodding once toward where Tigratavā had been. "I was starting to wonder if you were ever going to come down and say something, or if I'd have to finish this boar alone."
"I'm Tove," the woman added, turning back to her work without waiting for a reply. "You're all welcome to join me, because there's more boar here than I can eat."
She fed the fire generously, and tore off the better cuts of meat, passing them along before anyone asked for more. She laughed easily at Thana's dry remarks, and joined in the verbal sparring with Toria. By the time the meal was down to bones and embers, she'd made herself the most immediately likable person any of them had met.
Eventually, Tiata asked what the rest of them had been wondering.
"You're not Gothic," she said. "You're not local to any tribe within a hundred miles of this fire. And you speak Latin better than most of the Romans I've killed for the privilege of practising mine. I'd like to know how you came to be sitting alone in this forest."
"I'm Rugii," Tove said. "Though I doubt the name means much to any of you this far south. My people hold the coast a long way north of here — lovely cold water, bitterly cold winters. The kind of place Rome's never once bothered sending a legion, because there's nothing there Rome wants badly enough to freeze for it." A faint, wry smile touched the corner of her mouth. "The Goths wanted it badly enough, though."
"Gothic raiders took you," Tiata said.
"Took quite a few of us, over a few bad years. I wasn't the only one, and I wouldn't have been the last." Tove turned a piece of bone over in her hands, not quite looking at any of them now. "They didn't keep me for themselves, not for long. I was traded on. Somewhere in all that moving, I ended up in the keeping of Vandal traders. Hasdingi, if the distinction matters to you."
"It does," Tiata said, quietly.
"Then you'll know they'd made themselves useful to Rome by then. Land for loyalty, or something close enough to it — I never heard the whole of the arrangement, only enough to understand that whatever I was worth to them, some part of that worth was owed, one way or another, to keeping Rome content." The wryness in her voice had hardened into a touch of bitterness.
"How did you get free of them?" Toria asked.
"Mostly by being underestimated," Tove said, the warmth creeping back into her voice, threaded now with real satisfaction. "People who move captives for profit get careless about which of us they think capable of anything beyond carrying what we're told to carry. I waited until that carelessness became an actual mistake, and then I made sure it cost them more than they'd bargained for, and I've been on my own since. Long enough now that I'd stopped expecting company worth keeping — until an hour of watching five strangers not quite manage to sneak up on me."
"Any interest in trading one kind of alone for a different kind?" Thana asked.
Tove looked at each of them in turn, before speaking.
"Sure. Where are we headed?"
"We're sure not heading back to the outpost we silenced," said Thana, with a laugh. "But we are going to keep dogging the Romans."
"If it's Romans you're after, you don't want to go any further north," said Tove. "All you'll find there is Goths and Vandals, and I've seen enough of them for one lifetime."
Tacita rose to her feet, drawing her sword. She held it out, arm level, blade steady, and turned until it settled due west.
Tove studied the line of the blade for a moment, then looked back at the others with a smile. "West," she said. "That suits me fine."

10 — The Devil’s Wall
They moved west across the Iazyges plains as the winter broke.
The plains were familiar territory to Tigratavā, and she read the tracks and the low swells of ground from memory. Tove rode behind Tiata, whose horse was easily the sturdiest of the five and bore the both of them without complaint. Twice they sighted Iazyges horsemen, who simply watched them from distant ridges. Six armed women moving at a steady trot across the open plain were more of a curiosity than a threat.
Where the plain broke against timbered hills, they struck the line of the Danube again. It was late spring by this time, and the river was running high and brown, swollen with snowmelt coming down out of the mountains to the west. Along this northern bank, the country belonged to the Quadi. They were client tribes, bound to Rome by treaties and annual subsidies, their roundhouse villages sitting close to the water where Roman auxiliary forts watched from the southern shore.
Moving through their territory meant picking a careful path.
"I think we should only move at night," Tiata said, looking down from a crest at the river road below. "The Quadi won't fight for Rome out of loyalty. But they'll trade five horses and six sets of iron to an auxiliary prefect if it buys them goodwill and grain."
For two weeks they moved under cover of dark, skirting the Quadi camps. From the ridges, they could see the small cooking fires of the native settlements on one side and the square, torchlit ramparts of Roman stone forts across the water on the other. Long summer daylight forced them to spend the middle of the days laid up in deep ravines, resting the horses out of sight of the trails. Once, Tigratavā held them flat in the brush while a Roman river galley drifted past on the current, oars dipping in steady, muffled thuds through the dusk, before they slipped back onto the track.
Beyond the Quadi lands, the ground rose into the rougher hills of the Marcomanni. The river bent away to the south, and as the Romans no longer had the river to mark the boundary, they had constructed a continuous stone wall.
It was limestone, four paces high, plastered white and running straight across ridges and gullies without yielding to the terrain, known to the local tribes as the Devil’s Wall.
They halted to study it.
"Which side do we take?" Tove asked.
"We'll stick to the outside," Tiata said. "Inside, we'd run into paved roads and dispatch riders every few miles."
"But out here we run into whatever tribes are pushing south," Toria pointed out.
"We can handle them," Thana said. "Whether they can handle us is for them to discover."
They tracked the wall westward through the forest, keeping just far enough in the trees to stay off the cleared apron along the ditch. Stone watchtowers stood every thousand paces, but the auxiliaries on the timber catwalks were sluggish in the midday warmth, watching the tree line without expecting anything to come out of it.
Near Dalkingen, the wall had something they had never seen on a frontier before - a gate built on the scale of an imperial palace.
It was fine sandstone, wide-arched and finished with carved mouldings, with an imperial shrine set over the main entrance. A bronze statue of the emperor Caracalla stood high above the portal, his back turned to the northern wilderness as he commanded the road leading into the province, the metal gleaming in the clear early autumn sun.
"That’s a lot of work for a gate in the woods," Toria remarked.
"It's an advertisement," Thana said, looking up at the bronze. "Rome builds things like that when it needs to convince the border it isn't losing control. It’s cheaper than marching three more legions up from the south."
"Good bronze to melt down, whenever someone gets around to it," Tove said.
Two days past Dalkingen, at a steep, wooded gorge where a creek cut through the rock, the white limestone wall came to a sudden halt. Taking its place was a wooden palisade made of oak trunks split and driven upright into an earthen rampart, with a ditch wide enough to break a horse's stride fronting it. It turned sharply north, running deep into the forest.
This was the Upper Germanic limes. And the Alemanni were constantly breaking through it.
Tigratavā found a recent breach less than an hour before sunset. A twenty-pace section of the split oak had been pulled forward and broken, scarred with old scorch marks from fire. It must have been breached recently, as no repairs had been carried out. Down in the bottom of the ditch lay two auxiliaries, stripped of boots and armour, left where they had fallen only days earlier.
"The garrisons must be badly undermanned here," Tiata said, looking down at the bodies. "They're not even holding the palisade."

11 — In Full View
Thilda had been scouting along the Upper Germanic limes, when she came across a wide, unguarded breach in the palisade.
She had witnessed Alemanni raids before, when scores of Alemannic warriors would charge out of the forest with axes and pitch brands, relying on their numbers to breach the palisade before too many of them were struck down by Roman shafts and javelins. The Romans would then have the breach stopped and guarded the same day, and fully repaired within a week.
There was something else about this breach that caught Thilda's eye. The Alemanni raided on foot, yet here were the distinct tracks of several horses, much more recent than all the footprints.
Deciding to follow the horse tracks, she soon sighted a thin wisp of smoke curling up from beyond a distant ridge.
The fire, when she finally worked close enough to study it properly from a fold in the ridge above, belonged to six women — all of them armed, their weapons close at hand. She watched the way they sat, the way their eyes never all rested in the same direction at once, the easy economy in how one of them fed the fire without looking away from the tree line.
These were women who had survived together long enough to trust each other.
She watched for the better part of an hour, before she made her decision.
She stood, in full view, and walked down toward the fire.
They were on their feet before she'd covered half the distance — fast, controlled, six weapons raised. She stopped well short of striking range and simply waited, both hands visible, spear planted upright in the dirt beside her.
"I'm alone," she said, in workable if accented Latin. "I've been alone a long while. I saw your fire three ridges back and decided to investigate."
A tall woman with a falx stepped forward from among them — the one Thilda judged to be their leader. "That's a long way to walk on a guess."
"I've walked further on less," Thilda said.
They didn't lower their weapons yet, and after a long, measuring moment, the falx-bearer nodded once.
"Sit, if you're going to," she said. "We'll decide the rest after."
Thilda sat. She didn't tell them everything that had brought her to this ridge, this fire, this particular late point in a war she'd been fighting alone. That would keep. Some of it would keep for a long time yet.
But she told them enough — that Upper Germania was her home and had been her people's for centuries, that the losing of ground here had been slow and then sudden, that she had buried more of her own than she could remember.
It was enough for now.
Someone passed her a share of the evening's meal — a smaller woman, quick-moving, who set it down beside her without ceremony and went back to watching the tree line. Thilda ate without hurrying, the first meal in a long time that she hadn't had to eat standing, weapon in hand, watching her own back.
"You've fought Rome directly," the falx-bearer said, at length.
"More than I've fought anyone else, this past year," Thilda said. "Though the tribes pressing from the north give me nearly as much work. Rome, at least, I understand. It wants land, and it's patient about taking it. The others just want what's left when Rome's finished, and they're not patient at all."
"That's most of this frontier, in one sentence," the falx-bearer said.
"You're not from here," Thilda said.
"True, none of us are from anywhere near here," she replied. "I'm from Dacia. Tiata."
Then nodding to the one with the falcata, she said, "This is Toria. Iberian-born. Gladiatrix escaped from Byzantium."
"Thana," Tiata went on, indicating the one with the easy, sharp smile. "Greek. Sold into the same arena."
"Tacita." A level glance from the quiet one was the whole of her greeting. "She usually lets her gladius speak for her."
"Tove," she said, nodding to the broad-shouldered one with the warm smile. "Rugii, from a long way north of this frontier. Taken by Gothic raiders, traded on to Hasdingi hands, then decided to leave them and wander alone in the woods."
"And Tigratavā," Tiata finished, nodding to the one already watching the tree line.
Tiata paused there. "Which is, I realise, a name I've often spoken, but have never heard the story behind," she said, an admission that seemed to genuinely surprise her.
Tigratavā's mouth curved, faint and dry. "You've never asked."
"That's not an answer."
"No," Tigratavā agreed. "It isn't."
She was quiet a moment, and when she finally spoke it was brief, exact, and nothing wasted.
"Roxolani," she said. "One of the Sarmatian peoples. My father led forty riders as auxiliary cavalry for Rome for eleven years. Paid in grain and little else. When a Roman patrol raided a village to the north, the governor decided my father and his riders should pay with their lives for something they didn't do." She paused. "I was moving the herd east at the time. Came back to a burned-out camp and forty unburied dead." She drew in breath and looked out into the distance.
After a few moments, she said, "There were other family groups I could have joined, but all were confederates of Rome." This last she said with obvious distaste, before brightening a bit. "I watched these three for the better part of a day before I ever spoke to them. Decided they were worth the trouble before they knew I existed. That's simply how I do things. It served me well enough on my own. It's served well since."

12 — The Fort of Lorch
The following morning, as they began preparing to move on, Tiata looked across at Thilda.
"You know this ground better than any of us," she said. "Before we decide where we're riding next, I'd rather hear where you'd take us."
Thilda considered before answering.
"If you travel north along the palisade, you'll find watch-posts, patrols, the occasional supply line worth bleeding." She nodded westward, away from the frontier entirely. "West, the ground changes. Fewer eyes and less discipline. It's harder country to move through safely. To the south, not far from here, is the fort of Lorch, which is right at the point where the palisade meets the wall running east. All the other forts have the frontier on opposite sides; either north and south, or east and west. Lorch has the frontier north and east, so they don't think they need to watch west or south. You'd be surprised how much Rome doesn't watch."
"The fort of Lorch," Tiata said, slowly. "I think we should have a look at that."
⁂
Tigratavā took the mare and nothing that could rattle or catch light.
It wasn't long before she came to a ford — shallow and worn smooth by long use. Crossing the stream, she continued south until she reached a ridge running west. Cresting the ridge, a valley opened out, and there below her sat the fort of Lorch, a large stone auxiliary fort, holding upwards of three hundred men. She spent the rest of the afternoon working the tree line in a slow half-circle.
It sat at the exact point where the frontier turned north, just as Thilda had said. On the west side, a supply track ran wide and open.
She marked the road and the blind approach, then turned back before the light went.
It was full dusk by the time she reached the hollow again, later than she'd intended. Tiata was on her feet before Tigratavā had cleared the tree line.
"You found it?" Tiata said.
"I found the fort," Tigratavā said. "Stone-built. Real numbers, not a skeleton crew." She looked at Toria. "It's the one you were imagining, back in Dacia. We can't take the fort like we took the watch station, but the western approach is very loosely patrolled. I think we could make them stop believing that road is safe."

13 — Released
They crossed the ford at first light, and made their way through to the ridge overlooking the fort. From there, they separated into smaller scouting parties. Tigratavā and Thana went to ground on the west side, within sight of the supply road and the blind stretch of wall behind it — the side Tigratavā had already read as open, and the side that mattered most to confirm before anything else. Tacita and Toria worked around to the east, where the wall was better watched, reading what a real approach from that direction would cost, in case it was ever needed. Tove and Thilda ranged the ground behind the western tree line, marking cover, water, and a line the horses could hold without giving anyone away.
What the west side gave up matched everything Tigratavā had reported. Wagons rolled through the gate on that side on a loose, trusting schedule, waved through without a challenge called down from the wall. No patrol crossed that ground all morning. Every patrol that did go out swung east and north instead, walking the line the fort had always watched.
"They simply don't look this way," Tigratavā said, watching a wagon roll through unchallenged.
Tove found ground worth using not far back from the road, on the blind side — a hollow with water close by, and thick cover on three sides.
"There's a patrol runs somewhere near this ground," Thilda said, once she'd paced the surroundings. "Small. Four men, maybe five. Not part of the northern loop — a stray from it, most likely. If we hold here quiet, we could take it tomorrow morning."
They discussed a plan, working out positions for each, and how the ambush would unfold.
Tiata turned to Tigratavā. "Tomorrow, then, first light, you'll want eyes on their—"
She didn't finish it.
Tigratavā was already on her feet, one hand raised flat for quiet, head turned toward the trees south of the hollow. A moment later the others heard what she'd caught first — voices, unhurried and very close.
"That's them now," she said. "Come further off their line than they should have, and headed straight toward us."
Tiata was up, falx in hand.
"Positions," she said. "The ones we marked for tomorrow. We'll use them now."
They moved fast and silent, each of them already knowing where the plan had placed her, where Tove's strength would matter, where Tigratavā's bow would open it. The patrol walked into it — unhurried, unaware — and the ambush took four of them down within a dozen heartbeats of the first arrow.
The fifth was young, and when Toria's blade came level with his throat he didn't raise his own to meet it. He dropped it instead, both hands open, shaking hard.
"Hold," Tiata said, before Toria's arm had finished its swing.
Toria held.
Tiata studied the boy a long moment.
"How long have you served here?" she asked, in workable Latin.
"Six months," he managed. "Less."
"Then you're fresh enough to still remember what fear feels like," Tiata said. "That's useful to me today." She nodded to Toria, who lowered the blade. "You're going to walk back to that fort. Not run — walk, so you have time to remember all of it. Tell them four of your patrol are dead and you're the one left to say so. Tell them seven women did it, in less time than it takes to explain, on the side of the fort nobody thought needed watching."
The boy said nothing. He seemed to have lost the power of speech altogether.
"Tell them exactly that," Tiata said. "Don't soften it for your own sake, and don't guess at anything you didn't see. Just the truth, plain."
They stripped him of blade and shield, anything that might let him claim afterward he'd fought rather than cowed, and let him go.
Tigratavā watched him go until the trees took him, then turned to the others.
"That wasn't silence," she said. "That's the opposite of the watch station."
"It was meant to be," Tiata said. "A silent station tells its own story. A garrison this size, with men left who'll ask questions when four of their own don't come back — that fort needs a story it can't control, told by someone who was there and can't be accused of exaggerating it." She looked toward the fort, still standing exactly as it had that morning, not yet knowing what it had lost. "Let them wonder what's coming next."

14 — Movement in the Dark
They left the ambush site and moved deeper into cover, but still close enough to keep sight of the fort. They settled in, rotating the watch three times during the night.
Tigratavā took the vigil during the hours around first light — the hours most difficult to remain alert, yet most likely to be fruitful.
She set herself further out than the previous watch, out past the edge of camp where the noise of a sleeper turning or a horse shifting its footing couldn't reach her. She wanted nothing to distract her.
For a long while there was nothing. The outline of the fort remained unchanged against a sky just beginning to turn grey. Not a single torch moved along it. Nothing.
Then something changed — she couldn't have said what.
She froze and strained every sense into pinpoint focus on the base of the wall.
There it was again. A movement, then nothing.
Tigratavā held her focus.
Again, slightly north — a flicker at the tree line. Then stillness — complete, absolute.
Tigratavā came back toward the sleepers, and touched Tiata's shoulder once.
"Something came off that wall," Tigratavā said. "Another movement near the foot of the wall, then a third further north, moving into the trees."
"Down," Tiata said. "All of you. Now."

15 — The Tigress of Lorch
Taran had learned, over the last three years, that captivity didn't need bars to be a cage.
She awoke that morning, as always, in the small chamber off the Praefectus's own quarters, dressed in the clothes required by the household, her red hair loose and flowing, as required.
Breakfast she took standing, near the door, as required. She didn't mind it. Standing let her see the room.
The morning brought two officers up from Raetia, minor men on some minor errand, and the Praefectus did what he always did when new eyes came through his gate — found a reason to have her brought into the hall and, in the particular tone he used only for this, mentioned that she'd been taken in Caledonia three years past, that she'd fought three of his own men to a standstill, that even now, three years tamed, she could probably still put a blade through a man's throat if she were given the opportunity.
He held out the practice blade, ordering her to show them what she could do.
She showed them. It cost her nothing to move well for an audience that thought they understood what it meant — proof of Rome's reach, proof that even the wildest thing dragged out of the furthest edge of the map could be brought to heel and kept, years from home, still red-haired, still dangerous-looking. She'd learned to give them exactly that and nothing more. The part of her that actually knew how to kill a man, she kept for herself, and these shows helped to keep her hand in.
The two officers left before midday, suitably impressed, and the household settled back into its ordinary rhythm — one she'd had three years to learn in every particular. She knew which guards drank in the evening. She knew the armoury was checked at dawn and rarely again before dusk. She knew the Praefectus's moods better than the rest of the household. She knew when he wanted her silent, when he wanted her shown off, when a bad week made him crueller and a good one made him softer.
She had spent three years watching all of it. She had no plan yet, but had made herself patient instead. She knew that she would recognise the moment when it came.
Dusk came grey and early that day, cook-fires already lit, and she was standing where she always stood at that hour — near the Praefectus, in the long room where he took his reports.
A lad was brought in still shaking — stripped of blade and armour, dust and worse on him, eyes full of fear.
The Praefectus heard him in silence.
Four of his patrol were dead. They were killed inside a dozen heartbeats, on the western ground nobody watched, by seven women who'd laid an ambush. They'd let him go. They'd told him to walk, not run, so he'd remember it right. They'd told him to tell it plain.
The room went very still.
Taran watched the Praefectus's face — the flush, the anger, the fear. He shouted something at an aide. He demanded numbers, demanded a search, demanded answers. Nobody moved with any real purpose. Everyone in that room had spent so long believing the western ground didn't matter that not one of them had the first idea what to actually do now that it had proven otherwise.
She stood very still herself, and felt a cold certainty settle in her chest that she hadn't felt in three years.
Seven women were on the western ground, waiting, watching, moving exactly when the moment offered itself and not a heartbeat before.
She had no idea who they could be, but she knew that whoever they were, they were the same type of woman as her — women who had been taken and measured and put on display for what other people needed them to be, who had decided, at some point, to stop being anyone's trophy and start being something with teeth of its own.
The Praefectus was still shouting when she slipped out of the hall. She took the passage that led to the armoury.

16 — Between the Sentries
She took a pair of matching blades — a little larger than daggers — and a couple of other items from the armoury. Getting them out of the praetorium was the easy part.
The fort itself was another matter. The praetorium sat at its centre, walls in every direction, and centre-to-wall was ground she'd only ever crossed under guard.
Once out of the praetorium, she slipped into a gap between the granary and the stables, and waited.
The garrison took its time settling. Voices carried a while longer than she'd have liked, and lights moved behind the praetorium's shutters long after they should have gone dark.
By the deep hours all was quiet. She moved to the outer wall, counting the intervals between the sentries on the rampart above her.
She found her place at the wall's lowest stretch, where old repair work had left the stone rougher, and went up it hand over hand, pausing with every hold to listen.
She stopped short of the top and hung there, flat against the stone, and waited for the two sets of footsteps above her to meet.
She'd chosen her spot for exactly this — the point on the rampart where the two sentries' paths crossed, where both men would be furthest from her at the other end of their beat. Their footsteps met and passed, and she waited until they were distant, then she went up and over, flat on the walkway for less than a breath, and dropped to the outer face.
She hit the ground at the base of the wall and went still, folded small against the stone, and did not move again until both rhythms had passed a second time.
Then she slid along the base of the wall, keeping its shadow, until she stood opposite a stretch of forest to the north.
She waited once more. The footsteps met and passed again. She crossed the open ground, reached the tree line, and froze, listening.
She held there a moment longer, Fort Lorch behind her for the first time in three years.

17 — Unfamiliar Ground
For three years she had been an object of curiosity in a household of military men. She had never set foot outside the walls of the fort. She had no idea what to expect here, apart from what one frightened soldier's account had told her - seven women, and four Roman dead, west of the fort.
When she judged she was deep enough into the cover of the trees, she turned west, and held that line.
The sky was beginning to turn from black to grey behind her, the outline of the fort falling further behind, to her left.
After several minutes, she turned south, keeping the fort to her left.
She hadn't gone far on the new bearing when she was stopped in her tracks by a blade at her throat.
She went perfectly still.
Silence.
Then a form began to materialise out of the dark ahead of her, large and foreboding, closing without a sound, carrying a weapon she'd never seen the like of — a curved blade nearly the length of her own arm.
"Tacita. Thank you." The voice was that of a woman.
The blade left her throat as quietly as it had arrived.
Taran didn't move. Whoever this was had not lowered her guard, the curved weapon held loose but ready.
"You came from the fort?" the woman said.
"I did."
"Alone?"
"Yes."
"Why?"
"One of the Praefectus's men came back to Lorch last night talking about seven women and four dead men. I was in the room when he told it. I've spent three years there, taken from Caledonia, kept as something to be shown off at dinner. I didn't wait to hear it twice."
"Anyone see you leave?"
"No."
"You're certain?"
"If they'd seen me, half the province would know it by now."
The woman studied her a long moment. "You're telling me you walked out from under the Praefectus's nose, scaled the rampart, and picked your way in the dead of night through a forest you'd never set foot in, to look for a band of killers — on the strength of a report from some deranged young upstart?"
"That sums it up well."
"That was either very brave or very stupid."
"I've had three years there to learn that the difference doesn't always matter. Only the outcome does."
The woman moved in closer. "Tiata is my name," she said. "You'll answer for yourself properly once we're back with the others. Until then you walk between us, and you keep both hands where Tacita can see them."
"That's fair," Taran said.
"It isn't meant to be fair," Tiata said. "It's meant to keep you alive long enough to prove you're worth trusting."

18 — The Eighth
Four of them stayed at the camp. Toria took the near ground, Thana beside her, both watching the fort's dark wall. Tove and Thilda held the far edge, facing the trees — because whatever Tiata, Tacita, and Tigratavā had gone out into the dark to find could just as easily come back out.
Tigratavā reappeared first, moving low and fast between the trunks. "A woman from Lorch. Tiata's bringing her."
Tiata walked the stranger out of the trees, Tacita a pace behind, and stopped in the open ground at the centre of the camp. "This is Taran," Tiata said. She looked at Taran. "Tell them your tale. See if they believe you."
Taran didn't hesitate.
"Three years ago they took me out of Caledonia. Since then I've belonged to the Praefectus at Lorch — paraded in front of his guests as proof of what Rome does to things it can't kill outright. Tonight a soldier limped home with a story about seven women and four dead men on ground to the west of the fort. I heard him tell it before the household. I decided this was my moment. Securing some weapons, I slipped out of the praetorium and waited until just before first light before scaling the wall. Nobody in that fort knows I'm gone yet."
Thana spoke first. "A woman doesn't cross ground like that on a soldier's rumour unless she's either got nothing left to lose, or she's very good at making people believe she doesn't."
"Both have kept me alive this long," Taran said. "Which one I am is yours to judge."
"That shouldn't be difficult," Toria said. "We seven aren't still alive through luck alone."
Tove didn't speak. Her eyes held the look of someone who remembered exactly how it felt to be measured this way.
Tiata turned to Thilda. "No movement from the fort?"
Thilda shook her head. "Nothing yet."
"Good. But it won't be long."
The grey in the eastern sky was already beginning to give way to shades of deep purple and burnt orange.
Tiata looked at the eight of them gathered in the half-light — seven who'd earned their place, and one other.
Toria was already moving, passing what little they had left between hands. Tove checked the horses, running a hand down each foreleg in turn. Thana kept her eyes on the fort. Taran ate what was passed to her gratefully.
Dawn broke and the fort remained silent — no riders, no alarm, no patrol. Whatever else was unproven about her, that much of her story was true.
Tove was the first to draw alongside her. She said something too quiet for the others to catch. Taran answered her, just as quiet. After a brief silence, Tove reached out an arm and drew Taran into a gentle embrace. For the first time since Caledonia, Taran allowed herself to relax.

19 — Not Possible
The Praefectus was clearly rattled.
"Numbers," he said to his aide, slower this time. "I want to know exactly who went out on that patrol, by name, and I want it before I decide anything else."
The aide went, and he turned again to the lad.
"The western ground, you say? Tell me again."
The lad told his account again. Nothing in the second telling made any more sense than the first.
The Praefectus doubted him — not the lad's honesty, exactly, but his sense. A recruit six months into his service, sent out onto ground he'd never patrolled before, could mistake a great many things in bad light.
"And you say it was women."
"Seven of them."
Women didn't do that. They just didn't — well, Taran had put three trained men to shame—
He glanced toward the wall where she usually stood, and found the space empty. Gone to the kitchens, likely, or sent for water. It didn't matter. She wouldn't have gone far.
But Taran was Caledonian. Whatever the wilds out past the northern sea bred into its women, it wasn't bred into — not into Roman women, who were subdued and quiet and knew their households, not even into the Germanic women along this frontier, tractable enough once a firm hand had been laid on them. Taran herself had proven it. Three years, and she stood by a wall and said nothing unless spoken to. Even the wildest of them came to heel eventually.
"There were seven, you said."
"Yes."
"It couldn't be right! Not even seven women from these parts could stand against one patrol of my men. If we were in Caledonia, I might give it credence, but not Germania."
"They didn't fight the patrol so much as end it," the lad said.
"And they sent you back," the Praefectus said. "Say that part again. What exactly did they tell you?"
"To walk. Not run. So I'd remember it right."
Not possible. He'd fought Caledonians — men who killed everything in reach and left nothing behind to carry a message. Whoever had done this had thought about him specifically, had decided what he should be told and how he should be made to hear it, and that was a cruelty he did not think possible.
The gate captain's voice carried from the passage outside, and behind it the tramp of the sentries being gathered in.
"Ah, here is the watch! So, this lad is of the belief that he is the sole survivor of this evening's western patrol. It's a fabrication, isn't it!"
Nobody could answer him straight.
One man had stood a rotation two nights past and sworn the gate hinge had complained without a hand on it, and put it down at the time to wind. Another had heard nothing at all, and said so emphatically. A third began an account of a shape at the tree line that unravelled halfway through into something he could no longer swear he'd actually seen, and stopped, red to the ears, rather than finish it. The gate captain tried twice to bring some order to the accounts, but voices climbed over each other, each man's fragment both feeding the fear in the room and refusing to amount to anything tangible.
The Praefectus felt the room slipping out from under him.
"Enough!"
The room went quiet.
"Whatever is out there," he said, "will keep till morning. Go to your posts. All of you."

20 — Vanished
The villicus was up before the household.
He worked through the morning's accounting — stores, staff, bread, water, the kitchen fires.
Taran should have been at her door by now.
He didn't think much of it at first. The whole household was off its rhythm this morning; she'd be a few minutes behind, that was all.
He went back to the stores, and a while later he checked again. No sign of her near the Praefectus's rooms, nor in the kitchens either.
He went to her chamber, and eased the door open. The room beyond it was bare. The bed hadn't been slept in, and what few things were hers were gone.
He stood in the doorway a moment, then headed straight for the Praefectus's chamber.
The Praefectus was already in discussions with his centurions, arguing over what to do about the western ground. An aide took one look at the villicus and turned him away.
"Not now," he said. "He's occupied. I'll call you."
So the villicus went back to the stores and waited. It was close to an hour before he was called.
By then the sun was well up, and the household had moved on without him. Men were mustering in the yard — shields were being checked, horses were coming up from the stable fast.
He found the Praefectus in the middle of it, half into his armour, giving orders.
"Speak quickly," the Praefectus said, not looking at him. "I've a garrison to move."
The villicus told him.
The Praefectus's hands stopped on the strap he'd been buckling. First seven women destroy a patrol. Now Taran was gone from her chamber.
"Gone," he said. "Where? Since when?"
"I don't know. Her chamber was empty this morning."
The Praefectus raised a hand, and the yard went still.
"Halt it," he said. "All of it. Nobody rides west until I know what happened inside my own walls."

21 — The Ambush
Marcellus had known his cousin since they were boys, and he had never seen him the way he'd looked that morning — armour half-fastened, giving conflicting orders.
He didn't argue when the command fell to him. Someone with a clear head needed to ride out west.
"Take what you need," the Praefectus told him, gripping his forearm. "Bring back the dead. Bring back the truth, whatever it turns out to be. I'd rather know."
Marcellus rode out with a full century, foot and horse both, and they soon found the site of the previous night's ambush.
There were four bodies, roughly where the lad had said. Marcellus dismounted and knelt by the nearest of them.
The wounds were clean. Whoever had done this was skilled, and they hadn't been afraid.
"Recover them," he said, straightening. "Gently. They rode out as my men. They come home as my men."
The century went to work. The horses stood patiently and weapons were laid aside.
He walked the ground while they worked — churned earth where more than four had clearly stood and fought, and then nothing. No trail leading off in any direction. Whoever had done this had left no trace.
A shout went up from the tree line to the north.
Marcellus was moving, and four infantrymen fell in around him. "Hold the recovery," he called back to his optio. "Cavalry stays with the dead. I'll see what this is."
He didn't get thirty paces into the trees when the man leading his file went down — an arrow found his throat, buried to the fletching.
"Down! Cover!" The infantrymen scattered.
There was no second shot.
Then from the south a second shout went up, followed by the sound of another man falling.
Marcellus understood that this wasn't the same threat. The distance between the two was too great to be the same archer.
He turned back toward the horses.
"Cavalry, mount!" he shouted, as he reached his own horse.
The other men were already swinging into saddles around him, horses shifting, unsettled by the shouting and the smell of blood already thick from the four dead being loaded. Marcellus had one hand on the pommel, and was swinging his foot over, when the horse reared beneath him.
It gave him no warning, and he was thrown off before he'd gotten his weight over the saddle, hitting the ground in a daze.
A woman broke from the tree line to his left, a spear levelled, and put it into the chest of the nearest mount. Another came from the right with an axe, swinging low and taking a second horse's legs out from under it — the animal going down screaming, the rider caught under it.
Behind him, at the rear of the line, two more forms emerged, blades already out. Marcellus understood. This attack wasn't aimed at his men. It was aimed at the horses. Every single one of them.
He rolled over and got a hand under himself, lifting himself up, when the next woman approached.
She came unhurriedly through the chaos, a massive curved blade held with two hands, and went straight for his own horse where it still reared and screamed above him — one swing, low and hard, and the animal's legs buckled under it, sending it down in a heap. She was already moving on to the next.
He tried to get to his feet, but a hand closed over his shoulder from behind, and a blade found his throat.
"Don't," a voice said, close to his ear.
A second woman appeared at his other side, matching pace as he was hauled to his feet and moved away from the wreckage of his century. She gripped his arm with a power he would never have expected from a woman, and a faint smirk lifted one corner of her mouth.
"You shaved well this morning, centurion," she said. "Don't make us do it again now."

22 — Captive Ignored
Tacita and Thana walked the centurion into the clearing, blade still at his throat.
The others had also slipped away from the scene of chaos, and they all converged on the campsite.
Tiata suggested they take Marcellus to a lone birch at the edge of the clearing, where the firelight thinned into shadow, and to bind his wrists behind it.
"We should get him talking," Thana said. "See what we can find out about how that fort actually runs. See if he'll tell us anything useful."
"He won't be able to tell you much," Tigratavā said. "My father's men lived under it eleven years — Rome calls it Disciplina, a man drilled to hold his place and take his orders, nothing more. He's just a spoke in the big Roman wheel. He won't know much more than his own patrols. He'd have no idea what's happening further up the line."
"He deserves a good thrashing anyway," Toria said. "He rode out this morning thinking he was in command. Let's give him something else to think about instead."
"He was in command, as far as he's concerned," Tove said. "Trouble is, if we knock him around, he'll tell himself he took it like a soldier, faithful to his Emperor. It'll just give him more to crow about."
"He and his cousin treated me like a cur," Taran said. "He deserves no better himself."
Tiata was quiet a moment, before turning to Taran.
"You know, Taran," she said, "you're right. He doesn't deserve another moment of our time."
She turned back to Thana and Tacita. "He might as well enjoy the birch for company tonight, and we'll see to him in the morning."
Tacita had held the blade steady at his throat all this time, and they now escorted him to the birch, where they sat him down and bound him. They both stood and walked away, Thana casting a single sneer back in his direction before completely ignoring him.
Tove had built the fire up higher than usual, feeding it with her usual generosity. Thilda sat close to the flame with her spear across her knees, unhurriedly working a nick out of the blade with a whetstone. Thana sat down and stretched her legs toward the fire, letting her head fall back, eyes half-closed.
"Four horses down before any of them understood what was happening," Toria said. "I've never seen a line come apart that fast."
"They never had a chance to hold anything," Tigratavā said, not looking up from the arrow in her hands. "They split their own line chasing two shouts that weren't where the real danger was. By the time they went back for the horses, half the century was still in the trees looking the wrong way."
Tiata sat a little apart, the falx laid flat across her knees. She'd already heard the whole of it once, walking back — every angle, every kill, exactly how the ambush had folded itself back into the trees.
It was Thana who turned the talk toward Taran.
"And our newest terror," Thana said, tipping her chin at Taran, the old sharpness softened. "South side, drawing off half their foot before any of them knew there was a fight. First real work with us, and she puts an arrow through a man's throat before he's gone ten paces into the trees. I'd call that a fair morning's introduction."
"I had the angle Tigratavā gave me," Taran said. "She read the ground. I only had to be where she said."
"You only had to hold and not flinch," Tigratavā said. "Most people flinch the first time it's real. You didn't."
Tove reached over and knocked a shoulder against Taran, warm and solid. "First time with us, and already better than half the recruits I've ever seen a Roman drillmaster claim credit for."
"I've held a bow longer than I've held anything else," Taran said. She turned the yew stave over in her lap, the wood worn smooth in places. "This very one. Just not for three years."
Even Tiata leaned in at this.
"I went into the armoury for the blades," Taran said. "I never knew what he'd done with my bow — never really thought about it."
She paused.
"It was hanging above his own armoury rack. Something for his guests to admire and be told about — the wild woman's bow, same as the wild woman herself." Her jaw set, hard, before she went on. "I never knew he'd kept it. Not until I saw it hanging there last night."
She ran a thumb lovingly along the grain.
"I took it down with my own two hands," she said, with a hint of satisfaction. "It still remembers my draw."

23 — Marcellus is Missing
The Praefectus stood at the tower rail, the sentry beside him.
He had told himself that a commander belonged where he could see the ground his men were on. Below, in the yard, were the men who had watched him flailing for control. Up here there was only the western ground in front of him, the sentry beside him, and the wooden rail beneath both hands.
The sun had gone and the sky was darkening. He had been up there since midafternoon, watching the same stretch of trees for hours.
"Sir, over there," the sentry said, pointing into the dark. "Coming out of the tree line. Low ground, past the old ford marker."
The Praefectus followed the line pointed out by the sentry, and could just make out several figures moving in a loose scatter, coming on foot.
"How many?"
"Can't say yet, sir. Too far. Could be a dozen. Could be less."
His hands closed harder on the timber rail as the line came on slow and uneven, some of the figures leaning on others.
"Send down," he said. "Tell the gate captain I want torches on the wall, and I want every man who can carry a light on that approach."
The sentry went down, but the Praefectus stayed.
He peered into the darkness again, but he could not see a single horse anywhere in the broken line.
Torches were lit along the wall below him, one after another, and the approaching line of figures stumbled in. He watched for the unmistakable gait of his cousin, but did not find it.
The Praefectus went down from the tower and out into the yard, where the gate stood open and the wreckage of a century was still filing through.
A man near the gate, blood dried black down one side of his face, told it start to finish twice in a row, and the two tellings didn't agree with each other. Another, further in, hadn't heard any shout at all — he'd been at the horse lines the whole time, he said, and the horses had simply started dying around him, one after another. A third began an account of the centurion going down and stopped halfway through it, unable to say with any certainty whether he'd seen Marcellus fall or not.
He went from man to man himself rather than wait for the reports to be gathered. He wanted the accounts raw.
Slowly and unevenly, he began to piece together the story.
The recovery had been underway. Then a shout from the north, and a man down before he'd gone ten paces past the tree line, an arrow in his throat from somewhere out of sight. Then a second shout from the south and another man down — too far to be the same archer. This had been a well-planned ambush.
And then there were the horses.
It was clear from the accounts that the cavalrymen had not been the target of the attack. It was the horses that had been targeted, which was a tactic he could not fathom. Blades to the legs, a spear into a chest, one animal after another went down, its rider thrown or trapped beneath it.
In the middle of a heaving mass of screaming horseflesh and struggling cavalrymen, not one of them spared a moment to see who was where. It was only back inside his own gate, under the light of the torches, that any of them realised that the centurion was not there.
"At first light," he said. "A party goes out. Full strength. We bring back our dead. All of them. And we do not come back inside these walls until we know that every man is accounted for."

24 — Cold Captive
Tigratavā caught the movement at first light.
Torches were lit at the gate, but there were more of them than a normal muster needed. She watched long enough to assess - more men than yesterday, nobody riding out ahead of the rest. She then went back for Tiata and Thilda without waking anyone else.
"They're moving again," she said. "Same ground as yesterday, by the look of the column. More men this time."
Tiata was on her feet in an instant. "How many more?"
"They're not expecting an easy morning, I'll put it that way."
They went together, low along the same ridge Tigratavā had used the night before, and settled in the trees.
The column came on slower than yesterday's had. Men rode close together, foot soldiers keeping pace with the horses rather than falling into their own line, eyes going to the tree line every dozen paces.
The recovery went slowly, every man working with a hand on his weapon. A shout went up at one point, and the whole column went still for a full half-minute.
"They're jumping at nothing," Tigratavā murmured.
"That's the point," Tiata said. "Yesterday cost them a centurion and however many of that column they're still counting. Today they don't know if it's going to happen again, or when, or from where."
They watched the whole of it — the wounded horses put down where they lay, the dead loaded with more speed than care, a count taken and confirmed, men calling names back and forth across the field.
They watched the column go, then returned to the camp.
By the time they were back, the morning at their own fire had already moved on without them.
Marcellus looked like he hadn't slept at all.
Tiata went up to him and, crouching down, cut the binding at his wrists and ankles.
"The armour comes off," she said. "The tunic under it, the greaves, the braccae, the boots, the cloak. All of it."
"But I'm a Roman officer—"
"You were," Tiata said, and waited.
He gave it up piece by piece, hands shaking. When it was done there was a pile at his feet, and a man kneeling on the ground in an under-tunic, shivering in the cold breeze coming in from the east.
Tove dropped a bundle of grey wool on the ground in front of him. "That's the choice," she said. "Wear it, or don't. I don't care either way, but you might."
It was a peasant girl's dress, plain, worn soft at the seams. He looked away.
"I'll manage," he said.
Thana gave a snort. "That's what he thinks now."
They rebound his bare ankles, and bound his wrists in front of him this time. He was left at the birch while the camp went on exactly as it would have without him.
Taran strung the bow herself that morning, turning it in the light to check the tiller, and slung it across her back when she was satisfied. She sat when she wanted to sit and stood when she wanted to stand. She laughed and she joked. She crossed in front of him a dozen times through the morning, each time ignoring him.
By midday full cloud cover had blocked out the sun, and the wind was coming from the north.
"The Praefectus's silver boy is beginning to tarnish," Thana said, as she lounged by the fire. "He's going purple around the edges."
He was shivering uncontrollably now, but said nothing.
The light went early under the trees, and before long, he was shivering so hard his teeth were chattering.
"I'll take it," he stammered, when Tove walked past.
Tove bent down and picked the wool up off the ground. "Can't have you catching cold on top of everything else, can we now."
"Taran," she called. "Help me dress the sweet lad, if you don't mind. I think he took his virtus off with his armour and he wants his dress on now."
Taran laughed as she came across to help, bringing a blade to cut the binding from Marcellus's wrists. He watched her hands as she worked the blade. He knew those hands well — three years hanging idle at a wall, offered a practice blade only when his cousin wanted sport. Now he was watching them deftly handling a blade he must have recognised as belonging to his cousin.
"Arms in the air," said Taran, as she slid the dress down over his head.
"There we are," said Tove, chucking him under the chin. "That's much warmer than virtus, isn't it."
"What's virtus?" asked Toria.
"Virtus? It's a joke," said Tove.
Toria looked baffled. "I don't get it."
"It's the Romans' idea of what makes a man," said Tigratavā. "They think only men have the ability to act decisively under pressure."
Toria burst into laughter. "He made a decision all right."
"Virtus!" said Thana, between snorts of laughter, "looks more like a virgin now!"
They were all laughing now.
"Better tie it to the birch again," said Thana, wiping her eyes. "It'll shiver itself out of its sack, otherwise."

25 — Gone
The party had ridden out at first light, and the Praefectus had stayed in the yard through the whole of the morning, until they returned.
The Praefectus approached the nearest rider.
"Well?" he said. "Well?"
The rider was young and it took him a moment to understand he was the one being addressed.
"Sir, we recovered six more of the fallen, as well as the four fallen the previous evening. The ground was—"
"Is he among them?"
"No, sir."
"Then where?"
"We don't know, sir. We searched as far as—"
"You searched," he bellowed. "You searched and you come back and tell me you don't know."
"Sir, every man who rode out yesterday is accounted for now. Six dead. Fifty-four returned. Centurion Marcellus is the only one who isn't."
"Then go out again," he said. "Now. Every man who can still sit a horse."
"Sir, the light—"
"I did not ask you the hour." He had hold of the rider's arm now. "You will turn this province inside out before you tell me a man of mine isn't anywhere. Men don't simply—"
"Praefectus."
Centurion Rufinus had come up behind him, and touched his arm.
"Let him go, sir. He's told you what there is."
"There is more. There has to be more."
"There isn't. Not today. Not from him."
The yard had gone quiet. The litters were coming through. A horse shifted somewhere behind him and whinnied.
"Sir," Rufinus said. "Not here."
"I am not finished—"
"You are finished for now, sir."
Centurion Draco took the other arm, and between the two of them, they took him into the record room off the praetorium and shut the door.
It was several minutes before he was calm enough to speak rationally.
"They saw that," he said.
"They saw a man who's lost more in two days than most commanders lose in a year," Draco said. "It will pass."
"It will not pass. Four men. Then a woman gone from my own house without a trace. Then a full century sent out and come back in pieces. Now this. My cousin. Gone."
Neither centurion had an answer to this.
"You need to send word up the line," Rufinus said. "Tonight. Before it's a week old and looks like something you sat on."
"And say what?" He sounded exhausted. "Say that seven women ended a patrol and left a witness on purpose. Say that a woman kept three years under guard walked out of a secure fort leaving no trace. Say a centurion, my own cousin, vanished off a battlefield, right under the noses of his own century. Say that the same century returned in total disarray, having been shattered by the same hands — by seven women. Say that the entire cavalry division of that century returned on foot, having had — every man of them — his steed cut down beneath him. And I have no consistent account of any of it because not one man among them saw it clearly enough to give account." He looked at them both. "Tell me which part of that I write down, so whoever reads it doesn't think I've lost my mind."

26 — Nobody There
On the first day, Marcellus flinched at every shadow that crossed near the birch.
By the fourth, he'd stopped noticing anything around him.
He ate what was placed in front of him — bread, a strip of dried meat, whatever was left over — without looking up. He drank when the waterskin appeared near his knee. His wrists had been unbound since the second day.
Toria passed within a foot of him twice a day, on her way to the horses, and he'd stopped turning his head to follow her.
Thana had stopped needling him somewhere around the fifth night, only because she no longer got a response.
He answered Tiata once, on the sixth morning, when she crouched in front of him and asked whether he was still capable of hearing her. He said something none of them could understand.
He had simply become a fixture of the camp — tethered, fed, and otherwise unremarked.
⁂
Six days had passed and no patrol had crossed the western ground, yet the women made sure the fort knew they were still there. An arrow went into the gatepost on the fourth night, about two fingers from the sentry's shoulder. On the fifth day, a figure stood at the tree line in full daylight long enough for three separate men on the wall to swear to it.
On the seventh day, Tigratavā caught the muster leaving the fort just after sunrise, and was back at camp with the word before the column had covered a third of the ground.
"They're coming," she said. "Full strength again, and the Praefectus riding at the head of it this time. They are making straight for this camp."
There was no panic — the horses stayed tethered exactly where they stood, saddled and waiting.
They all slid silently into the tree line and watched the column approach. Tacita stayed at the birch until the column was almost on them.
She cut the tether at Marcellus's ankles with one stroke, and vanished into the foliage.
⁂
Draco had argued against the Praefectus riding out himself, but Rufinus had only asked how many men he wanted at his back, and set about arranging them.
They approached with extreme caution, every man watching the trees. None of them had expected an easy day, in fact, many of them expected not to come back to the fort alive.
They found the camp — a fire still smoking, bedding laid out around it in a rough circle, tack and gear stacked. Horses stood tethered at the tree line's edge, saddled and waiting.
A form lay near the birch, half-curled on the ground. The grey wool dress Tove had thrown at his feet days before hung off his shoulders. He had no bindings on his wrists, nor at his ankles. There was nothing holding him there at all.
The Praefectus was first into the clearing, and first to the form on the ground. He went down on both knees beside it and turned the face up out of the dirt — the same man whose forearm he'd gripped at his own gate, the same Marcellus he'd grown up alongside.
Marcellus's eyes found the Praefectus's and held there a moment, flat and still — without recognition. He said nothing. He made no sound at all, even when the Praefectus said his name.
Rufinus bent down and took the Praefectus by the arm. "Come, sir."
He rose quietly, and the men behind him stooped, lifting Marcellus between them.
Every man in that clearing had spent the walk braced for an arrow out of the trees, a dart from somewhere unseen. They had come prepared to do whatever was necessary to secure Marcellus — to fight for him, to buy him, to beg for him.
They didn't linger to search the tree line. They left the way they'd come, every man among them certain that eight pairs of merciless eyes were watching their every move.

27 — Rome on Its Head
The last frightened Roman was barely out of earshot before the clearing rang with laughter, and those eight pairs of merciless eyes finally let the amusement show.
"I've fought plenty of men who feared dying," Thana said. "This is the first time I've built one that fears living."
Tigratavā nodded. "A man afraid of dying is done costing you anything the moment he's dead, but a man afraid of living spreads it into every room he sits in, for as long as he lasts."
Thana laughed. "I won't argue that."
"I keep thinking about tonight's report," Toria said. "The one the Praefectus has to write now, once his hand stops shaking enough to hold the stylus. What does a man like that even say? I lost a patrol. I lost a woman I kept three years and never once thought could leave. I lost half a century, and now I've lost my cousin's mind. I'd almost pity the wax."
They all laughed.
"It won't stay at Lorch," Thilda said. "Whatever he writes goes up the line to someone who's never smelled that fort's air. That man reads numbers he can't reconcile, and he has two choices. Believe it, and admit the frontier's rotting. Or doubt it, and hope the next report proves him right. Either way, he won't sleep well either. Fear moves up a chain slower than it moves down one. But it does move up."
"That's the part I like best," Tove said. "Not just Lorch. Whoever sits above the man who sits above the Praefectus. Some official who's never thought about a frontier fort by name, holding a dispatch that says eight women undid a century and walked a Roman officer home in a dress, and nobody can say how it was done." She shook her head. "That's no longer just a bad week for one Praefectus. That's the whole of Rome on its head."
"You're imagining a very large collapse from one very small dress," Thana said.
"Am I? Rome's whole trick is convincing everyone that who stands over whom can never change. Man over woman. Captor over captive. Citizen over everyone who isn't. Dress one man exactly the way he'd have dressed one of us, tether him exactly the way he tethered Taran, and you haven't just humiliated a centurion. You've shown every man who believed in that order that it isn't fixed. It only looked fixed because nobody had yet had the nerve to turn it around on them."
Thana let her head fall back against the log and gave a dry snort. "Maybe not on its head just yet," she said, the old smirk returning. "But we've got it on its knees, at least."
Thilda looked up, glancing south through the dark toward where Lorch lay hidden behind the ridges. "Right at the knee of their own frontier," she said.
She turned to Taran. "You probably heard them talking about the knee, did you?"
"That's exactly what they called it," Taran said. "Genu. The Knee. The officers cursed it every muster. Said a wall with a crook in its leg was twice the trouble to guard."
Thana let out a short laugh. "They were too busy watching the knee to see us coming up behind the hamstring."
"I only care what it's done to a brute who used to hand me a practice blade and call it generous," Taran said, her eyes narrowing. "For three years, he stood there and let his cousin show me off like a hound that had learned to heel. Now half a garrison lies awake wondering if the thing they can't see is standing at their own door." She smiled a thin smile with no warmth in it. "Stand the empire on its head if you want to. I only wanted the one house to know it isn't as safe as it thought."
Tiata had let the whole of it run without steering it. Only now did she weigh in.
"All of that is true, and exactly how we meant it to be," Tiata said. "But Rome doesn't come apart because of one dead century, or one broken man, or one report a Praefectus can't bring himself to write plainly. It comes apart from years of it, done over and over, by people it never thought worth watching. Tonight was one weight added to the pile. There'll be others before we're finished."

28 — Moguntiacum
Priscus had been cornicularius for six years.
He worked through the week's dispatches at his table — a requisition for tent leather, a complaint about the grain measure at Nida, two decurions arguing in writing over a horse neither of them had paid for. Under those sat a thicker stack, tied with cord, that he'd been putting off since morning. Reports off the limes filled it, fort after fort, Alemanni raiders attacking and destroying at will.
He cut the cord and read them in order. Men dead, numbers given exactly. Ground lost, or held at a cost the writer wanted noted. Some asked for grain. Some asked for men. Some asked for neither, wanting only the loss set down on record.
The dispatch from Lorch was near the bottom, its wax seal already broken by whoever had carried it this far.
A patrol destroyed west of the fort, one man released alive to carry word of it back. A captive Caledonian woman gone from the Praefectus's own household that same night. A century sent out to recover the dead from that patrol, ambushed in turn, every horse under its mounted division cut down. And the centurion who'd led that century — the Praefectus's own cousin, the report made a point of saying so — gone missing in the same ambush, not found, though the search had covered every foot of ground his men could reach.
Priscus read it a second time, tracing the lines with one finger.
Cassius had the next table, a tribune six months into the posting, a stack of wax tablets in front of him from the morning's work.
"Cassius. Read this one."
He came over without putting his stylus down, and read it standing, lips moving slightly.
"Peculiar," he said.
"That's the word I had for it."
"The patrol, the century, the missing centurion — that's one enemy, one fight, spread over a few bad days. He writes it plainly enough." Cassius tapped a line partway down the sheet. "A captive escaping in the night is unlikely to be tied to two ambushes and a missing man, yet he seems to think it's all one and the same."
"Why would Alemanni raiders abduct a single Caledonian woman?" Priscus said.
"He's lost his cousin," Cassius said. "More than a cousin, from the way he writes about him. Grief does strange things to a man."
"So it goes on the pile with the rest?"
Cassius shrugged, already turning back to his own table. "We've had worse than this off that stretch this month, from men who kept steadier hands while writing it."
Priscus set the Lorch dispatch down with the others — Dalkingen, Abusina, a granary burned near the Nida road — and reached for the next in the stack.
⁂
More than a week passed before the second dispatch from Lorch came.
It came in with a courier at dusk — a single sheet of papyrus, shorter than the first had been, the wax seal still soft enough that Priscus's thumb left a print in it when he broke it. He read it standing at the window, the last of the light going grey outside.
The centurion had been found alive. The first recovery, sent for four dead, had cost the fort the centurion himself. The second, sent the next morning, brought back ten bodies and confirmed he was the only man still missing. Then six days had passed with no patrol sent out at all, before the Praefectus rode out himself with every man who could still sit a horse. They found a camp — fire still smoking, horses saddled — and the centurion lying near it, alive, stripped of his armour, and out of his mind. He hadn't spoken since. The physician at Lorch had no explanation for his condition.
Priscus carried the dispatch to Cassius's table himself.
"The Lorch report. From a fortnight back."
"I remember it."
"The cousin's turned up."
Cassius set down his stylus. "Alive?"
"Alive. Not right in the head." Priscus held the dispatch out. "Read it."
Cassius took it to the lamp and read it slowly.
"That's not grief," he said, when he'd finished.
"No."
"He writes that six days passed with nobody willing to cross that ground at all. Then he writes that when they finally did, they found a camp — fire still smoking, horses saddled, gear stacked as if whoever left it meant to come back for it." Cassius set the dispatch down. "Six days of nothing, and then a camp that is obviously still inhabited, but no inhabitants."
"He's held that fort three years. Longer than most out there."
"A young man losing his nerve after a bad week is nothing," Cassius said. "This one served on the Caledonian frontier before Lorch. He's no novice, and every other report off that stretch reads like the rest of them. This is something different. We need to send a beneficiarius out to Lorch to see what's happening."

29 — The Beneficiarius
The position of beneficiarius fell under no centurion. He answered directly to a governor's office, and took whatever posting he was given. Some months he might have none at all. When a report needed checking in person, he would go and check it, and write down what he found.
Ligarius was one such beneficiarius. He had done this work in three provinces before Germania Superior, and had been recalled to testify at two courts-martial on the strength of his reports.
Most of his postings were unremarkable. He had settled a dispute over a quartermaster's numbers, and once found that a death on the road blamed on bandits was a quarrel between two soldiers. Generally, that was about as far as his role took him.
Priscus came to his table one morning in early autumn, two sheets of papyrus in hand.
"Lorch," he said. "You'll want to read both of these before I explain any of it."
Ligarius read the first, then the second, taking his time over both.
"What else do you know?" Ligarius asked.
"The Praefectus there has held the post three years, and his previous posting was on the Caledonian frontier," Priscus said. "He's no novice. The tribune thought it was grief. I let it go on that understanding, the first time. I'm not willing to let it go a second."
"On what grounds?"
"The second dispatch." Priscus leaned over and tapped the sheet in Ligarius's hand. "A found man should be a relief. Instead he's a fresh problem. He can't explain the six days nobody moved, the camp still warm with nobody in it, or what's wrong with the man he found." He paused. "A man losing his composure over a death, I can understand, but not this."
"You want me to go?"
"Someone with a clear head needs to go to Lorch, and ask the questions the Praefectus doesn't seem to be able to answer."
Ligarius took the dispatches to carry with him, and left the following day.
The road southeast ran well for the first day, then narrowed and roughened as it left Rome's settled country behind. Ligarius rode alone, a single packhorse behind him, and crossed in four days without incident.
Lorch didn't look like a fort under siege. The walls stood intact. The gate was manned. Nothing about it explained two ambushes, a missing woman, and a centurion out of his mind.
The sentry who took his name at the gate skipped the routine questions — travelling alone, what business, how long he meant to stay. He waved him through and kept his eyes on the tree line west of the fort.
The Praefectus received him immediately and didn't try to hide his relief. Most men in his position treated an investigator's arrival as an accusation before a word had passed between them.
"You've read the dispatches," the Praefectus said.
"Both."
"Then you already know most of it." He said it without shame. "Ask what you need. I've nothing left to hide."

30 — The Rider
Tigratavā had watched the fort from the same ridge for eleven days, taking the dawn hours because that was when a gate crew was most likely to be tired and careless. Most of what she'd seen in that time hadn't changed - a sentry walking the rampart, smoke starting from the kitchen fires, no patrol willing to cross the western ground. This morning a rider came down a trail from the northwest.
He rode alone. A packhorse followed him on a lead, loaded with two hide cases and a bedroll. No escort came with him, and his pace didn't match a courier's — couriers rode fast and pushed their mounts hard; this man came on at a walk.
His horse was better fed and better shod than anything she'd seen a common soldier ride. He wore a plain travelling cloak, no armour visible under it, and carried a leather document case slung across his back.
The gate opened for him when he reached it. She had watched that gate for weeks. Every other arrival — patrol, courier, muster — had been made to stop and answer a series of questions before the gate moved. This one was barely stopped at all.
She went back and told Tiata what she'd seen.
"Was he questioned at the gate?" Tiata asked.
"No. It opened for him as soon as he reached it."
"Any second rider? Held back out of sight?"
"None that I saw."
"How was he dressed?"
"A travelling cloak. No armour showing under it. He carried a case for documents on his back."
Tiata sat with this for a while before she spoke again.
"Someone at Lorch must have known he was coming, and had told the gate to let him straight through," she said.
"Either that, or they recognised him," Toria said. "Perhaps he's an officer sent from another fort."
"An officer travels with at least one man at his back, usually more," Tiata said. "This one came alone, with nothing but supplies and papers. That rules out an officer. It could still be a man the gate crew knows by sight — but a familiar face doesn't explain the case of documents, or why he came alone. I'd rather read him as someone sent to look at what happened here and write it down — someone who answers to an office above the Praefectus."
"So he's here to ask questions," Thilda said.
"That's what that looks like," Tiata said. "It's also exactly what we set out to achieve. It doesn't mean we've won." She paused, then added, "Yet."

31 — Eight Bedrolls
Ligarius spent the first day on testimony, which included spending time with Marcellus himself.
He had asked to see the centurion and was taken to him — a man sitting upright, watched over, silent, eyes open and fixed on nothing. The physician had nothing to add beyond what the second dispatch had already said, but Ligarius was certain he could see a spark of knowledge in the otherwise vacant stare. He asked if he could be alone with the centurion, and the guard withdrew to the outer chamber, closing the door behind him.
Ligarius sat opposite Marcellus for a time, saying nothing. He watched his eyes, and he could tell Marcellus was not entirely catatonic, yet there was something strange about his stare. He tried to reason with him, asking what he remembered of the ambush. Marcellus lifted his gaze momentarily, then his eyes dropped again.
He asked him what he remembered of the six days in the camp of the attackers. For a moment, he thought he had broken through. He was sure he saw Marcellus forming the word "attackers" with his lips, but there was nothing more.
Ligarius decided to change tack. He stood over Marcellus now and raised his voice.
"Does Disciplina mean nothing to you?" he shouted. "How can you claim to be a centurion when you allow your men to walk back to camp in disarray and without you? A Roman soldier holds his order even in captivity. How much more a centurion?"
Marcellus faintly shook his head, but there was no other response.
"Where is your Virtus? I've seen the dress. How could you have allowed such a thing?" He leaned down and spoke directly into Marcellus's ear. "And your Fama is destroyed. Do you have no regard for your father's house? Have you not thought of the man who put his confidence in you? You have brought shame on yourself, and your cousin, the Praefectus, is a ruined man. Do you not care about how your men will remember you?"
He sat down again, opposite Marcellus, and watched him. For a few moments there appeared to be no response. Then Marcellus lifted his head and looked Ligarius in the eye.
"Feminitas outweighs Disciplina," he said. "I have seen Virtus in women beyond anything you or I will ever have. Fama has no more substance than the morning mist."
Then his chin dropped to his chest, and he said no more.
Ligarius sat watching him for a few moments longer, turning the words over in his mind, but he couldn't make any sense of them. Whatever fever had produced them, it wasn't anything he'd encountered before. He rose silently, and left the chamber.
⁂
The second day he asked for an escort to take him out to the western ground, but the Praefectus was unable to raise one. The men simply refused, one excuse after another, until the Praefectus ordered Rufinus to select four men he could trust, and give them the option of forming the escort or immediate dishonourable discharge. Rufinus was also ordered to accompany the escort.
The clearing where the ambushes had taken place was exactly as described in both dispatches — ground churned wide by more than one fight, and drag marks all running the same direction, toward where the recovery party's carts must have stood. All of this was as he expected, but there was absolutely no clue as to the identity of the assailants.
After he had combed the ambush site, he asked the escort to take him to the place where they had found Marcellus. The escort brought him within sight of the camp and refused to go any further. They simply would not take another step closer. Rufinus reiterated the threat of immediate dishonourable discharge, but all four said they would rather dishonourable discharge than face what Marcellus had faced. Ligarius had never witnessed terror like this in seasoned soldiers before. Even Rufinus insisted that he needed to stay with the men to prevent them running. So Ligarius approached the camp alone.
He stepped cautiously out of the tree line, into the clearing that was clearly a lived-in camp, laid out exactly as the second dispatch had described it — the fire was burning low, eight bedrolls arranged around it, five horses tethered nearby, calm and well-cared-for.
He looked again — eight bedrolls.
The lad had said seven. Ligarius had gone back over that testimony more carefully than any other he'd taken, because it was the only account not given in the middle of a fight, yet here were eight bedrolls. Either the lad had been in error, or an eighth had joined them. Could this be the Caledonian captive who vanished?
He knew the woman only by report - three years a captive, taken from Caledonia, kept for display rather than labour. He had built his working picture of this band from the only material available to him — local, frontier-born women, if they were indeed women, who shared a grudge against the local Roman fort, but a Caledonian would have no place among them.
She had no kin on this frontier. No tribe here that he knew of had connections with Caledonia. By every account, she had stood three years at a wall in that fort and said nothing to anyone beyond what was required of her. He couldn't fathom how a foreigner could join a band of locals, who would then trust her enough to fight beside her the very next day. In his experience, that kind of loyalty took years to build.
Even granting the eight — granting Taran, granting whatever local grievance had raised the rest — he could not make the numbers answer for what had been done to Marcellus's century. Two ambush points had worked in coordination, timed apart just far enough to split the response. Every horse in a mounted division had gone down inside minutes, in a fight fifty men had not been able to hold together long enough to protect their own mounts. A centurion had been taken from under the noses of his own escort, and not one man among fifty had seen it happen. This was command — someone reading the ground, dividing the labour, timing the strikes to land when the confusion was thickest. He had questioned officers, in three provinces, who could not have managed that with a century of trained men under them.
He walked back to the fort as the light went, putting the report into its final order in his head. He would write what he had, plainly, the way a report should be written. Seven confirmed at the first engagement, on the word of a released witness he judged reliable. A camp, found after, showing eight. A captive missing from the Praefectus's own household that same night — the timing noted, the connection unproven. Coordination in the second engagement beyond what he would expect of an undisciplined local band, suggesting direction from a source not yet identified.
He would leave one thing out of the report - that eight women alone might have done all of it. He turned the sentence over as he walked and could not make himself set it to wax. The ground had told him plainly enough. What he lacked was a place in his own mind to hold it.

32 — Watched in Turn
Thilda held the ridge above the fort's west gate, the morning after the outsider had arrived.
It was about mid-morning when the escort came out, four men on foot, one centurion and the rider Tigratavā had seen the previous morning. They were keeping a steady walking pace, and were heading directly for the ambush site.
She was back at camp within the half hour.
"There's an escort heading this way," she said. "Four foot soldiers, one centurion and the stranger."
They left the camp and moved to the ground above the clearing, keeping to the tree line the whole way. Below them, Ligarius worked the churned earth on foot, crouching at the drag marks, following them out to where the recovery carts had stood. He worked the site methodically, noting all his findings on a wax tablet he kept in his document pouch.
"He's thorough," Tigratavā said.
"None of the others have spoken to him since they got here," Tiata said.
When Ligarius straightened and turned to the escort, asking them to take him to the camp, the women followed, staying out of earshot.
The escort's nerve broke within sight of the camp.
Rufinus appeared to be ordering the four soldiers to continue on, but none of them moved. Ligarius waited, saying nothing, while the argument played out in front of him. In the end they watched Ligarius continue alone.
Tiata turned to alert Tigratavā to prepare her bow, and found her with bow half-drawn, and an arrow tracking Ligarius across the open ground. Tiata nodded and said nothing.
He stood at the edge of the clearing a while before he moved further in, taking in the fire, the horses, the bedrolls, without touching any of it. He crouched by the fire once, close enough to feel its heat, and didn't disturb the ash. They watched him counting the bedrolls, moving his lips.
Tigratavā's bow didn't waver the whole time.
He left the way he'd come, unhurried, and joined the escort to return to the fort.
"The eight bedrolls seemed to interest him," Tove said, after the escort had left.
"That's it," Thana said. "The boy from the first patrol would have reported seven, and nobody in that second attack would have been able to count anything."
Taran's jaw was tight. "He'll figure out I'm here, won't he?"
"You've left Lorch, that much he knows," Tiata said. "But for him to believe that you're still this close, he'll be thinking either he's mad, or you are." She held Taran's gaze a moment. "Rome won't attack what it doesn't believe in."

33 — Three Sides
"I think it's time we paid our new friend a visit," Tove said. "He came all this way. It would be rude not to return the courtesy."
"Gatecrashing seems impolite," Toria said. "We should send a message ahead, to let them know we're coming."
"We could have sent it with those courier boys who just left," Thana said.
Tove grinned at that. "I must say, it would be nice to have a warm welcome when we visit."
Taran looked up.
"Tigratavā and I could send messages from both north and south," she said. "The same as we did with the century."
Thilda weighed in next. "Better still if we combine it with a patrol strike." She looked to Tiata. "What do you say, Tiata?"
"I think we could make it work," Tiata said. Turning to Tacita, she asked, "Where would you put the strike?"
Tacita lifted her gladius and pointed it due east.
"I like that idea," Tiata said. "A patrol strike on the east would really upset the balance if we accompanied it with the shots on either flank. We'll want to get the timing right, though."
"There's an eastern patrol that usually returns to the fort just on sundown," Thilda said. "I've already got a good ambush site in mind."
"I know a couple of good vantage points, north and south, where the sentries on the ramparts are easily within striking distance," Tigratavā said. "The wind around sundown is usually from the north-west, so the north shot will probably be the easier of the two. Do you have any preference, Taran?"
Taran thought for a moment. "I'd like to try the south shot."
Tiata looked around the group. "The plan will be - strike the patrol, release a witness, split north and south." She paused. "We need to synchronise these two shots, so I'm thinking Tacita could tail the witness to the fort, then signal the two of you the moment he enters the eastern gate."
Tacita looked across at Tiata, and nodded.
Tove had been quiet through the planning, turning something over. "There's a second part to this, if you want it," she said. "They've been in this camp twice now. If we accompany tonight's strike with a camp shift, they won't know where to look for us next."
Tiata considered that. "You might be onto something there. Any suggestions of where to go?"
"Well, yes actually," Tove said. "There's a hollow to the south of the fort with good water, thick cover, and a rise behind it giving a clear line down onto the fort below."
"I think I know the spot you mean," Taran said. "I was looking at it yesterday."
Tiata stood up. "We'd better be packing up, then."
⁂
They broke camp within the hour. Tove led them south, to the new camp site. It was mid-afternoon by the time the last of the gear was settled and the horses picketed.
From there Thilda led them east, toward the new ambush site. They reached it with the light beginning to slant low through the trees, and took their positions.
The patrol came round the bend to find their path blocked. Thilda's shield filled half the width of the track, Thana's oval shield locking in beside it. The patrol paused before raising their own shields.
Taran dropped from the branches overhead with a scream that even caught Tiata off guard. She landed in the middle of their formation with her twin blades flying. Tove came in low on one flank, axe swinging, and Toria appeared on the other side, falcata levelled.
One man at the rear suddenly felt cold steel at his throat and a harsh rasp in his ear. "Stay."
Tiata and Tigratavā stepped up and relieved him of his blade and shield, and began to strip him of his armour, leaving him in only his under-tunic.
"We haven't gone anywhere, have we?" said Tiata. "Off you go now, and tell your visitor we look forward to meeting him. Walk, don't run, and if you value your life, don't look behind you. Now, go."
She turned him to face the fort and sent him on his way.
He began walking immediately, and Tacita silently fell in behind him.
Toria's forearm had taken a blade, deeper than she'd first thought. Tove noticed it first, and immediately attended to her.
"You'll live," Tove said, already binding the arm. "But you're done for today."
Tove and Thana walked with her back toward the new camp, Toria leaning on Tove's shoulder.
⁂
The rest split without discussion. Tiata went north with Tigratavā. Thilda went south with Taran. Both pairs had marked their ground already, and both had a clear line to the same point east, where the returning soldier would have to pass on his way home.
Tacita went alone, following the young man's track at a distance.
She watched him all the way to the gate. The moment he was admitted, she raised the gladius and held it there until she saw the response from both directions.
⁂
Taran had the sentry on the southern rampart in sight before the signal came. She loosed her arrow the moment she saw Tacita raise her gladius, and the man folded without a sound.
To the north, Tigratavā's arrow found its mark in the same breath.
Both women were already out of sight before a shout could be raised on either wall.
⁂
They came into the new camp after dark, Tacita first, followed by the other two pairs. Toria sat close to the fire, her arm bound and resting across her lap, watching the others settle.
"Four on the road," Tigratavā said, dropping to sit. "One north, one south. All in less time than it takes to walk a survivor through a gate."
"He'll have barely finished passing on our regards," Thana said.
Tiata looked around the fire at all of them, and let the quiet hold before she spoke.
"I wonder how soon their visitor will be leaving," she said. "There isn't a side of that fort he can trust."

34 — Every Side
Ligarius had sent his report north that afternoon, sealed and given to a courier bound for Moguntiacum. He meant to follow within the week. His part at Lorch was done, and he had told the Praefectus so over the evening meal.
The Praefectus had ordered the best wine he'd yet put in front of him. The talk ran easier than it had since Ligarius arrived — old postings, a mutual acquaintance at Moguntiacum, nothing that touched the western ground.
Suddenly, shouts rose from every side — north, east, south.
The Praefectus was on his feet and moving toward the yard with Ligarius close behind him, out through the praetorium and into the evening air.
Men were running toward the north rampart stair. Others were running toward the south. A knot of soldiers stood crowding around the eastern gate, and a runner was crossing the open ground at a sprint, heading directly for the Praefectus.
"Sir." The runner reached them out of breath. "North wall. A sentry's down. Arrow. From the north-east ridge."
A second man arrived before the first had finished speaking. "South wall the same, sir. Both of them, struck at the same moment."
The Praefectus was visibly shaken. He turned toward the gate, where the knot of soldiers had begun to move, and Ligarius went with him.
They parted to let the Praefectus through. In the middle of them stood a soldier stripped to his under-tunic, skin raised in gooseflesh in the night chill.
The Praefectus had seen this before. It was the same pattern as the first strike, several weeks earlier.
"Report," the Praefectus said.
"Five of us went out on the eastern loop." The soldier's voice was halting. "We didn't come back on time. They were waiting for us on the bend before the ford. I'm the one they sent back with a message."
"How many?" the Praefectus asked.
"I never saw more than four. Could have been more I didn't see."
"Weapons?"
"I don't know, sir. It was fast."
Ligarius stepped in before the Praefectus could ask the next question, and the Praefectus let him.
"You said they sent you back with a message," Ligarius said. "What was the message?"
"The big one said to tell your visitor we look forward to meeting him."
Ligarius paused at this. "Is that all they said?"
"No, sir," said the soldier. "They said 'We haven't gone anywhere, have we?'"
"And the north wall," Ligarius said. "The south. Did they say anything of those?"
"No. Nothing."
Ligarius looked first to the north stair, then to the south, then to the gate where the soldier stood. He said nothing for a moment.
"Bring me the men who saw the wall shots," Ligarius said. "Whoever was nearest each one. I want them separately, and I want them now."
The order went out through Rufinus. Within a quarter hour Ligarius had two men in front of him, in the record room off the praetorium, the Praefectus standing by while Ligarius asked the questions.
Both accounts agreed on the key points. Neither man had seen anything at the tree line, or heard a sound before the sentry beside him fell. Both had the timing to within moments of each other. Neither could say from what distance the shot had come, only that it came from deep within the tree line.
Ligarius set down his stylus and closed his eyes for a moment before he spoke again.
"Two dead on the wall," he said. "Struck within the same breath, on two sides of this fort that face away from each other. A patrol taken on a third side, the same evening, by the same method that was used on the first attack. One survivor released, stripped, sent back walking." He looked up at the Praefectus. "I've spent the last two days telling myself this was contained to the ground west of your walls. I don't know what to think now."
"Then it's not seven women in a forest," the Praefectus said. "It's something that can stand at my own walls and put arrows through my sentries."
"It's still seven women," Ligarius said. "Or eight. What's changed is where I believed the edge of their reach to be." He stood. "I know where their camp is. I've stood in it myself. And by every report I've gathered since I arrived, it hasn't moved in over two weeks."
"You think it's carelessness?"
"I think it's confidence, possibly overconfidence," Ligarius said. "This garrison has refused to set foot on that ground since well before I arrived. They've had two proofs now that nobody from this fort will follow them home. A band that bold, sitting that long in one place, isn't hiding from you. It's decided it doesn't need to." He paused. "Tonight, while this fort is watching in every direction, that camp is very likely unwatched. They've had no reason yet to think we'd dare attack."
The Praefectus looked at him for a long moment.
"You're proposing we go to them."
"Before sunrise tomorrow," Ligarius said.

35 — Torches
Tove had the last watch before dawn the following morning, and it was still over an hour till first light when the torches in the fort began to be lit.
The fort usually held no torches through the night, not since the first week Tigratavā had watched it. Tonight the wall was lit end to end, and men were moving in the yard.
She went back to alert Tiata.
"Something's happening at the fort," she said. "Torches lit along the whole wall. The yard is alive with men on the move."
They all watched in silence as a column, more than twice as large as either of the previous columns, passed through the gate and took its bearing.
"They're headed for the old site," Thana said.
"They are indeed," Tiata said.
She turned to Tove. "You had us move camp yesterday. What did you see that I didn't?"
"I really don't know." Tove paused. "I just didn't like how quiet it had been."
"Keep not liking it," Tiata said. "It's useful."
Toria had been watching the gate rather than the departing column. "More than half that garrison just walked out its own front door," she said. "That's an opportunity we shouldn't miss."
"We can't take the fort," Taran said.
"Nobody's suggesting that," said Toria. "But we can give them a surprise to come home to."
They talked through it standing where they were — the gate, the granary, the stables. It was Tigratavā who finally had an idea that might work.
"There are two ridges to the north that look straight down into the yard," she said. "I've marked them before."
She nodded toward the fort, where an orange haze was spilling over the ramparts into the pre-dawn dark.
"Look at the glow coming off that compound," Tigratavā said. "They don't have that many regular sconces. Not for an ordinary watch. To get that many men and horses moving in the pitch dark, they’ve had to drag out every torch in the stores and stick them wherever they’d hold."
"Temporary torches, you think?" Tiata said.
"Has to be," Tigratavā said. "Half of what's burning down there must be temporary brands wedged in a hurry into railings, timber joints, whatever was close to hand. If we get above them, a few heavy shafts into the wood will snap those handles clean through. If enough burning heads come down into whatever they were lighting up, that yard is going to catch."
"You're certain of the range?" Tiata said.
"I am."
"Of course you are," Tiata said. "I needn't have asked."
"No harm in certainty," said Tigratavā.
She turned to Taran. "Do you think you could snap some handles, too?"
"Gladly," said Taran, as she began to collect her gear.
They went east first, keeping the fort's bulk between themselves and the column, then cut north and climbed the ridge. From their vantage point, the yard sat open beneath them. The flames served as glowing markers in the dark — torches jammed in haste into the walkway timbers and a scatter more thrust into fence-rails down near the stores.
Tigratavā selected the first mark. She notched an arrow tipped with a heavy trilobate head, drew, and loosed it.
The trilobate head took the pine handle dead-centre below the pitch-wrap, splitting the dry grain clean through with a sharp crack. The burning top sheared away and tumbled into the yard, rolling straight into what looked like stacked fodder.
Taran matched her on the next. Her first shot just missed, clattering off stone, but her second split a brand wedged into a granary railing. They worked methodically in the dark, loosing several more shafts until five burning brands had been sheared down into the courtyard. One smothered on the packed earth, but the others found dry straw and fuel.
They made their way around to the north-west ridge, and once again, the yard opened out beneath them.
After several more shots, two trilobate heads from Tigratavā and a solid hit from Taran brought three more torches down among the timber and straw, and it wasn't long before the stables began to burn.
They found another rise close enough to watch both the fort and the road the column had taken, and waited.

36 — Seven Fires
Draco had watched the column go, the gate barred behind it. The morning should be simple enough - hold the walls and wait for word.
The yard settled quickly once the dust from the column's departure cleared. What remained of the garrison went back to its rhythms — the ovens, the stores. Draco walked the wall himself once, start to finish. The column was in the field now, chasing down whatever had been haunting them. By evening, or tomorrow at the latest, it would be finished.
A short time later, he was in the record room, going through the week's requisitions with the clerk, when the first shout was raised.
"Fire!"
Within moments another shout was raised, then a third. Draco reached the yard, already filling with running men, as a fourth shout echoed from the walls.
Smoke was rising from the granary roof, and even as he watched, a fresh plume rose from further along, near the stores shed.
"Water!" he shouted. "Get the cistern open. Buckets, anything that holds water, move!"
Men ran for the cistern. By the time the first buckets reached the granary, another fire had caught in a stack of tent leather near the south wall.
The first fire was almost smothered when a fifth fire caught in a supply cart near the east wall, flame running up its canvas cover.
Then a sixth caught near the armoury door.
Then the heap of fodder behind the stables went up.
The stables themselves were past saving. The roof was gone to flame end to end. Horses screamed somewhere inside it, and the men who had gone in there to save them soon came out choking on the smoke.
Seven fires, and not one man in that yard had seen a single one start.
That was when the shouting reached him from beyond the wall.
It couldn't be the column. They'd barely been gone two hours — nowhere near long enough to have found the western band, destroyed them all, and ridden back. Whoever was out there at the gates weren't friendly.
The Alemanni had been pressing this whole stretch of frontier for a season now. Other forts along the line had gone the same way — overrun in an hour, garrisons that should have held for at least a day were gone before help could reach them. Draco had seen some of the reports. Now, with more than half the garrison outside the walls and the remnant fighting fires, it looked exactly like the kind of attacks those reports described.
"Archers!" he shouted, already moving for the rampart stair himself. "Get me archers on that wall, now!"
Several men peeled off from the fire lines and ran for the stair behind him, bows snatched up on the way.
He reached the rampart just as the first volley went out.
Through the smoke drifting low along the wall, the ground before the gate was thick with figures, milling and shouting.
He gave the order again, and a second volley went out into the haze, aimed at the thickest of the movement below.
A horse screamed somewhere in the murk beyond the gate. Then a man's voice, high and furious, cut through the noise — a voice he recognised.
⁂
The Praefectus had smelled the smoke before the fort came into sight — thin and acrid. He'd pushed the column hard, and by the time Lorch's walls rose up ahead of them through the haze, he could see the smoke was coming from inside the fort.
He approached the walls and called for the gate captain to open up, but there was no response. After a few more moments of men shouting at the gate, a hail of arrows came down out of the smoke.
The man riding just ahead of him went down with a shaft through the shoulder, his horse bolting into the column and throwing two more into confusion.
"Hold!" the Praefectus shouted, though nobody heard him over the shouting — a second volley, closer to the mark this time, and another man screaming as his horse went down beneath him.
"CEASE!" he roared, standing in his stirrups, both arms up. "It's the Praefectus! Hold your arrows — it's the column, it's your own men—"
His voice didn't carry far enough, and a third volley of arrows rained down on them.
⁂
The women watched from the ridge as confusion spread through the fort.
"That's more than we lit," Thilda said.
"It's spreading fast," Tigratavā said. "One fire feeding the next and hardly a soul left inside to fight it."
Tove said nothing for a while, just watched the stables collapse in on themselves, a column of sparks going up where the roof had been.
It was Tiata who caught the column first, and said nothing until the first volley came down out of the smoke along the wall.
"They're firing on their own," she said.
Below, a second volley found its mark, and even at this distance the sound of the column breaking apart in confusion carried up to them — horses screaming, men shouting.
Thana laughed. "We didn't do that part."
"No," Tiata said. "They managed that bit themselves."

37 — Let All of Rome Burn
It took another volley, and a fourth horse screaming down under its rider, before the Praefectus's shouting finally cut through.
By the time the gate opened, Draco was there to meet him, soot-blackened and drenched.
"Sir." Draco kept his voice steady. "Seven fires. Nobody saw a one of them start. The stables are gone. The stores and the armoury are half destroyed."
The Praefectus rode through into the yard — smoke hanging low over everything, flames still visible along the stores shed and the stables, men dashing across with buckets of water.
"How many dead?" he said.
"I haven't had time to check, sir," Draco said. "I fear more were lost from the arrows than the fires. I gave the order myself, not knowing who was riding up out of the smoke. I hadn't expected you back so soon."
The Praefectus turned away without another word.
He walked the yard alone — the granary roof was gone, the stores shed was still ablaze, and the stables were a pile of burning timber, the screaming from within beginning to die down.
Rufinus found him, near what was left of the armoury door.
"Sir. What are your orders?"
The Praefectus said nothing. He was still looking at the stables, where the mangled bodies of horses were visible through the flames.
It was Ligarius who answered, coming up beside them, a streak of ash across one cheek, his document case still slung across his back.
"There won't be another report from this desk, Praefectus," he said. "I've seen enough here myself now. I'll write what I witnessed, which is a fort that can no longer hold its own wall, doesn't know its own enemy, and just put arrows into its own returning column. I have the authority to give this order. Lorch is finished as a position. We're wasting our time trying to put these fires out — it's too far gone. I want the fort evacuated by nightfall."
The Praefectus turned to look at him.
"You'd have me give up a Roman fort?"
"This fort has already given up on itself," Ligarius said. "You lost it the moment your own archers couldn't tell friend from foe through the smoke. And besides, there'll be nothing left to hold, even if we do get the fires out."
The Praefectus held Ligarius's gaze a moment longer, then looked away, back toward the wreckage of the stables.
"Orders," he said. "Let the fires burn. Begin packing the carts. Wounded first, then whatever stores we can salvage."
⁂
The women remained on the ridge throughout the afternoon.
Below them, the fires still raged as carts were being loaded at the gate.
"Well, they've decided they aren't staying," Thilda said.
"So it appears," Tiata said. "They're not wasting any time about it, either."
Toria watched a moment longer, her bound arm resting against her side. "That's the end of that garrison."
"What's left of it," Tigratavā said.
Thana folded her arms, a look of amusement on her face. "We didn't take that fort."
"No," Tiata said. "They gave it up."
The column moved off just before sunset, beginning the long retreat to Moguntiacum. Taran watched until it had shrunk to a thin dark line in the dusk.
"He's in there somewhere — riding out of the only place I've known for three years." She paused. "I wonder if he knows I'm watching."
The group was quiet for a while before Tiata spoke. "They'll write this up as something else," she said. "Alemanni, most likely — that's what's been eating this frontier all year, and it's an easier thing for Rome to believe than the truth of what actually happened here. Let them. It doesn't matter what name goes on it. All that matters is that Fort Lorch is gone."
Tove was shaking her head. "I can't believe they just let it burn."
Then Tacita spoke.
"Let it burn," she said. "Let all of Rome burn."

Historical Note
Female gladiators were a genuine feature of the Roman arena, recorded by historians such as Tacitus, Suetonius, and Cassius Dio, and depicted on contemporary stone reliefs. Although Emperor Septimius Severus officially banned women from combat around 200 AD, private and provincial spectacles continued to stage bouts in defiance of imperial decree.
The fort at Lorch was a real Roman fortress. It sat at a point known today as the Limesknie — literally, the "limes-knee," the bend where the Upper Germanic limes turned sharply east to meet the Raetian limes on its way to the Danube. "Genu" is the Latin word for knee. The site lies beneath the present-day town of Lorch, which has prevented the same kind of full excavation carried out at other forts along the frontier, such as Zugmantel. The only part of the fort still visible above ground is a single tower base at the west gate. The last significant excavation was carried out in the 1980s, and found the remains preserved under about 1.5 metres of hillside silt and more recent construction.
Lorch was located at the point where two distinct border construction styles met - while Upper Germania fortified its line with an earthen rampart and split-oak palisade, Raetia constructed a continuous wall of plastered limestone, remembered in folklore as the "Teufelsmauer" (Devil's Wall).
The wider collapse of the frontier referenced in this book is also historical, known as the Limesfall. Further details are provided in the Historical Note at the end of the sequel to this book.

Character Index

Thana
Pronunciation - tHAH-nuh
Origin - Greek
Meaning - "death"
Thana is the first of the eight introduced to us on the sand of the Kynegion arena in Byzantium. One of Servius's many gladiatrices, she had been taken by pirates from her hometown on the coast of Greece. She was fourteen at the time, from a family of good standing and modest means.
As a child, she had excelled in her lessons, her exacting and analytical nature finding its true passion in arithmetica. This preternatural ability to solve problems instantly, using any means available, served her well in her role as gladiatrix, allowing her to walk victorious from eleven death matches. Over time, her ruthless calculating directness hardened into a smug, cynical outlook on life.
In combat, she handles her xiphos with calculated dexterity, unafraid to step into close hand-to-hand bouts, prepared to fall back on her xiphidion if required. Even at a distance, she is deadly accurate with her akontia.

Toria
Pronunciation - toh-RREE-uh
Origin - Iberian
Meaning - "conqueror"
Toria is the second of the eight we see, first shown to us as opponent of Thana, set to battle to the death, before Tacita intervenes. She has been under the brutal hand of Servius for longer than the others, having been taken as a small child when Roman soldiers were sent to quell unrest in her native province on the Iberian Peninsula. She remembers little more than the sound of her mother's voice being cut off mid-word, and the smoke that did not belong in that hillside village.
Her outgoing, fun-loving nature was severely curtailed during her years at the Kynegion in Byzantium, forcing an underlying dominance to the fore. This served her well during those years, building in her a fiery spontaneity, a quick eye and a lethally fast hand.
In combat, she will face down the toughest of enemies, her falcata leaving a trail of destruction. Through her hastiness, she is prone to put herself in harm's way, but rarely does she remain there long enough for harm to reach her. So ready is she to lead in close combat, that the spear she carries seldom serves any greater purpose than to simply be there if required.

Tacita
Pronunciation - TA-sih-tuh
Origin - Roman
Meaning - "silent" or "secret"
Tacita is introduced in the Kynegion arena in Byzantium. Owned by Servius, she has been a gladiatrix there for three years, after being sold by her father to settle a debt.
Although she was already a quiet girl by nature, the betrayal of paternal duty forced her to build an unbreakable resilience through silence. She has an unplumbed depth of emotion — a unique understanding of hardship that, though silent, does not refrain from offering her own peculiar form of compassion. She had always been steady and precise, but due to her past and her extreme self-discipline, these features have sharpened to lethality.
Her role in combat is never on display. She is the silent steady hand holding the blade that doesn't so much appear as just suddenly exist at a throat. She acts as the silencer of the survivor, and the extractor of the hostage. Her gladius is more an extension of her presence than a weapon, when in close combat, and a handful of plumbatae carried at the belt are reserved for silencing from a distance.

Tigratavā
Pronunciation - tee-grah-tah-VAH
Origin - Sarmatian
Meaning - "with the strength of an arrow"
We first see Tigratavā as she tracks a Roman patrol on the Sarmatian steppes. Born into the Roxolani tribe, she is the sole survivor of her family, after her father and the forty men who rode with him were disarmed and killed by the Roman officers they had ridden with for the previous eleven years. The blame for a Roman raid on a village north of their territory was laid on them, and Tigratavā, who had been moving herds to the east at the time, returned to a burned camp and forty unburied dead. From that time on, she had fended for herself, surviving by pillaging passing Roman patrols and supply trains.
Her natural analytical precision served her well through those lonely years, and her patient observation allowed her to choose her battles well. This same reserved caution has made her the ideal scout for the band.
Tigratavā is often the one to open combat, precisely placing one of her trilobate arrow heads through the throat of an enemy, sending it from her composite recurve bow, and usually from concealment. When called upon to assist in close combat, she is no less adept at wielding her long sword.

Tiata
Pronunciation - tee-AH-tuh
Origin - Dacian
Meaning - "daughter of the strong"
Tiata is a larger-than-life mystery, first seen openly displaying her latest victim. The mystery is her past, which she has never cared to share, and has never been asked to reveal, however, hints of it are visible in her unmitigated vendetta against the Roman surveyor — any Roman surveyor. It hints at a past that has built in her a controlled yet bitter resentment of the Roman partitioning of the Dacian territory.
Her self-sufficiency is predominant when we are introduced to her personal campsite, where everything is arranged decisively to serve its most practical use. She wears her dominance openly, yet her natural conscientiousness leads her to seek the opinions of others before making her final decisions. This habit of leading by listening softens her otherwise superior bearing, making of her a trusted and trusting unranked commander.
In combat, her initial role is in planning. Her direct and decisive manner ensures everyone plays their part correctly. When it comes to the battlefield, her size and her two-handed falx leave nothing standing before her. So meticulous is she in her preparedness that she also carries a long sword at her left hip, though she rarely finds cause to use it.

Tove
Pronunciation - TOH-veh
Origin - Scandinavian
Meaning - "beautiful thunder"
Tove is first seen in the forest between the Iazyges pastures and the Hasdingi holdings, unbothered by an hour of being watched from the tree line. Rugii by birth, from a cold coastal homeland far to the north, she was taken by Gothic raiders and traded onward to Hasdingi hands. She allowed her captors to underestimate her until the opportunity presented itself for her to flee, and had been alone in the forest since.
Tove's warmth and affability are her most disarming traits, inviting the band to join her on their first meeting and willingly sharing her meal. She's the first to welcome a newcomer, and the first to offer help to the wounded.
In combat, Tove is willing to lead the strike, her raw focus and the brutality of her axe carrying the weight of her past into every battle. She discovered that revenge alone offered no relief to the pain of her past, and even her affability can be weaponised by using it as a patronising corrosion to an enemy iron soul.

Thilda
Pronunciation - THIL-duh
Origin - Germanic
Meaning - "strength in battle"
Thilda comes onto view scouting along a stretch of border, when she comes across the campfire of the band of six women. Born to a people who held relatively resource-poor ground in Upper Germania, she fought first under self-important commanders, and later alone, coming to know both Rome's patient encroachment from the south and the raiding tribes pressing down from the north. We are not told what happened to her people other than that she had buried more of her own than she could remember.
Whatever the hardships were that she faced during her early years, they had only served to strengthen and stabilise a quiet confidence in her own ability to read the landscape. The caution first seen in her covert observation of the others around their campfire was no less present in her bold approach, after deciding they were worth investigating.
In combat, Thilda holds the line, her tower shield planted as solidly as herself, an immovable object against which seemingly unstoppable forces break themselves. She is unafraid to use her framea in direct attacks and will turn to the spatha when close combat requires it.

Taran
Pronunciation - TAHRR-uhn
Origin - Caledonian
Meaning - "thunder"
Taran is the last of the eight, first seen while still in captivity at Fort Lorch, standing silent by a wall as the Praefectus displays her to visiting officers as proof of Rome's control even beyond the frontiers. Taken from Caledonia as an exotic curiosity, she has spent three years treated, in her own words, like a hound that had learned to heel. She breaks her captivity the night a frightened soldier's account of an ambush reaches the household, lifting weapons from the Praefectus's own armoury before scaling the fort wall in the dead of night.
From the little we are told about her life in Caledonia, we can see that her speed and dexterity was more than a match for a Roman soldier. This fiery-tempered nature never burned out during her captivity, and even the practice blades she was proffered were used to keep her skills sharp. Over the three years of captivity, she learned a unique cold patience that now acts as a faucet through which she channels her frenzy, however, it never chilled her natural warmth which resurfaces the moment she allows herself to relax into Tove's embrace.
In combat, Taran appears to be the embodiment of a contradiction. She has the immense patience required to be a highly skilled archer, and other times, she's a screaming banshee who drops from overhead onto five armed men with her twin blades already flying. The tuagh she has at her belt has not yet seen action since Caledonia, but would no doubt prove as deadly in her hands as her other weapons.
