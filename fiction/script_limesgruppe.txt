1 — North of the Wall

Deep snow had settled over the Swabian ridges before the year died, burying the tracks in knee-deep drifts and weighting the spruce boughs until they brushed the horses’ withers. Moving eight mounts through the timber meant keeping below the crests where the wind stripped the stone bare, picking a slow line through frozen birch and hazel scrub.

TORIA
(looking back over her shoulder)
We're barely making ten miles a day. A crippled ox moves faster than this.

TOVE
A crippled horse doesn't move at all. Let them pick their footing. If a mare breaks a leg in a hole, you're the one carrying the tack.

THANA
No Roman is out on these roads in this weather. Be glad of the snow. It saves having to look over our shoulders.

TORIA
(muttering)
It's too quiet.

NARRATOR
Up ahead, Thilda checked her mount where the timber thinned against a shelf of weathered rock, and the rest pulled up behind her.

Ahead, the forest sloped gently south towards the cleared apron of the Raetian border. Through the bare trees, the ruins of Dalkingen sat fifty paces below.

Three months had done little to soften the wreckage. Snow had drifted over the collapsed sandstone arch, turning the imperial monument into an ugly mound of yellow rubble jutting from the ditch. Six feet of crushed ashlar choked the road, the toppled bronze emperor buried somewhere beneath the ice and stone.

On the catwalk above the wall, two auxiliaries huddled under a wicker windbreak, their cloaks pulled over their ears, staring into a three-legged iron brazier between their boots. Neither looked north.

Taran leaned forwards over her pommel, watching them through the bare birch twigs. Her eyes narrowed, and a slow, wicked twitch touched the corner of her mouth.

TARAN
(softly)
They look cold.

TORIA
(grinning)
You're like a Cupid from the underworld.

NARRATOR
Taran was already sliding the yew bow from her back. She laid a heavy broadhead against the riser, drew smoothly to the corner of her jaw, and loosed.

The shaft cleared the lower spruce boughs with a soft hiss and dropped sixty paces down the slope.

The heavy iron broadhead struck the rim of the brazier with a sharp, ringing crack.

The tripod buckled, and the bowl flipped sideways. A shovel-load of red-hot charcoal and glowing ash dumped straight into the lap of the nearest sentry, spilling across his greasy wool cloak and his companion's boots.

The man let out a screech that cut through the frozen woods like a stuck pig. He threw himself backwards off his stool, rolling frantically in the slush on the walkway and slapping at his smoking groin. The second guard slid around precariously on the icy planks, trying to kick glowing coals off the edge and swearing at the empty air.

Above in the tree line, Taran chuckled softly.

TARAN
They look warmer now.

NARRATOR
Toria shook with quiet laughter, and even Tacita's eyes crinkled in the shadow of her hood. They turned the horses together, slipping along the contour of the ridge, leaving the two sentries dancing on the catwalk.

They tracked the frontier for two weeks, staying in the tree line north of the wall.

The white limestone ran straight across the country, four paces high, ignoring the hills and ravines. Every thousand paces a stone tower stood above the ditch, its catwalk empty, smoke leaking from the roof shingles as the sentries burned whatever dry wood they had left.

The tribal lands to the north were dead quiet. The summer villages were buried in drifts, the livestock penned into the back of the roundhouses, the paths wiped clean by the wind. They crossed the frozen trough of a wolf pack heading south towards the game trails. Further east, they held the horses flat in a spruce hollow while five hunters drifted along a ridge above them without ever looking down.

Near Hienheim, the country opened out onto grey, wind-scoured flats, and the limestone wall ended.

The stone simply ran down a low bluff into the frozen shallows of the Danube, and stopped.

Taran pulled her mount up, squinting through the glare at the dead masonry.

TARAN
Did they run out of stone?

THILDA
The river takes over here. Rome doesn't build a wall when water can do the work.

TORIA
(looking down at the vast, white plain of ice)
It's not doing much work today. They forgot about winter.

TIATA
It will in a month. Until then, it's open ground.

NARRATOR
They swung away from the dead wall, pushing northeast into the skirts of the Bohemian Forest.

The spruce closed in, dense and black against the granite hills. They kept well north of the river, bypassing the fortress at Castra Regina. From the ridges above the Regen, they could see the haze of coal and woodsmoke hanging over the valley where the Third Legion sat in its barracks.

By this stage, the deep continental freeze had set in.

Every evening, Tigratavā saw to the mounts with the steady routine of the steppe, picking ice from their frogs and wrapping their lower legs in coarse wool against the snow-gall.

On the fourteenth day past the Regen, the granite bluffs broke open.

They dismounted and led the horses down through a narrow, boulder-strewn gully until the trees thinned.

Below them lay the gorge.

Two hundred feet down, three rivers met in a frozen basin. The Ilz came down from the northern rocks, dark and narrow. The Danube swept in wide from the west, its broad current locked under two feet of black ice dusted with powdered snow. And cutting in from the south was the Inn, its milky Alpine waters frozen into a jagged, uneven highway dividing Raetia from Noricum.

Across the expanse, sitting on the tongue of land between the rivers, was Boiodurum.

THILDA
The near bank is Raetia. Across the bridge, with the wharves and the stone warehouse, is Noricum.

TIATA
And the fort?

THILDA
Down by the bridgehead. Auxiliary foot. A few cavalry for the road, but mostly men minding gates.

NARRATOR
The Danube below was dead silent. The cargo cranes on the wharves stood bare, their ropes stiff with rime. The timber barriers across the river gates were barred shut, and the stone towers along the water wall showed only thin curls of brazier smoke.

Tacita looked down at the ice, then caught Tiata's eye, raising an eyebrow.

Tiata responded with a nod.

TIATA
I think I know just what you're thinking. <pause> Tigratavā, you've taken horses across the frozen Danube before. What's the best way to avoid detection?

NARRATOR
Tigratavā dropped to her knees beside her horse, pulling bundles of coarse burlap from her saddle roll.

TIGRATAVA
We bind the hooves. Three turns around the iron. It muffles the stone, and it bites into the ice.

NARRATOR
They worked without talking, kneeling in the drift. When they finished, the eight horses moved with a soft, muffled rustle, their iron shoes deadened against the rock.

They waited for the hours past midnight.

The moon slipped behind the western hills, leaving the gorge washed in blue starlight. The air was dead still.

Tigratavā took the lead down the switchbacks on foot. At the riverbank, the shore-ice groaned beneath their weight—a dull, hollow sound that echoed off the granite cliffs—but the crust held.

They stepped out onto the Danube together.

The crossing was fifty paces of open, wind-packed snow over iron-hard ice. Underfoot, the frozen river boomed occasionally as the current pushed beneath the crust, the sound vibrating up through their boots. The horses walked quietly, stepping in each other's tracks.

2 — The Cold Ledger

They made their camp in a wooded ravine high on the bluffs above the Inn, hidden in deadfall and scrub.

A shelf of rock kept the river wind off the bedrolls. Tove blocked the open side with packed snow and pine boughs, keeping their charcoal fire small and low so it gave off neither smoke nor flame. With eight horses to feed, they stripped scrub and melted snow by turns, never showing themselves on the skyline.

They spent the first two weeks working the station in pairs, trading watches from before dawn until well after dark.

Tigratavā and Thilda took the low ground, crawling through the frozen willow scrub along the river to read the timber and the water approach. Thana and Tacita held the bluffs, watching the road gate and timing the sentries. Higher up, Taran kept her eyes on the stone house, tracking movement behind the shutters whenever the smoke cleared.

When they gathered by the coals at night, the pieces came together quickly.

THANA
Three guards on the road. They hate the wind. One man stands by the turnpike until his knees go stiff, then he bangs on the shack door and the next man takes his place. The road is clear for twenty paces while they swap.

TIATA
And the fort across the bridge?

THILDA
Fifty auxiliaries on foot. You can see their sentries on the river wall, but they stay behind their own ditch. The customs post handles the road. Unless a courier sounds a horn, the bridge gate stays shut.

TIATA
(looking at Taran)
What about the stone house?

TARAN
Two clerks. A fat one in furs and a thin one at a desk. They have a charcoal pot between them, and the room is full of scrolls and wooden tablets. They don't look up, and they don't go outside. A boy brings them peat twice a day, and that's all.

TORIA
Why sit in the cold rewriting old paper?

TARAN
Because they're terrified. I watched the Praefectus at Lorch rage about it for three winters. When the roads freeze, Rome stops fighting and starts counting. The clerks have to prove every bushel of grain and every copper coin taken in the province matches the duplicate records before the auditors arrive in the spring. If the numbers don't balance, people lose their pay or their heads. Those two down there aren't watching for an enemy. They're trying to save their own skins from their superiors.

TIATA
And the road?

TARAN
Dead until the melt. Then it goes mad. The officers at Lorch talked about it every winter. The second the roads thaw, the orders come down to push fresh mounts and grain west to the Rhine for the spring campaign. Any herd coming down that highway from the east has to halt at this post. The drovers have to clear the papers before they're allowed across the bridge.

TIGRATAVA
And while they wait, the horses go in the lower pen.

TIATA
Tell us about the pen.

TIGRATAVA
Built on the gravel terrace below the road, right against the river. Heavy post-and-rail oak. The road side is solid, but the river fence sits right on the drop into the shallows. That's where they put the troughs.

THILDA
So the horses face the water when they drink.

TIGRATAVA
Their backs face the road. A line of horses at that rail blocks the view from the stone house completely.

NARRATOR
Tiata looked around the fire before she spoke again.

TIATA
If we rig that pen now while the ground is frozen and nobody's watching, when the thaw comes, the trap will be waiting.

NARRATOR
The sabotage was done a couple of nights later.

A thick river fog came off the water, cold and wet, blotting out the road and the bluffs.

Tacita and Tigratavā went down after midnight.

They reached the riverbank from the water side, keeping low along the shelf where the shore-ice met the mud. Above them, the customs post was quiet, smelling of charcoal smoke and wet timber.

The holding pen sat dark and empty in the fog.

They worked the river fence from the shallows below the drop.

The rails were heavy oak, set into mortises in the upright posts and locked with square wooden pins. Tigratavā wedged a blade under the head of the first pin and eased it up. It came free with a dull scrape, and Tacita slid it out.

In its place, Tacita tapped in a green pine peg. She had carved it to match the square hole, but whittled the center down to the thickness of a straw. From above, the head looked flush and solid in the timber. Under the weight of a horse, it would snap instantly.

They moved along the five spans of the river rail. Ten oak pins pulled and packed into Tacita's pouch; ten weakened twigs tapped flush into the mortises.

At the center post, where the long rails met over the main trough, Tigratavā levered the rail up until it cleared the mortise lip, resting it on a loose river pebble.

One hard shove from inside, and the whole river span would fall into the water.

They were back up the bluffs before the fourth watch.

Two weeks later, the weather broke.

A warm wind blew in from the south, turning the hard frost into grey slush. Down in the gorge, the ice on the Danube began to crack with dull, heavy booms as the snowmelt came down out of the hills.

Tigratavā lay in the brush above the eastern road, watching through the bare branches.

She came back to the hollow before midday.

TIGRATAVA
Mounted men. Coming from the east.

NARRATOR
Tiata climbed to the ledge with her.

A mile down the valley, moving at a steady walk through the mud, came the convoy. Eight riders in helmets and cloaks, leading thirty dark cavalry stallions hitched in neat strings of five.

3 — The Paperwork of the Empire

The paved highway approaching the Inn was a trench of yellow mud.

Gaius Valerius Vindex rode at the head of the file, his spatha slapping rhythmically against the skirts of his scale cuirass. Behind him, thirty Norican war-stallions stepped with heavy, sucking plops through the thawing clay, their breath rolling off their broad chests in thick grey clouds.

They had pushed forty miles in two days from the legionary depots at Lauriacum. The thaw had arrived with a vengeance, turning the packed snow into icy slush, and Vindex had no intention of losing another day on the road.

Ahead, where the highway met the timber bridge spanning the swollen waters of the Inn, sat Statio Boiodurensis.

A heavy oak turnpike barrier, banded in iron, barred the approach to the bridge. Flanking it stood the stone guard hut, its small chimney leaking peat smoke into the damp air, and behind it rose the tiled roof of the tabularium.

A toll-guard stepped out of the sentry box, his wool cloak pulled high around his neck, raising a spear across the road.

GUARD
(barely glancing at the column)
Halt the file. State your business.

VINDEX
(snapping)
I am Strator Consularis Vindex, seconding remounts for the Rhine legions under direct mandate of the Governor of Noricum. Raise the beam.

GUARD
The beam stays down until the papers clear the desk. Thirty horses can't sit on the road. Take them down to the lower pen.

VINDEX
(snarling)
These are imperial mounts, fool. They don't sit in the mud while civilian clerks scrape wax.

GUARD
(pointing with his spear butt toward the timber slipway)
They sit where the regulations say they sit. Or they don't cross the bridge. Take it up with the vilicus.

NARRATOR
Vindex held the man’s gaze, his jaw tightening until the leather straps of his helmet bit into his cheeks. Behind him, the lead stallions shifted, their hooves clattering on the cobbles.

VINDEX
(barking over his shoulder)
Sextus. Take the strings down to the water. Get them tied and watered.

NARRATOR
The drovers turned the lead lines, guiding the six strings of horses down the muddy track toward the river corral.

The enclosure sat on the gravel terrace fifteen paces below the road, right against the churning, grey current of the Inn. It was built of heavy post-and-rail oak, its far side running directly along the drop to the water, where a long timber watering trough was fed by a mountain runoff leat.

The four drovers led the strings inside, their boots sinking ankle-deep in freezing mud. They hitched the lead horses to the heavy river-facing rail above the troughs, spacing the strings so the stallions had room to lower their heads into the water without snapping at each other.

Thirty horses, heads down, immediately formed an unbroken wall of dark croups and swishing tails, completely screening the far fence from the road above.

Up on the gravel apron, the senior drover, a veteran named Marcus, swung down from his saddle. He unbuckled his cloak, looked down at the pen, then turned toward the junior recruits, Vibius and Sextus.

MARCUS
You two stay with the lines. Watch the troughs. If a stallion tangles his lead or starts biting, sort it out.

VIBIUS
(wiping slush from his cheek)
It's freezing down there, Marcus. Can't we rotate?

MARCUS
You'll rotate when I tell you to rotate, recruit. You're at the bottom of the roster, and that's where you stay. Keep your eyes on the beasts.

NARRATOR
Marcus and the other senior drover slipped through the leather curtain of the guardhouse, joining the two outriders and the turnpike sentry by the glowing charcoal brazier. The heavy curtain fell shut behind them, cutting off the draft.

Vibius cursed under his breath, took his shovel from the pack-mule, and trudged down into the pen beside Sextus.

Up at the customs house, Vindex pushed through the double oak doors of the tabularium, his boots leaving greasy smears of clay across the flagstones. Behind him trotted Hilarus, the legionary librarius, clutching the boiled-leather document case against his ribs like a shield.

The office was stiflingly warm and reeked of lamp oil, damp wool, and scorched peat.

Behind a heavy oak counter sat Faustinianus, the station vilicus, a portly imperial freedman wrapped in sheepskin robes, warming his fat hands over an iron charcoal pan. Beside him sat Florianus, the counter-scribe, a thin, sour-faced man with ink-stained fingers and a pile of pine wax tablets stacked neatly beside his elbow.

Neither man looked up immediately. Florianus continued drawing the point of his iron stylus down the wax of a day-book, muttering tally numbers under his breath.

Vindex stepped to the counter and brought his fist down on the oak with a hollow crack.

VINDEX
I have thirty cavalry stallions bound for the Rhine front. I want the clearance stamped and the turnpike raised. Now.

FAUSTINIANUS
(slowly)
Thirty head. Entering the jurisdiction of Raetia from the province of Noricum. That is subject to the portorium Illyrici.

VINDEX
(snapping)
These are military remounts. Immune from tax under imperial decree.

FAUSTINIANUS
(smoothly)
Nothing is immune until the records prove otherwise, officer. The Emperor himself doesn't cross this river without an entry in my day-book. Show the diploma.

NARRATOR
Vindex gestured impatiently, and Hilarus scrambled forwards, fumbling with the bronze buckles of the leather tube. He extracted two tightly rolled sheets of papyrus and a three-leaf wax diptych, laying them on the counter.

Florianus picked up the first papyrus. He held it close to the oil lamp, examining the red wax seal at the bottom.

FLORIANUS
(murmuring, voice dry as parchment)
Seal of the Legatus Augusti pro praetore of Noricum. Intact. Travel warrant for thirty remounts, four drovers, two outriders, one clerk, one officer. Destination: Moguntiacum.

VINDEX
You see? Now stamp the receipt, and let us be on our way.

FLORIANUS
Not so fast. This is the notae equorum. The descriptive manifest. <pause> Number one: bay stallion, six years, star on forehead, off-hind white. Number two: black stallion, five years, branded Pegasus on near shoulder... Thirty entries.

NARRATOR
Florianus set the diptych down, reached for a blank wax tablet, and picked up his stylus.

FLORIANUS
I will need to inspect every animal.

NARRATOR
Vindex stepped forwards, his scale armor clinking.

VINDEX
Inspect them? Are you mad? That's thirty horses! It will take an hour!

FAUSTINIANUS
(flat and unyielding)
It will take as long as it takes to ensure no unmanifested stock is crossing this border. You seem very keen to move them through unchecked. Your hostile manner is enough to raise suspicion. You can be thankful I'm not one to be suspicious, only meticulous, because if one horse has a blaze instead of a star, or if a local trader slipped a civilian mount into your string to dodge the toll, the penalty falls on my ledger. Florianus will examine the teeth, the markings, and the brands.

VINDEX
(shouting)
The herd is certified by the legionary medicus at Lauriacum!

FLORIANUS
The medicus at Lauriacum doesn't answer for the publicum portorium. I do. The law is explicit, officer. No verification, no stamp. No stamp, no bridge.

NARRATOR
Vindex glared at the two bureaucrats, his hand resting on the pommel of his spatha, his chest heaving with silent fury. He knew the law. He could cut both men down where they sat, and by nightfall a cohort from the fort would have him in chains for treason against the imperial treasury.

VINDEX
(hissing)
Do it quickly.

FLORIANUS
I do it thoroughly, officer. The Empire runs on precision, not speed.

NARRATOR
Faustinianus held up a hand.

FAUSTINIANUS
Before we inspect the animals, we must ensure all the documents are in order. Are you travelling with any pack-mules?

4 — Girls from Home

Down in the mud of the pen, Vibius leaned his shovel against the outer fence and blew into his hands.

His boots were soaked through with freezing slush. Ten paces away, thirty Norican stallions stood at the troughs, their heads down, their tails twitching against the wind. Up on the road, the customs house stood shut, its windows dark behind the oiled parchment panes.

SEXTUS
(shivering)
How long will they take in there?

VIBIUS
Hours. Clerks don't care who freezes.

NARRATOR
He turned his back on the horses, resting his arms on the top rail of the road fence, staring into the mist hanging over the track. It was thick enough to obscure the timber bridge thirty paces out, deadening the sound of the Inn to a low rumble.

SEXTUS
(nudging him)
Look at that. On the road.

NARRATOR
Coming down from the bluff were three figures, walking along the frozen ruts.

As they drew near, Vibius straightened.

They were not locals. They did not wear the coarse homespun of the Celtic river-folk or the sheepskins of the hill tribes. They had dark hair, olive skin, and Mediterranean features.

On the left walked an Iberian girl, the tallest of the three, sun-bronzed, lean, and athletic. Beside her was a Greek girl of middling height, with high cheekbones, dark ringlets escaping her hood, and an aquiline nose. On the right walked a Roman girl, slightly smaller than the other two, wrapped in an Italian wool mantle, keeping her eyes down.

Sextus threw his shoulders back. Vibius scraped the mud from his boots against the lower rail, hooked his thumbs into his sword-belt, and stood his ground.

SEXTUS
(resting a boot on the bottom rail)
Well now. What brings three women down to the river in this cold?

NARRATOR
The Greek girl stopped at the fence, tilting her head. She looked Sextus up and down, taking in his red nose and the mud on his tunic.

THANA
(in clean, smooth Latin)
We heard thirty soldiers had arrived from the east. We found two boys with a shovel instead.

NARRATOR
Sextus's face tightened, but he laughed and leaned over the wood.

SEXTUS
We rode forty miles through the snow from Lauriacum. We're the cavalry escort. Direct orders from the Governor.

TORIA
(voice low, dry and unhurried)
A cavalry escort. You have no mounts. You are minding horses for men who went inside.

VIBIUS
We're letting them drink. Imperial remounts. The best war-stallions in Noricum, heading for the Rhine. They don't hand them to just anyone.

TORIA
Of course. And they left you here in the mud while they sit by the brazier.

VIBIUS
We're on the roster. Security. Can't leave imperial stock unwatched.

NARRATOR
He glanced past her to the Roman girl. She stood half a pace back, her face shadowed by her hood. When she felt him watching, she looked up, meeting his eyes for a second before looking down again.

THANA

A black stallion fell down the bank, hit the water on its side, and scrambled to its feet against the current. SEXTUS

NARRATOR

VIBIUS

SEXTUS

NARRATOR

MARCUS

What is that shouting?

NARRATOR

NARRATOR

Behind them came Faustinianus, the station master, shouting about the leat and the road terrace, while Florianus, the scribe, hurried after him with his wax tablet under his arm.

VINDEX
(shouting)
My horses! Get into the river! Cut them off from the current!

5 — Ash and Wax

From the birch scrub behind the stone tabularium, Tiata and Taran watched the post uncoil.

The crash came from the river pen—the sharp snap of pine pegs giving way under the weight of thirty horses, followed by the heavy splash of the center oak rail dropping into the shallows.

Tigratavā and Tove had timed their work to the breath. Down at the river fence, out of sight of the road, Tove’s weight had cleared the loosened timbers, while Tigratavā’s knife sliced through the remaining hemp lead-hitches with three quick strokes. A dozen stallions surged through the breach, their iron shoes clattering on the wet gravel before plunging into the freezing grey current of the Inn.

Above on the road, Thana, Toria, and Tacita were already moving. The moment Toria pointed toward the water, the three women dropped below the road berm, slipping into the willow gully to begin their climb back to the ridge. Tigratavā and Tove melted into the river mist a moment later, leaving thirty panicked horses and two terrified recruits in the mud.

Then the double doors of the customs house flew wide.

Vindex burst onto the gravel terrace, his sword drawn, shouting orders that were drowned out by the roar of the water. Behind him came Hilarus clutching his leather document tubes, followed by Faustinianus and Florianus, their sheepskins flapping as they scrambled down the muddy slipway after the drovers.

Every man at the post was running for the river.

Tiata touched Taran’s arm.

TIATA
Now.

NARRATOR
They crossed the gravel without hurrying, their boots making no sound on the wet stones.

The double doors stood wide open, the flagstones inside smeared with yellow mud from the boots of the men who had just fled.

The room was warm, smelling of scorched peat, olive oil, and damp wool.

On the oak counter lay the administrative life of the frontier.

The two papyrus scrolls bearing the red wax seal of the Governor of Noricum sat beside the open diptych. The wax of the notae equorum showed sixty lines of scratched Latin—names, ages, brands, and markings for thirty imperial stallions. Beside it sat the open day-book of the portorium Illyrici, its pine leaves spread flat, covered in columns of tax tallies and ledger numbers.

Taran stepped to the counter. For three years at Lorch, she had watched the Praefectus curse these very tablets—the duplicate rolls, the fear of missing entries, the panic whenever an auditor rode in from the south.

TARAN
(quietly)
This is what holds them together. Wax and ink.

NARRATOR
In the center of the flagstones sat the three-legged iron brazier, its peat coals glowing red. Along the back wall, pine shelving held years of customs rolls, stacked in bundles of dry papyrus tied with flax cord. On the floor sat two large earthenware jars of lamp oil, plugged with rags.

Taran pulled the rag from the first jar and tipped the vessel across the counter. The dark olive oil washed across the diptych, the travel warrants, and the day-book, pooling in the grooves of the oak.

Tiata took the second jar, walked the length of the shelving, and emptied it down the papyrus bundles and pine uprights.

She stepped back to the brazier, hooked her boot beneath the iron rim, and kicked the bowl sideways.

The glowing coals spilled across the flagstones, rolling straight into the pool beneath the counter.

The oil caught with a dull, hollow thud.

A sheet of yellow flame climbed the counter legs, consuming the governor’s papyrus warrants in seconds. The beeswax of the diptych bubbled, melted, and dissolved into black slag. Across the back wall, the oil-soaked archives caught fire, thick black smoke rolling up against the timber ceiling and spreading toward the doorway.

They stepped onto the terrace. Tiata caught the bronze ring of the right-hand door and pulled it shut, leaving the left open a hand’s breadth to draw the draft.

They crossed the road at a steady walk, entered the willow ravine, and climbed the rock shelves into the granite gorge.

Thilda stood waiting at the timber line, holding the leads of the mounts. Thana, Toria, and Tacita were already in their saddles, their cloaks drawn tight against the damp cold. Tigratavā and Tove rode up through the hazel a moment later, their leggings soaked with river spray, their faces flushed from the climb.

Tiata and Taran swung onto their horses.

Through the bare birch branches, the slate roof of the tabularium sat eighty paces below them.

A thin wisp of grey smoke began to leak from beneath the eaves. It thickened, darkening to soot, and then a jagged tongue of orange flame cracked through the mortar of the chimney.

6 — Fire

Marcus hauled the big bay onto the gravel bar, cold water pouring from the horse's withers in grey sheets.

The stallion lunged, iron shoes slipping on the wet river stones, before Marcus got two turns of the lead rope around his forearm and threw his weight against the bit. MARCUS
(roaring)

NARRATOR

FAUSTINIANUS

NARRATOR

HILARUS

NARRATOR

FAUSTINIANUS

The books! The rolls! The registers!

MARCUS
(bellowing)
The line! Catch the third string!

NARRATOR

The lead line twisted around their hocks, binding their legs together. Heads broke the surface for two heartbeats—nostrils flared wide, ears pinned flat, rolling white eyes—before the heavy current of the Inn sucked them under.

The five linked stallions swept past the timber bridge pilings in a wash of grey foam, tumbling downstream toward the gorge of the Danube, gone in twenty paces.

Marcus stood frozen in the shallows, the empty lead rope slipping from his numb fingers.

VINDEX
(roaring)
Leave them! Get to the post! Get to the post!

NARRATOR
Vindex ran for the slipway, water sloshing in his boots, his scale cuirass heavy as lead.

On the counter, the papyrus warrants were gone, reduced to grey flakes spinning in the updraft. The wax of the diptych was bubbling into grease, running in smoking black rivulets across the flagstones.

Faustinianus reached the threshold on his knees, gasping in the thick soot.

FAUSTINIANUS
(wheezing)
The day-book, the cross-tallies... twenty years of the portorium...

NARRATOR
He lunged forwards, reaching both hands toward the flaming counter, but Vindex caught him by the collar of his sheepskin and hauled him backwards onto the gravel.

VINDEX
(snarling)

FAUSTINIANUS
(wailing)
My ledgers! The receipts! The treasury audits! I am answerable to the governor for every copper!

VINDEX
(roaring)
The travel warrant! The duplicate warrant! Where did you put it?

FAUSTINIANUS
(crying)
On the counter! You saw me! Florianus laid it beside the diptych!

NARRATOR

Vindex turned on them, his face black with soot, his right hand gripping the pommel of his spatha.

VINDEX

Nobody, sir! We were in the sentry box! We ran out the second the rail gave way!

NARRATOR

VIBIUS

MARCUS

7 — The Limesgruppe

The turf-and-stone rampart of Kastell Boiodurum sat two hundred paces back from the river, its timber north gate looking down over the wharves.

Through the falling mist, the column filed up the gravel track from the bridgehead. Marcus and the two outriders led the twenty-five surviving stallions, the beasts shivering violently, ice crusted thick along their withers and flanks. Behind them stumbled Vibius and Sextus, eyes fixed on the slush, their soaked tunics stiffening in the wind.

At the tail of the file walked Vindex, with Faustinianus waddling beside him, screaming into the empty air.

The timber gate stood half-barred. A dozen auxiliary infantrymen of the Second Breucian Cohort stood on the catwalk above the ditch, their oval shields resting against the parapet, watching the black column of smoke rising from the customs house across the road.

Prefect Trebonius was already in the gateway.

He had held command of the Norican border post for six years. He was fifty years old, thick-set, with grey hair cut close to the scalp and an old scar running through his left ear. He stood with his thumbs hooked into his heavy officer’s belt, his red wool cloak pinned against the damp chill, watching the greasy soot settle across his gravel apron.

FAUSTINIANUS

TREBONIUS

FAUSTINIANUS

TREBONIUS

Twenty-five.

TREBONIUS

VINDEX

TREBONIUS

VINDEX

TREBONIUS
You need stabling.

NARRATOR

VINDEX

TREBONIUS

VINDEX

TREBONIUS

TREBONIUS

NARRATOR

MARCUS

From the shelf of granite two hundred feet above the gorge, the view of the valley floor was unobstructed.

TORIA

Look at the fat clerk. He's still waving his arms in the mud.

NARRATOR
9 — The Ledger of the Empire

The fortress of Lauriacum sat on the high gravel terrace above the Danube, its grey ashlar walls guarding the great military highway of Noricum.

Snowmelt dripped from the eaves of the legionary principia into the stone drainage gutters. Inside the heated record hall behind the basilica, charcoal braziers kept the damp chill from the archive racks where the rolls of Legio II Italica filled cedar pigeonholes from floor to ceiling.

Tribune Caeso Licinius sat behind a heavy oak table, reviewing the monthly forage returns from the Enns valley. Opposite him sat Aurelius Hermias, a lean imperial freedman sent from the procurator’s office at Ovilava to audit the military transport manifests.

A leather curtain parted, and the camp cornicularius entered, followed by a courier spattered to the chest in yellow mud.

The courier unbuckled his bronze cylinder and laid it on the cedar boards.

COURIER
(voice hoarse)
From Kastell Boiodurum. Prefect Trebonius dispatched me at dawn.

NARRATOR
Licinius broke the clay seal with his thumb. He unrolled the papyrus, smoothing the curling edges against the wood, and began to read.

Hermias watched him over the rim of an unrolled parchment tally.

HERMIAS
Has Vindex cleared the crossing?

LICINIUS
(dangerously level)
He has not cleared the crossing.

NARRATOR
Hermias set down his reed pen.

HERMIAS
Has the ice held him up?

LICINIUS
The customs post is gone. Statio Boiodurensis destroyed by fire on the fourth day before the Kalends of March. The tabularium burned to the foundations. All tax registers, day-books, and archive rolls of the portorium Illyrici destroyed.

NARRATOR
Hermias rose from his stool so fast his inkpot rocked on the stand.

HERMIAS
The portorium registers? Twenty years of provincial tolls?

LICINIUS
(looking up)
Ash. Faustinianus has accused Vindex of setting the brazier to his desk to cover fraudulent stock. Vindex claims the station master was asleep when the chimney caught.

HERMIAS
Where are the thirty horses?

NARRATOR
Licinius turned the sheet over.

LICINIUS
Twenty-five horses are impounded in the auxiliary stables at Boiodurum. Trebonius has refused them transit into Raetia.

HERMIAS
And the other five?

LICINIUS
(flatly)
Drowned. In the Inn. Under the bridge. The river fence collapsed while the beasts were at the trough.

HERMIAS
Five Norican war-stallions drowned in sight of a Roman fort? How?

NARRATOR
Licinius read further, his mouth twisting into an expression of bitter disgust.

LICINIUS
Trebonius questioned the drovers. The two recruits at the paddock claim they were approached at the road fence by three Mediterranean women. An Iberian, a Greek, and a Roman.

NARRATOR
The room went completely silent. Even the cornicularius by the door stopped turning his roster.

HERMIAS
(softly)
Women.

NARRATOR
Licinius tossed the papyrus onto the table.

LICINIUS
Women. Three civilised women walking through winter mud forty miles from the nearest town. One engaged them in conversation, pointed at the water, and asked if the stallions were supposed to be in the river. When the recruits looked back, the fence was broken and the women were gone.

HERMIAS
(with a dry, rasping laugh)
A fairy tale. The drovers fell asleep over their shovels, the stallions broke the timber, and the boys invented three phantoms to save their skin from the vine-staff.

LICINIUS
And Vindex lost his head. The Emperor is sitting at Moguntiacum with the Rhine army, waiting for remounts to break the Alemanni. The entire northern command is crying out for heavy horse, and twenty-five prime Norican stallions are locked in an auxiliary ditch because a civilian clerk and a staff officer cannot settle an argument over a burned table.

NARRATOR
He stood up, pushing his stool back against the stone wall.

LICINIUS
This ends today. The governor will not answer to the Emperor for five drowned horses and a closed frontier. <pause> Summon Centurion Domitius from the cavalry lines. Immediately.

NARRATOR
The clerk saluted and hurried through the leather curtain.

Licinius walked to the wall map of the Danube provinces. His finger traced the road west from Lauriacum, following the river past Ovilava to the confluence at Boiodurum.

LICINIUS
The horses will not stay in that ditch. Domitius will take twenty veteran troopers from the legionary cavalry. They will ride light, take custody of the twenty-five stallions directly from Trebonius, and cross the Inn. I will draft a provisional military transit warrant under the governor's direct seal. The horses will be driven across Raetia without setting foot in another customs house.

HERMIAS
(voice sharp)
And the destroyed station? Who answers to the procurator for twenty years of lost revenue?

LICINIUS
(turning from the map)
You will ride with Domitius. You will take four legionaries to secure the prisoners. Vindex is relieved of duty and placed under formal military arrest. His drovers will be put in irons and marched back to Lauriacum to face tribunal for dereliction and gross negligence. Faustinianus and his scribe will be delivered in a cart to the procurator’s court at Ovilava. If Faustinianus burned his own ledgers to hide a shortfall, the inquisitors will have the truth out of him before the week ends.

HERMIAS
And Trebonius? He barred the crossing.

LICINIUS
(coldly)
Trebonius acted within the letter of the law. Which is the only reason he is not being marched back in irons beside them. But he will receive an official reprimand for allowing an imperial customs station to burn under the eyes of his garrison without raising a bucket.

NARRATOR
Half an hour later, the courtyard of the principia rang with the clatter of iron shoes on wet cobbles.

Centurion Domitius stood before the steps, his scale cuirass gleaming beneath a heavy wool travelling cloak, twenty veteran troopers of Legio II Italica mounted in two disciplined ranks behind him. Behind them stood four pack-mules and a covered supply wagon fitted with iron window bars.

Licinius came down the stone steps, holding two sealed parchment rolls bound in green silk cord. He handed the first to Domitius.

LICINIUS
The provisional transit warrant. Twenty-five Norican cavalry stallions, passing through Raetia under direct legionary escort. If an auxiliary officer or a customs clerk so much as looks at your reins, show him that seal and ride over him.

NARRATOR
Domitius took the roll and slid it into his leather dispatch case.

DOMITIUS
Understood, Tribune.

NARRATOR
Licinius handed the second roll to Hermias, who was climbing into the front bench of the wagon.

LICINIUS
The arrest warrants. Bring Vindex and his drovers back to Lauriacum, then take the clerks to Ovilava.

NARRATOR
Domitius raised his right arm, wheeled his black charger, and led the column toward the west gate.

The heavy iron-shod hooves hammered across the flagstones, kicking up sprays of melting slush, marching west along the military highway to clear the border.




NARRATOR
10 — River-Gate

They tracked the southern bank of the Danube east for three days, keeping off the paved military highway whenever the ground allowed.

The Norican countryside was thick with Roman authority. Every five miles brought another stone watchtower, a courier post, or a timber stockade minding the river crossings. They moved between the limestone ridges and the river flats, bypassing the garrison at Arelape by cutting through the scrub behind the town before circling back to the water.

Ten miles past the garrison, the terrain tightened.

The granite bluffs of the Bohemian Massif pressed hard against the southern bank, squeezing the road into a rocky ledge twenty paces wide, with vertical cliffs rising on the right and the grey, swirling current of the Danube dropping off on the left.

Around the bend, blocking the defile, sat the river-gate checkpoint of Namare.

The Romans had built their post where the rock pinched narrowest. A two-storey timber watchtower anchored the riverside, joined to a stone-and-timber gatehouse built directly against the rock wall. Between the two buildings ran a heavy oak turnpike barrier, banded in iron, dropping into a slotted timber receiver on the far side.

The eight women reined in behind a shoulder of broken scree, fifty paces short of the bend, hidden in dwarf pine and hazel scrub.

Tigratavā dismounted, passed her reins to Tove, and crawled to the lip of the rock.

From her vantage point, twenty feet above the road, she had a clear line of sight through an unglazed rectangular window in the upper wall of the gatehouse.

Inside the small record room, a lone scribe was working at a long trestle table. Parchment rolls and pine wax tablets covered the boards, alongside inkpots and scrapers. At the far end of the room stood a timber framework built against the stonework, divided into dozens of square pigeonholes packed with bundles of provincial customs rolls.

In the center of the table sat a heavy three-flame brass oil lamp, its wicks burning with steady yellow points. Directly beside it sat an earthenware gallon jar, still wet around the neck where the scribe had just topped off the reservoir.

Tigratavā lowered her gaze to the road below.

An ox-cart loaded with heavy timber casks stood halted before the lowered barrier. Two auxiliary guards were engaged in an angry argument with the carter, poking spears into the load and waving a wax tablet in the merchant’s face. A third guard leaned against the watchtower railing with his thumbs in his belt, watching the dispute.

Taran crawled up through the briars and settled beside Tigratavā, pulling her yew bow from her shoulder.

Tacita moved without a word. She dropped down the loose scree behind the rock shoulder, stepping from boulder to boulder without dislodging a pebble, and melted into the dead ground behind the stone rear wall of the gatehouse.

Tigratavā notched a heavy trilobate shaft to her recurve bow, her eyes fixed on the open window. Beside her, Taran laid an iron broadhead against the riser of her yew stave, sighting down the slope toward the ox.

Down on the road, Tacita emerged from the blind corner of the masonry. She dropped low beneath the road barrier, slid her hand along the oak beam to the slotted receiver post, and worked the heavy iron locking pin free with a firm, steady twist. With the pin in hand, she backed into the shadow of the cliff.

Tigratavā drew to her ear. Taran drew in the same breath.

Tigratavā loosed first, and Taran’s bow hummed an instant later.

Tigratavā’s shaft flew through the rectangular window opening. The heavy iron head struck the earthenware oil jar dead-center.

The clay vessel shattered. A gallon of refined olive oil erupted across the trestle table, drenching the dry papyrus rolls and cascading straight over the three burning wicks.

The oil caught with a hollow roar.

A sheet of yellow flame leaped from the table, lashing the timber framework against the wall. The dry rolls of customs archives in the pigeonholes caught instantly, feeding the blaze until a wall of solid fire climbed to the roof beams. The scribe screeched, kicked his stool over, and threw himself headlong through the interior doorway into the passage.

Down on the road, Taran’s broadhead struck home.

The heavy iron point buried itself deep into the rump of the near ox.

The beast bellowed, threw its head up, and lunged forwards in blind agony.

The second ox was dragged into the charge. The team threw their combined weight and the momentum of the loaded cart straight into the unpinned barrier arm.

Without the iron pin to hold the far end, the force sheared the pivot post clean through with a loud, tearing groan. The heavy oak beam swung wildly across the road, slamming the two arguing guards into the ditch mud, before the cart surged over the broken wood and clattered through the gap.

The terrified oxen bolted down the eastern road at a dead run, hooves hammering the gravel, the timber casks bouncing and crashing in the bed. Behind them, the carter ran through the broken gate, screaming curses into the dust, while the third guard ran after him with his spear raised.

High above the river, the watchtower horn began to blare.

Auxiliaries tumbled down the timber ladders, snatching up bronze fire-buckets and shouting in confusion as they ran toward the riverbank to draw water for the roaring gatehouse roof.

Tove rode around the bend at a trot, leading the mounts of Tigratavā, Taran, and Tacita by their halter ropes.

She pulled up thirty paces short of the barrier, where Tacita stepped out from the rock face and the two archers dropped down the clay bank from the scrub.

As their boots touched the stirrups, the ground behind them began to tremble.

Tiata, Thilda, Thana, and Toria rounded the corner of the defile at a flying gallop, riding four abreast along the narrow shelf. Their horses’ iron shoes threw sparks from the granite, weapons raised, completely dominating the track.

The guards scrambling with water buckets on the riverbank turned, froze, and scattered into the rocks to avoid the charge.

Tacita, Tigratavā, and Taran swung into their saddles. Tove dug her heels into her mare’s ribs, and the four joined the rear of the file.

They swept through the broken barrier at full speed, riding through the falling soot and the heat radiating from the burning gatehouse.

No Roman had time to level a spear.

They kept their horses at a hard gallop along the river terrace, leaving Namare behind them in black smoke.

Half a mile down the road, where the track bent away from the Danube, they passed the ox-cart. It lay overturned in the ditch on its side, the wheels spinning slowly in the cold air, broken casks leaking red wine across the slush, while the winded oxen stood tangled in their harness further down the brush.

The eight women did not check their stride, sweeping past the wreckage and driving east into the open plains of Noricum.




NARRATOR
11 — Two Gates

The hollow beneath the granite shelf sat two hundred feet above the gorge, tucked deep into the rocks where no river sentry could spot the glow.

Tove had a kettle of salt pork and dried peas bubbling over the coals. She stirred the pot with a flat piece of pine, laughing so hard her shoulders shook, while Toria stood by the rock face reenacting the carter at the turnpike.

TORIA
(gasping, clutching her ribs)
He had both hands in the air. Screaming at the oxen, screaming at the guards! The barrier arm was wedged right through his front wheel, dragging along the gravel, and two casks were rolling down the ditch spitting red wine over everything!

TARAN
(grinning from ear to ear)
I put that broadhead right in the meat of the rump. The beast didn't even drop his head—he just took the turnpike with him.

NARRATOR
Thana looked across the fire at Tacita, who sat on a boulder quietly cleaning her thumbnail with the tip of her knife.

THANA
Did that receiver pin give you any trouble, Tacita?

NARRATOR
Tacita looked up, her dark eyes catching the firelight, her mouth twitching into a small, dry smile.

TACITA
(mouth twitching into a small, dry smile)
No. It was greased.

THANA
(purring, stretching her long legs toward the embers)
Imperial maintenance. Everything done by regulation. They oil the iron so we don't strain our wrists.

NARRATOR
Even Tigratavā let out an appreciative chuckle from where she sat checking the fletching on her arrows, shaking her head at the fire.

TORIA
(turning around, face flushed with delight)
And Boiodurum. Don't forget the drover boy! Vibius! He stood at that fence puffing his chest out, telling me about his family farm near Aquileia! Offering to buy me wine across the bridge!

TARAN
(laughing)
While Tove and Tigratavā were dismantling the river fence behind his back.

THANA
(voice dripping with amusement)
He was so busy trying to impress her he didn't hear thirty horses sliding into the water. And then the sergeant knocked him flat into the mud for daring to say three women had been standing in the road.

TARAN
(wiping a tear of laughter from her eye)
The poor lad told the absolute truth. Word for word, exactly what happened. And they hit him in the mouth for it.

THILDA
(dry, steady cadence)
A Roman officer cannot allow women to steal thirty horses. If fifty barbarian deserters cross the river, he keeps his dignity. If three girls talk to his sentries, he loses his mind.

TORIA
(gloating)
And the fat clerk. 'My ledgers! Twenty years of the portorium! The receipts!' Dropped his robes right in the mud and crawled after his inkpots while the roof fell on his head!

NARRATOR
Around the fire, the laughter rose into the granite cleft, loud and unburdened.

Tiata sat on a flat stone with her knees pulled up, resting her chin on her forearm. She had watched the whole display with a broad grin, her eyes bright in the firelight, laughing along with them at every fresh retelling. She didn't add her own barbs to the mockery—she didn't need to—but she enjoyed the spectacle of Rome tripping over its own boots as much as any of them.

She waited until the laughter began to taper off, until Tove was dishing out the hot peas into wooden bowls and the breathing around the hearth slowed.

TIATA
(warm, carrying lingering humor)
They'll be raking ash out of those two stations until midsummer.

NARRATOR
She took the bowl Tove handed her, blew the steam from the surface, and let the quiet hold for a heartbeat before looking around the circle.

TIATA
We had our fun with Noricum. Now we have to get through to the east.

TORIA
What does the country look like past these bluffs?

NARRATOR
Thilda shook her head, dipping her spoon into her bowl.

THILDA
Don't ask me. My people held ground six hundred miles west of here, along the Neckar. Upper Germania I know. This granite is the furthest east I've ever set foot in my life.

NARRATOR
Toria turned to Tiata.

TORIA
You're from Dacia. Do you know this road?

TIATA
(quietly)
Dacia is another world downriver. Between here and my mountains sits Pannonia. I know it belongs to Rome, and I know the Danube cuts right through the middle of it. Beyond that, we are riding into ground none of us have ever walked.

THANA
(leaning back against her cedar root)
They seem to be using the water as their border all through here. If there are big legions out there, their stone fortresses will probably sit on the riverbank to guard the shipping.

TIGRATAVA
I will ride out an hour before first light. Take the mare up the eastern ridge and see where these rocks break. If the bluffs open into farmland, I'll find where the timber runs and where Rome has its watch posts before we take the horses down into the open.

NARRATOR
Tiata nodded, satisfied.

TIATA
That seems reasonable. If you spend the day tomorrow scouting along the river, the rest of us can do a bit of foraging nearby, and replenish our stocks. We might even meet a fully laden supply train.




NARRATOR
12 — The Last Wagon

An hour before first light, the granite cleft was black and silent.

Tigratavā rose without disturbing the gravel. She checked her mare’s hooves in the dark, ran a hand down the animal's legs, and tightened the cinch. Her gorytos and recurve bow were already slung across her back.

Tiata shifted on her bedroll by the dead hearth, her eyes opening in the shadows. The two women traded a single nod. Tigratavā took the mare’s lead rope, led the horse down the loose scree on the balls of her feet, and melted into the eastern timber.

By dawn, grey light had filtered through the bare canopy of the Dunkelsteinerwald.

Tove was the first on her feet, blowing life into the buried embers and heating water in the bronze kettle. The others stirred, shaking the night’s frost from their cloaks, checking tack and feeding dry birch bark to the fire.

THILDA
(tightening the straps on her greaves)
Tigratavā will have reached the river flats by now.

TORIA
(tossing a dry leather nosebag aside)
And we have empty grain bags. The horses need oats, not scrub.

NARRATOR
Thilda looked up through the bare branches at the wooded ridge above them.

THILDA
The tracks coming down off the interior plateau hug the high contour. If anything is moving toward the Danube, it will come through the narrow pass.

NARRATOR
They saddled their seven mounts and rode out as the sun cleared the ridges.

For two hours they worked east through dense stands of spruce and damp birch, keeping below the crests to avoid outlining themselves against the sky. The granite country was cut by deep runoff ravines where snowmelt roared toward the Danube, drowning the sound of their hooves.

It was Thilda who caught the first sign.

She pulled up on a forested shoulder and pointed down through the hazel scrub.

Two hundred paces below them, cut into the steep hillside, ran a secondary military track dropping down from the interior plateau toward the Danube. Deep ruts were gouged into the yellow mud, and drifting up through the trees came the squeal of iron-tired wheels and wooden brake blocks.

They tied their mounts in a brushy hollow out of sight and moved to the edge on foot.

Below them, a Roman freight convoy was descending the pass in single file. Four heavy freight wagons, their oak beds covered by arched hoops of greased cattle hide lashed tight to the sideboards. Each wagon was pulled by a pair of heavy draught oxen, their breath rolling from broad nostrils as they braced their hooves against the descent.

On the steep slope, the wagons kept fifteen paces between them. Brake blocks shrieked against the hubs, iron chains clattered against timber, and the carters shouted hoarse curses at their teams, their eyes locked on the outer ruts.

An auxiliary optio rode his mule twenty paces ahead of the lead wagon. Two foot soldiers walked beside the second team, while three auxiliary infantrymen trailed fifty paces behind the last wagon, their shields slung across their shoulders, their heads down against the wet wind.

TOVE
(murmuring)
Army freight. That's grain from the interior depots.

NARRATOR
A hundred paces down the slope, the track cut hard around a granite shoulder, forming a blind hairpin bend. The cliff wall on the right blocked the view ahead, while the roar of the mountain creek below drowned any noise from the rear.

TARAN
(looking at the ancient oak leaning out over the road)
That rock shoulder shuts out everything behind it. And there's a good sturdy bough hanging right over the track.

NARRATOR
Without another word, the group divided and took their ground.

They left the seven horses tied to dwarf pines eighty paces up a side gully, out of sight and scent of the road, and slipped down the slope.

Tiata, Tove, and Thilda took their ground behind a granite outcrop just past the apex of the bend. Ten paces down the slope, hidden in the brush along the road verge, Thana, Toria, and Tacita crouched in the briars. Above the track, Taran worked her way onto the heavy oak limb, balancing on her haunches, her weight resting on the bark.

The rumble grew loud as the column approached.

The lead wagon reached the hairpin. The carter leaned his weight into the brake lever, hauling on his left rein, and the heavy wagon creaked around the rock. The second followed. Then the third.

The rock shoulder cut off the line of sight.

Below the crag, the iron wheels of the three lead wagons ground against the stone, their shrieking brakes echoing off the cliff face.

The fourth wagon approached the turn.

Its two draught oxen lumbered forwards, their yokes creaking, their heads down as they reached the apex of the bend.

Behind the wagon, thirty paces back, the three rear guards walked with their cloaks clutched against their throats, looking at the mud beneath their sandals.

The carter sat on his bench, bracing both feet against the timber footboard, his hands white on the leather lines.

Taran dropped from the bough, her weight coming down across his shoulders from behind. The impact drove him forward and down into the footwell, his face smashing into the floorboards before he could draw breath. She came down hard on his back, a knee between his shoulder blades pinning his weight, while her hand clamped over his mouth and her blade found his throat. The leather lines slipped from his lifeless fingers, trailing into the mud.

Simultaneously, Tiata and Tove broke from the shadow of the crag.

Tove brought her heavy axe down with a two-handed sweep, shattering the yoke-beam and driving the iron head into the skull of the near ox. Beside her, Tiata stepped inside the offside horn, bringing the falx around in a low, concussive arc that cleaved the beast’s forelegs clean through.

Both oxen collapsed into the mud, their heavy weight driving the wagon tongue into the earth and wedging the front wheels against the rock face.

The wagon stopped dead.

At the tailgate, the sudden halt made the lead rear guard look up, his hand reaching for his spear.

Before he could shout, Tacita stepped out from the brush beside the rock wall. Her gladius drove upward beneath his jaw, catching him under the ear and bearing his weight down into the slush without a sound.

Thilda stepped into the road directly behind the second man, driving her framea through his mail cuirass, pinning his chest against the rock.

The third guard spun round, reaching for his spatha, but Toria closed the distance in two strides. Her falcata swept across his throat before the blade cleared the scabbard. Thana lunged forwards, catching his falling shield by the iron rim before it struck the cobbles, easing the dead man into the ditch.

The entire strike took twelve seconds.

Around the rock shoulder, forty paces down the mountain, the remaining three wagons continued their noisy descent, their iron wheels grinding on the stone, their carters oblivious to the road behind them.

Thana drew her xiphidion and slashed the rawhide ropes binding the rear flap of the wagon tilt. She hauled the greasy cattle hide aside, exposing the cargo bed.

Sacks of grain were stacked three deep, flanked by barrels of salt pork and linen hampers of dried peas.

Tove threw her axe to the ground, caught the nearest burlap sack by the corners, and heaved it out onto the road.

TOVE
Horse oats. Whole grain.

NARRATOR
Toria and Thilda were already hauling sacks across the ditch into the side gully. Tove hoisted a second sack of oats onto her broad shoulder, while Thana cut away three heavy slabs of smoked pork hanging from the center hoop, rolling them into a strip of clean canvas.

Tacita found a small wooden keg wedged behind the meal bins, smashed the head with the butt of her gladius, and scooped two linen bags full of coarse rock salt.

They worked with quiet, practiced speed.

Within two minutes, four sacks of grain, three slabs of bacon, a sack of dried peas, and the salt were carried up to the waiting mounts.

The sound of the other three wagons was fading down the gully, moving toward the lower valley without checking pace.




NARRATOR
13 — The Beneficiarius at Namare

The smell of wet soot and burned olive oil hung thick in the narrow granite pass.

Beneficiarius Marcus Ulpius Aper stepped through the charred doorway of the gatehouse, his iron-studded boots crunching on broken roof slates and soggy ash. Water from the bucket line dripped from the ceiling timbers onto his bronze shoulder-guards, hissing against the hot stone of the hearth.

In the corner, curled on the damp flagstones, sat the scribe, his forearms wrapped in grease-stained linen, staring blankly at the floor.

Aper kicked a blackened piece of pine shelving out of his path.

APER
Get up and go to the barracks. Sit by the brazier and hold your tongue.

NARRATOR
He turned back to the threshold and surveyed the defile.

The fire was out, but the post was in ruins. Smoke rose in thin grey wisps from the roof frame, drifting out across the swollen current of the Danube. Down on the road, two auxiliary guards were hauling the splintered remnants of the oak turnpike barrier into the ditch, their tunics soaked with muddy water. A third guard sat on the ditch bank holding a broken collarbone, while the wine carter stood ten paces off, wailing about his ruined casks.

Aper walked to the center of the road.

He was forty-four years old, a seasoned veteran detached from Legio II Italica to hold the governor’s road police station at Namare. He had governed the checkpoint for four years, settling disputes between bargees, checking transit passes, and logging imperial couriers.

He stopped beside the slotted timber receiver post where the barrier arm had rested. The wood of the receiver was intact, bearing no splinter marks or crushed grain from a forced blow. Aper crouched and scanned the mud at the base of the rock wall. Two paces into the shadow of the cliff, half-buried in yellow slush, lay the iron locking pin.

Aper picked it up. The pin had not been sheared by the impact of the wine cart, but had been worked loose by hand before the ox ever touched the wood.

He rose and turned the heavy iron pin in his palm.

APER
Decurion.

NARRATOR
The watchtower commander, his face smeared with black soot, trotted across the road and came to attention.

APER
Bring the arrow from the carter's ox. And bring whatever the men raked from the records room hearth.

NARRATOR
The decurion returned moments later, carrying a heavy iron broadhead sticky with dark blood, and laid a second object on an upturned cask. It was a heavy trilobate iron head forged for a composite recurve bow, burned black by the oil fire, its tang twisted from impact.

Aper held the two arrowheads side by side. They were completely different in weight, length, and origin, confirming two distinct archers.

APER
Who was on the tower platform when the roof caught?

DECURION
Varro and Lucius, sir. They were watching the dispute at the barrier. When the room went up, they ran for the water buckets.

APER
And the riders?

DECURION
They came from the west, round the blind shoulder of the rock. Four abreast filling the width of the road, with four more right behind them, cutting through the gap before the bucket line could drop their pails.

APER
Eight riders. Describe them.

NARRATOR
The decurion shifted his weight in the mud, glancing toward the guards hauling timber in the ditch.

DECURION
The guards who were knocked into the mud by the ox swear the front rider had a bronze cuirass and bare arms. They said the waist was narrow.

APER
(with cold contempt)
You were overrun by eight mounted shock riders who coordinated an arson strike, breached an unpinned barrier with a stampeded ox, and cleared a defile in twenty heartbeats. Do not repeat tavern gossip on this road, Decurion. If an auxiliary mentions women again, dock his pay for a month and put him on ditch duty.

NARRATOR
Before the decurion could answer, a dull, grinding screech echoed down the mountain pass.

Through the mist, an auxiliary optio on a shaggy mule came down the rocky grade, followed by the lead wagon of a heavy freight convoy. The draught oxen leaned back into their yokes, iron-shod wheels grinding against the stones as the carter hauled on his brake lever. Behind the first wagon came a second, its cattle-hide tilt dripping melting frost, with two foot auxiliaries walking alongside the team. Then came the third wagon, rolling to a halt behind the second while the driver wiped grit from his eyes.

Aper stood waiting, but the road behind the third wagon remained empty.

APER
Where is the rest of your column?

NARRATOR
The optio swung down from his mule, looking back over his shoulder with a frown.

OPTIO
We are four wagons out of Cetium, carrying grain, salt pork, and pulse for the river wharves.

APER
I count three.

NARRATOR
The optio turned around, squinting up the track.

OPTIO
He was thirty paces behind us when we took the upper grade, and the rear guards were right with him.

NARRATOR
Aper turned on his heel toward the two mounted outriders waiting by the sentry hut.

APER
Take the track and find that wagon.

NARRATOR
The two cavalrymen spurred their horses up the rocky pass, their mounts' hooves clattering over the granite shelves before disappearing into the mist.

Aper did not stand idly while the riders were gone. He took the double-leaf wax manifest from the optio, cracked the seal, and read the tallies. The four wagons carried identical consignments of twenty burlap sacks of whole-grain horse oats, six timber casks of salt pork, ten linen hampers of dried peas, and three small kegs of coarse rock salt.

Aper walked to the lead wagon, had the driver throw back the cattle hide, and climbed onto the hub to inspect the stowage. He counted the sacks and casks, then moved to the second wagon and the third, repeating the inspection. Every wagon was packed to the exact same imperial pattern.

Twenty minutes passed before the drumming of hooves sounded from the upper slopes.

The two outriders burst out of the gully at a hard canter, their horses blowing hard as they hauled their reins.

TROOPER
(breath coming in ragged bursts)
At the hairpin bend, a mile up. The wagon is wedged hard against the crag. Both oxen are dead, the carter has had his throat cut, and the three rear guards are dead in the ditch.

NARRATOR
Aper swung into the saddle of his black gelding.

APER
Decurion, take four mounted troopers and follow me.

NARRATOR
Ten minutes of hard trotting brought them up the pass to the blind hairpin bend, where the roar of the mountain creek drowned out every sound beyond five paces.

The fourth wagon sat wedged across the narrow shelf, its tongue driven into the mud. The near ox lay on its side with its skull caved in, while the offside beast had collapsed where it stood, its forelegs severed above the knees by a sweeping blade. The carter lay face down in the footwell with his throat opened to the bone, and the three rear guards lay dead in the ditch slush.

Aper dismounted to inspect the bodies.

The first guard had a clean puncture under his jaw where a blade had driven straight into the brain. The second was slumped against the granite wall, his mail shirt punched through by a heavy thrusting spear. The third lay sprawled on his back, his neck laid open from ear to ear, with his sword still locked inside its scabbard.

Aper walked to the rear of the wagon, pulled the slashed cattle-hide flap aside, and checked the cargo bed against the tallies he had verified below.

Four sacks of oats had been dragged from the top tier, leaving sixteen behind. Three slabs of salt pork had been sliced cleanly from the hanging hooks beneath the center hoop, while the casks remained untouched. One linen hamper of peas was gone from the forward corner, and one of the three small salt kegs had its head smashed in, with a small portion scooped out from the top.

Aper stepped back onto the road and examined the rest of the scene.

The three riveted mail cuirasses remained strapped to the dead guards, their bronze-fitted belts were intact, and their four military spathas lay in the slush where they had fallen. Under the carter's bench, the heavy oak tool chest sat bolted to the frame with its bronze padlock unbroken, the spare iron chains hung from their hooks, and the carter's leather coin purse remained tucked into his belt.

Men did not conduct an ambush in this manner.

Deserters, brigands, and barbarian war-bands invariably stripped iron, stole weapons, peeled mail from corpses, and looted every coin they could find. Yet whoever struck this wagon had ignored the silver, left four Roman swords and three coats of mail in the mud, and bypassed fifteen hundredweight of grain and meat. They had taken only four sacks of grain, three slabs of bacon, a handful of peas, and some salt, which was precisely the amount needed to feed eight horses and eight riders for a few days on the march.

Aper stood looking at the untouched weapons, and the wild rumours that had filtered down the Danube from Raetia during the winter came back to him.

Riverboat men and couriers had brought stories from Lorch and Aalen of garrisons paralyzed by women, and drovers at Boiodurum had been beaten for claiming Mediterranean women had sabotaged their corral. Aper had dismissed all of it as the shameful excuses of incompetent officers seeking to cover their own failures.

Looking at the dead guards, the severed legs of the ox, and the total absence of male greed, Aper realized that the rumours were true. The attackers were not deserters, and they were not men.

He stood in the cold wind, considering the consequence of that realization.

If he reported the truth—that four soldiers were dead and an imperial wagon stopped, only for the attackers to leave the swords, mail, and silver behind while taking four bags of oats—the headquarters at Lauriacum would ask questions he could not answer. An audit would begin, and any mention of women would make him look like a fool or a coward hiding behind the same excuses as the western commanders.

Aper turned to the decurion, his voice steady and flat.

APER
(voice steady and flat)
Get to work and clear this track.

NARRATOR
The troopers unfastened the harness chains and dragged the dead oxen into the ravine, sending the carcasses tumbling down the slope. They lifted the dead guards and the carter into the back of the wagon, hooked the optio's spare mule team to the rear axle, and levered the front wheels out of the rock face.

APER
Lead it down to the station. Put the stores in the shed, and lay the dead behind the sentry hut until the cart from Cetium arrives.

NARRATOR
Aper mounted his horse and rode down the pass through the falling mist.

An hour later, Aper sat behind the stone desk in the lower guardroom at Namare, dipping his iron reed pen into the ink. Before him lay the burned trilobate arrowhead, the broadhead from the ox, and the unpinned iron receiver pin.

To protect the command and his own standing, he shaped the report into a conventional military disaster, deliberately inflating the losses to fit the actions of armed deserters:

To Tribune Caeso Licinius, Administrative Transport Office, Legio II Italica, Fortress of Lauriacum.

From Marcus Ulpius Aper, Beneficiarius Consularis, Station Namare.

On the fifth day before the Nones of March, Station Namare suffered a dual, coordinated sabotage assault.

At the fifth hour, a diversionary breach was executed at the river barrier, utilizing an unpinned turnpike and an arrow-struck draught ox. Simultaneously, a high-poundage composite archer discharged a heavy trilobate shaft through the records room window, igniting an earthenware lamp-oil vessel across an open flame. The upper gatehouse and the complete customs registers of the district were destroyed by fire.

A mounted vanguard of eight shock cavalry forced the barrier before defenses could assemble, proceeding east at a hard gallop.

Subsequent to the river-gate engagement, at the sixth hour of the following morning, a four-wagon freight column descending the Dunkelsteinerwald pass from Municipium Aelium Cetium was intercepted at the hairpin defile.

The rear wagon was isolated from the column and overrun by the hostile detachment. Both draught oxen were slaughtered, the driver killed at the reins, and the three rear infantry escorts overwhelmed and killed in close combat. The vehicle was systematically plundered: twelve sacks of grain, four casks of salt pork, and all harness were taken, along with the weapons, mail cuirasses, and iron tools of the escort detail, before the attackers withdrew into the high timber.

The scale of the plunder confirms the hostile force is operating in strength, heavily provisioned, and actively re-equipping itself from imperial supply lines.

Certain auxiliary personnel, seeking to excuse their failure at the barrier, repeated absurd rumors that the assailants were women. I investigated the matter, dismissed the notion as a disgraceful fabrication invented to conceal panic, and administered disciplinary flogging to the men involved.

Station Namare is secured. The pass is cleared, and the surviving three freight wagons have reached the river wharves. The district customs archives are a total loss.

The hostile detachment is operating eastward along the Danube corridor, fully provisioned and armed, moving toward the Pannonian frontier.




NARRATOR
14 — Into Pannonia

Tigratavā returned mid-afternoon.

The haul from the wagon was already packed away. Tove had the slabs of salt pork wrapped in canvas, and the grain had been divided into four smaller sacks so the spare mounts could carry it without galling.

She dismounted by the picket line and let the mare drink from a runoff pool before joining the others.

TIGRATAVA
The river road is out. Word from Namare must have reached Favianis by mid-morning. The garrison there has outriders on the road, and they're stopping wagons and barges to check papers.

TIATA
Are they searching the high ground?

TIGRATAVA
No, they're sticking to the paving. Past Favianis the valley opens out, and there's no cover along the river anyway. But south of the road, the hills rise into a long timbered ridge called Mons Cetius. It runs south for miles, keeping between the river plain and the interior. It carries the provincial border, too—Noricum on this side, Pannonia on the other.

THANA
That works in our favour. A Norican officer won't lead his men into another province without orders, and whatever happens on this side of the ridge isn't Pannonia's problem.

TIGRATAVA
The ridge track will be slower, especially with the thaw turning the dirt to mud. But the timber runs right over the crest.

TIATA
We've got the grain to feed the horses now. Taking the ridge keeps us well away from their dispatch riders. We might as well get moving.

NARRATOR
The climb through Mons Cetius took three days.

The thaw had set in properly, making the going heavy. Snowmelt had turned the lower gullies into running streams of brown mud, and in places the horses sank past their fetlocks in wet clay. The stolen oats made all the difference; with good feed in their nosebags, the mounts held their condition despite the gradient and the heavy going.

They kept below the ridge tops, using old tracks made by local charcoal burners to stay off the skyline. Once, they had to pull the horses deep into the scrub while a convoy of timber carts creaked past on a lower track, the teamsters too busy keeping their oxen from sliding in the ruts to look up into the trees.

Near the top of the ridge, they passed an old stone marker half-hidden in the brush, recording the boundary between Noricum and Pannonia Superior. They rode past without stopping, leaving Noricum behind them.

Descending the eastern slopes, the country changed completely.

The tight granite ravines and thick forests of Noricum fell away, opening out into the wide, flat basin of the Pannonian plain. From the lower ridges, the Danube could be seen stretching away to the east, spreading into multiple wide, shallow channels cut through grey willow flats and vast stretches of reed beds.

Ten miles to the north, a smudge of smoke marked Vindobona, the stone fortress of the Tenth Legion. They skirted well to the south of it, using low swells in the ground and the hedges of native farmsteads to hide their movement.

By the fifth evening, they had made camp in a dry gully fifty miles inside Pannonia.




NARRATOR
15 — The Record at Lauriacum

Dawn broke grey over the gravel terrace of Lauriacum.

Inside the heated record hall behind the basilica of Legio II Italica, the air was thick with the scent of burning cedar charcoal and dry sheepskin. Tribune Caeso Licinius sat behind his oak desk, an iron ruler in his hand, checking the final quarterly forage returns for the Enns valley.

He felt a deep, professional satisfaction with the state of his office. Five days earlier, news of the disaster at Boiodurum had threatened to unravel the western border. Licinius had moved with standard legionary precision. Centurion Domitius and Auditor Hermias were four days out on the western highway, backed by twenty veteran cavalrymen, carrying direct warrants to impound the twenty-five surviving Norican stallions, arrest Vindex, and drag the station master to the procurator's court at Ovilava. Licinius considered the western border secure.

The leather entrance curtain parted, and the camp cornicularius entered the room.

Behind him walked an auxiliary dispatch rider, his leather vest and wool trousers stiff with dried river mud, his eyes red from an overnight ride. The man carried a bronze dispatch cylinder bearing a fresh lump of green wax.

Licinius laid his ruler on the ledger.

LICINIUS
From the west?

CORNICULARIUS
From the east, Tribune. Dispatched from Station Namare yesterday at the ninth hour.

NARRATOR
Licinius looked at the cylinder. Namare sat fifty miles downriver, guarding the rocky defile where the Danube cut through the granite bluffs toward the Pannonian border. The green wax bore the personal signet ring of Beneficiarius Consularis Marcus Ulpius Aper.

He cracked the wax with his thumb, pulled the papyrus roll from the tube, and unrolled it across his blotter. The writing was Aper's: the rigid, unsparing cursive of a veteran soldier detached from the legions to govern an imperial road post.

Licinius read through the report with mounting disbelief. A turnpike unpinned and an ox stampeded. A heavy iron arrow through a records-room window. A gatehouse burned to the flagstones, and twenty years of customs ledgers with it. Then, the next morning, an army freight wagon butchered in the pass, its escort slain and its cargo stripped of grain, meat, harness, mail and iron.

At the foot of the sheet, Aper noted that the surviving guards had tried to blame women for the disaster, and that he had flogged them for it. His assessment followed: a disciplined, heavily equipped detachment of military deserters, living off imperial supply lines.

Licinius set the dispatch down, his mouth set in a thin, bitter line.

LICINIUS
Aper did well with the vine-staff. Cowards in a ditch will swear to Medusa herself before admitting they ran from an enemy.

NARRATOR
He walked to the large sheepskin map of Noricum hanging between the cedar archive racks, and traced the course of the Danube with an unsteady finger.

At the western boundary sat Boiodurum. At the eastern boundary sat Namare. Between the two posts lay ninety miles of river, with the fortress of Lauriacum sitting right in the center.

The tactical reality struck him with brutal clarity.

Five days ago, convinced the threat was an isolated staff scandal on the Raetian border, Licinius had sent his only mobile reserve, twenty veteran cavalrymen, marching twenty leagues to the west.

While Domitius was riding toward the setting sun to arrest a horse-drover, an organized shock force had slipped past the fortress in the dead of winter, bypassed the legionary pickets, and struck fifty miles to the east.

Both river-gates of Noricum were in ruins. Twenty years of customs ledgers at each post, the financial spine of the portorium Illyrici, had been reduced to floating soot. An imperial freight wagon had been slaughtered in a mountain pass, its mail shirts and spathas stripped to arm a growing band of mutinous deserters who were now riding east with fresh oats, cured meat, and Roman iron.

CORNICULARIUS
(quietly)
Tribune?

NARRATOR
Licinius turned from the map, his face pale.

LICINIUS
Sound the assembly for the First Cohort. Double the rampart watches. Saddle thirty mounts from the second decuria. Detail an outrider century to comb the river flats between here and Arelape.

CORNICULARIUS
Are we pursuing the deserters, sir?

LICINIUS
(snapping)
The deserters are twenty leagues ahead of us. Aper dispatched this letter eighteen hours ago. By now that host has cleared Favianis and is pushing toward the Pannonian ridges.

NARRATOR
He returned to his table, swept his forage ledgers aside, and pulled two fresh sheets of Egyptian papyrus before him.

He could not bury this inside the walls of Lauriacum. The burning of two imperial customs registries was a direct assault on the treasury. The killing of Roman soldiers by armed deserters demanded the attention of the supreme command.

He dipped his reed pen into the black ink and addressed the first dispatch to the capital of the Empire:

To the Praefectus Annonae and the Imperial Treasury Chancellery, Tabularium of Rome, with urgent transmittal to the Senate.

From Caeso Licinius, Senior Staff Tribune, Legio II Italica, Fortress of Lauriacum.

Within six days, the customs stations at Boiodurum and Namare have been destroyed by fire, and the registers of the portorium Illyrici for both districts are a total loss. An imperial freight column has since been ambushed in the Dunkelsteinerwald pass, four soldiers slain, and the cargo plundered of provisions, harness, and the mail and weapons of the fallen.

Evidence compiled by Beneficiarius Marcus Ulpius Aper confirms an organized, heavily provisioned vanguard of military deserters, operating with Roman discipline and foreign archery. The arterial highway of Noricum is compromised. Patrols are deployed and an inquiry is begun into the origins of the mutinous detachment.

He sanded the ink, rolled the papyrus, and sealed the cap with the red wax impression of the legion's capricorn. Then he drew the second sheet before him.

If this armed force was driving east, they were heading straight for Pannonia Superior. He addressed the letter to the consular legate at the great fortress downriver:

To Legatus Augusti pro praetore Titus Fabius Titianus, Headquarters of Legio XIV Gemina, Carnuntum.

From Caeso Licinius, Senior Staff Tribune, Fortress of Lauriacum.

An armed force of Roman deserters, using steppe archery, has forced the eastern defile of Noricum at Station Namare and plundered an imperial freight convoy of weapons, mail, and provisions. It was last confirmed proceeding east at a hard gallop toward your frontier. Close all river barriers and detain all unmanifested mounted traffic.

Licinius pressed his seal into the second cylinder, blew the loose wax away, and handed both tubes to the waiting cornicularius.

LICINIUS
The first goes south over the Alps by the accelerated post to Rome. The second goes downriver with two fresh horses to Carnuntum. Tell the riders they do not sleep until those seals leave their hands.




NARRATOR
16 — The Boundary Stone

The convoy pulled out from the west gate of Brigetio an hour after dawn.

A pale, watery sun struggled through low river clouds, casting a greasy sheen across the limestone paving of the Danube highway. Optio Marcus Calpurnius rode his bay gelding at the head of the file, his spatha resting flat beneath his left thigh, his red wool cloak spattered with old mud from the barracks yard. Behind him clattered two auxiliary cavalrymen with shields slung, followed by the heavy four-wheeled freight wagon, four infantrymen on foot, and two outriders bringing up the rear.

The freight wagon was a heavy raeda, its four ash wheels banded in thick iron, drawn by two pairs of broad-chested Moesian mules. Arching over the timber bed stood five steam-bent ash hoops, covered by a cattle-hide tilt cured in rendered tallow and laced to the sideboards with greased hemp.

Inside sat the administrative lifeblood of Pannonia Inferior: six iron-bound cedar chests, ten wicker hampers of dry papyrus rolls, and forty pine diptychs. Every line recorded grain. Three years of delivery receipts, barge manifests from Aquincum, and requisition tallies proving the province had shipped forty thousand bushels of wheat north to feed the Emperor’s field army at Moguntiacum.

Sitting on the wagon’s forward bench beside the driver was Helius.

The imperial clerk wore three layers of dyed wool beneath a heavy sheepskin mantle, his thin fingers curled inside his sleeves. He carried the brass seal-box of the provincial procurator on a strap across his ribs, and spent every mile glaring at the road ahead.

HELIUS
Keep the mules to the crown of the paving. The vouchers for the Fourth Cohort are in the forward hampers. A single splash of water through the floorboards and the procurator will have your skin.

NARRATOR
The carter, a grizzled veteran named Blescius, spat over the near wheel into the ditch.

BLESCIUS
The paving ends at the creek, clerk. From there to the provincial stone it is black Pannonian clay, and the thaw has turned it to porridge. Worry about the axle before you worry about your ink.

NARRATOR
They crossed the stone bridge over the Concubia before midday.

As Blescius had warned, the dressed paving gave way to an unpaved military road. The winter frost had boiled out of the ground, leaving a trench of yellow slush and saturated grey clay that sucked at the mules' hooves with heavy, wet smacks. The heavy iron wheels cut deep ruts, dragging the axle-trees down until the timber beds scraped the high center of the track.

Two hours past noon, the road climbed a low swell of scrub and limestone rubble.

Beside the track stood a square stone pillar, four paces high, carved with the weathered titles of the Emperor Caracalla. The inscription recorded the boundary: Pannonia Inferior to the east, Pannonia Superior to the west.

They crossed the line at a slow walk, the mules blowing grey froth from their bits.

CALPURNIUS
Three miles to the mansio at Ad Mures. A dry yard, a roof, and hot wine before sundown.

HELIUS
(adjusting his mantle)
And a magistrate who understands the law. Superior has held back their audit receipts for two months. Once I present these warrants to their station clerk, the liability shifts to Carnuntum.

NARRATOR
A half-mile past the boundary stone, the ground betrayed them.

The road dipped through a willow bottom where the spring runoff had carved a blind ditch across the track. Blescius hauled on the leather reins, bracing his boots against the timber footboard, but the descending weight of the four-ton wagon carried the mules forwards.

The near-rear wheel dropped off a shelf of submerged limestone into an axle-deep sinkhole.

The iron tire struck the rock ledge with a sickening, hollow crack.

The wagon lurched violently to the left, throwing Helius sideways against the hide tilt. A loud snap echoed through the mist as the timber axle-spindle sheared clean through at the linchpin housing. The heavy wheel buckled inward, wedging tight beneath the bed, and the rear of the vehicle dropped into the yellow clay with a massive, sucking thud.

The draught mules lunged against their collars, squealing in terror as the wagon tongue drove into the mud.

CALPURNIUS
(bellowing)
Hold them! Get to their heads! Cut the traces if they thrash!

NARRATOR
The infantrymen threw their spears into the bank and waded into the muck, grabbing halters and throwing their weight against the mules' shoulders. Blescius scrambled down from the bench, his face white, and fell to his knees in the mud beside the crushed wheel.

He thrust his arm into the yellow water, feeling the shattered spindle, and looked up at Calpurnius.

BLESCIUS
(voice flat)
Sheared through at the shoulder, Optio. The spindle is broken inside the hub. We need a forge, a fresh timber, and four draught oxen to lift the bed.

HELIUS
(clutching the leather lacing of the roof, face grey with terror)
The hampers! The grain tallies! We are stranded in the road!

NARRATOR
Calpurnius looked at the western sky. The sun had already dipped behind the distant ridge of the Bakony hills, leaving the flat willow scrub washed in cold, violet twilight. The nearest station at Ad Mures was two hours away on foot through freezing mud, and no carter could move four tons of imperial records on three wheels.

CALPURNIUS
Uncouple the mules. Unship the spare baulks from the undercarriage. We wedge the axle on timber and bivouac on the verge.

HELIUS
(shrieking)
Here? In the ditch? We are in Pannonia Superior! The jurisdiction is unconfirmed!

CALPURNIUS
(coldly)
The axle is broken, clerk. Unless your parchment can grow four legs and haul this timber out of the mud, we stay right here until daylight.

NARRATOR
Night fell over the Pannonian plain, cold, damp, and absolute.

They worked for two hours by the light of a single horn lantern, cutting birch trunks with hand-axes and wedging them beneath the rear axle-tree to keep the cargo bed level above the water. By the time the timber held, the men were soaked to the waist, their limbs trembling with exhaustion.

They picketed the mules in the sheltered lee of the ditch bank, throwing out armfuls of damp marsh hay. On the narrow strip of gravel between the road ruts and the willow thicket, they built a small, miserable fire of green willow wood and dry horse droppings, the smoke hanging low in the freezing mist.

Calpurnius sat on an upturned oats tub near the hearth, wrapped in two wool cloaks, his spatha across his knees. He had set a single sentry rotation, four two-hour watches, and threatened to hang the first man who let the banked coals go cold.

Helius had checked the lacing of the cattle-hide tilt six times, his fingers shaking with cold, ensuring no damp air reached the wicker hampers of papyrus. Satisfied that the archives were safe, the clerk crawled beneath the tilted front axle, wrapped himself in two sheepskin blankets, and fell into the heavy sleep of total exhaustion.

By the second watch, the camp was dead silent.

The fire had burned down to a mound of red embers, casting a faint, dull ring of heat no wider than three paces. The soldiers lay curled in their sodden cloaks along the verge, their breath rising in thin, faint wisps.

Rufus took the midnight watch.

He was twenty years old, an auxiliary recruit from the Breucian lowlands, three months into his first enlistment. He wore a heavy wool tunic beneath his iron mail shirt, his oval shield propped against his shins, clutching an ash-hafted lance with hands stiffened by frostbite.

His boots were caked in yellow clay that had frozen into hard ridges around his ankles. He stood ten paces from the embers, keeping his back to the wind, his eyes watering as he stared at the dark bulk of the broken wagon.

The wagon sat tilted awkwardly on its propped logs, four paces off the road. The mist had thickened, clinging to the tallow-cured cattle hide until the leather gleamed like wet slate in the dark.

Rufus shifted his weight, his iron-studded sandals grinding softly on the gravel.

From inside the dark cargo bed came a sharp, unnatural sound.

Zzzzzip-thwack.

Rufus froze. His grip tightened on the lance until the ash wood creaked.

The sound had come from the upper corner of the tilt, five feet above the ground. It was a sharp, tearing hiss—like a seam of dried leather splitting under sudden strain—followed instantly by a dull, solid impact against the timber crates within.

Silence settled over the road again, broken only by the snort of a picketed mule.

Rufus stood motionless for ten heartbeats, his breath catching in his throat.

His mind raced through the afternoon's labor. The wagon was wedged on makeshift birch baulks, tilted at an unnatural angle, bearing forty hundredweight of dead weight. The green wood had been groaning all evening.

The prop slipped, he thought, cold sweat prickling beneath his wool arming cap. Or the lashing snapped.

If a tie-down had parted and the cattle hide flapped open, the river mist would soak the forward hampers before dawn. Helius would inspect the seals at first light, find the damp papyrus, and Calpurnius would lay the vine-staff across Rufus’s back until the skin broke.

RUFUS
(whispering into the fog, voice cracking)
Who's there?

NARRATOR
No answer came from the scrub.

He turned toward the fire pit. The soldiers lay like corpses beneath their blankets; Calpurnius was snoring softly into his beard. Waking the Optio over a creaking timber meant an immediate beating for cowardice.

Rufus laid his lance against the wheel hub. He walked to the dying hearth, picked up a dry pine torch wrapped in coarse tow and pitch, and thrust the head into the red coals.

The pitch flared with a soft whoosh, spitting yellow sparks and black smoke into the mist.

Holding the brand high in his right hand, Rufus walked back toward the rear of the wagon. The light threw his elongated shadow across the yellow mud, reflecting off the dark, wet surface of the leather tilt.

He reached the rear corner where the vehicle leaned lowest to the ground.

Three feet above the axle, right beside the second ash hoop, the light caught something wrong.

The drum-tight cattle hide bore a clean, three-inch slit, the edges frayed where something had punched clean through the leather.

Rufus stopped, his brow furrowing. He stepped onto the hub of the broken wheel, his boots slipping on the clay, and reached out with his left hand. He caught the stiff, grease-slick hem of the tilt and pulled it back from the timber frame, exposing the dark interior.

He thrust the burning torch forward through the opening to see what had torn the hide.

The yellow flame illuminated the cargo bed.

Right in the center, embedded thumb-deep into the cedar lid of an archive chest, stood a heavy ash shaft fletched with goose feathers.

Rufus stared at it. His pupils contracted in the glare. His mind stalled, completely unable to reconcile an iron-tipped arrow standing inside an enclosed imperial baggage wagon on a quiet Pannonian road.

Zzzzzip-thwack.

The second shot came out of the black willow scrub fifty paces away.

The heavy iron broadhead clipped the pine torch just below the flaming head.

The kinetic shock of the impact traveled down the wood like the blow of an iron hammer. The long handle levered violently against Rufus’s relaxed thumb, wrenching the torch clean out of his grasp with an agonizing, stinging burn across his palm.

The intact, blazing torch flipped end-over-end through the air, deeper into the dark interior of the wagon.

It landed dead-center into an open wicker hamper packed tight with two hundred dry, tightly rolled papyrus scrolls of the imperial grain audit.

For half a heartbeat, there was only the smell of hot pitch.

Then the dry Egyptian reed fiber and animal-bone glue caught.

A hollow, roaring whoosh tore through the enclosed space.

A brilliant wall of orange flame erupted inside the cattle-hide roof, illuminating the steam-bent ash hoops from within like the ribs of a burning beast. The flax ties snapped with sharp, crackling pops, and sheets of parchment curling into blinding white ash burst against the roof.




NARRATOR
17 — Ashes on the Border

Rufus scrambled backward on his hands and knees through the mud, his voice tearing from his throat.

RUFUS
Fire! Up! Get up!

NARRATOR
The camp erupted.

Calpurnius sprang from the tub, his spatha raised. Across the road verge, four infantrymen scrambled out of their cloaks, slipping in the clay, clutching shields and spears against an unseen enemy. Down by the ditch, the four draught mules went wild, braying in terror, throwing their heavy chests against the picket ropes until the hemp groaned.

CALPURNIUS
(roaring)
Form line! Shields up! Where are they?

NARRATOR
From beneath the tilted front axle of the wagon came a high, animal shriek.

Helius crawled out of the mud on his hands and knees, his sheepskin mantle smoking. He took one look at the roaring cargo bed, threw his arms into the air, and lunged straight for the flaming tailgate.

HELIUS
(wailing, eyes wide and bloodshot)
The hampers! The Aquincum rolls! The receipts!

NARRATOR
He reached for the burning wood with his bare hands, but Calpurnius caught him by the collar of his tunic, hauling him backward into the slush with enough force to knock the breath from his ribs.

CALPURNIUS
(barking)
Hold fast, fool! The hoops are coming down!

HELIUS
(shrieking, thrashing in the mud)
The grain! The procurator's vouchers! The duplicate manifests! Pull them out!

CALPURNIUS
(yelling back)
With what?

NARRATOR
Two infantrymen ran forward with their brass helmets full of yellow ditch water, tossing the muck against the sideboards. The water evaporated with a violent hiss, rising in clouds of white steam that vanished into the heat. The tallow-soaked leather burned with an uncontrollable fury, shedding ribbons of liquid flame into the cargo bed. Inside, the cedar chests cracked like dry bones, the wood splitting along the grain as the heat consumed the pine diptychs and blackened the wax into greasy soot.

With a heavy, grinding crash, the forward ash hoops gave way.

The burning roof collapsed into the bed, sending a column of white ash and glowing sparks fifty feet into the night sky.

The men fell back, holding their cloaks across their faces against the blistering heat.

They stood in the freezing mud, watching the fire eat the timber frame down to the iron tires. The four mules thrashed in their picket lines until Blescius and the outriders got ropes around their eyes, blindfolding the beasts to keep them from breaking their legs in the ditch.

By the fourth watch, the roar had died to a dull, hissing sputter.

The wagon was an iron skeleton. The ash hoops were gone, the floorboards burned down to charcoal ribs resting on the buckled axle-tree. A drift of pale grey ash covered the mud for thirty paces around the wreck, carrying the bitter, pungent stench of burnt animal glue, scorched tallow, and roasted papyrus.

Calpurnius stood by the wheel hub, his red cloak streaked with soot, his jaw set like iron.

He turned his gaze toward Rufus.

The recruit was sitting on an upturned baulk of birch, shivering violently, his left arm wrapped tight against his chest. His right hand hung between his knees, the skin across the palm and thumb swollen into an ugly, blistered welt where the friction of the wrenched torch had stripped the flesh raw.

Calpurnius walked over, his iron-studded boots crunching on the frozen crust, stopping a pace away.

CALPURNIUS
(voice quiet, flat, and hard)
You had the watch.

RUFUS
Optio—

CALPURNIUS
A torch. Inside an enclosed cattle-hide tilt. Two hours past midnight.

RUFUS
(stammering)
It was an arrow, sir. I swear it by the standards of the legion. An arrow struck the leather.

CALPURNIUS
An arrow.

RUFUS
(breath hitching)
It punched through the hide. I heard the tear. I thought the lashings had given way under the tilt. I brought the torch from the hearth to inspect the timber. I peeled the flap back, and I saw it, sir—an ash shaft standing right in the lid of the forward chest.

CALPURNIUS
And then you threw your brand into the papyrus.

RUFUS
(crying out, voice cracking)
The second shot struck the wood! The arrow came out of the willow scrub, fifty paces out in the pitch dark. It hit the pine right below the pitch wrap. The blow tore the handle from my grip, sir! Look at my palm! It stripped the skin clean to the meat! The torch tumbled into the hampers!

NARRATOR
Calpurnius looked down at the burned hand. The blistering was deep, torn horizontally across the thumb-joint in a manner consistent with a violently wrenched stave.

Calpurnius stepped past him to the smoldering wreckage of the bed.

He drew his dagger, stepped onto the iron wheel rim, and poked the blade into the heap of white ash where the cedar chest had stood. The steel clicked against hot metal.

Calpurnius wedged the blade beneath the object, levered it up, and caught it in the heavy wool of his cloak.

He blew the grey dust away.

It was an iron broadhead. The heat of the fire had annealed the metal, curling the tip, but the forged triangular fins and the scorched stub of an ash shaft were unmistakable.

Calpurnius held the hot iron in his palm, feeling the weight.

He stepped down into the mud and walked twenty paces into the black willow scrub, following the angle Rufus had pointed out. He stood on a low rise of limestone scree, looking back at the wagon. At fifty paces, in the dead of a misty night, the tilted rear opening of the wagon was a slot no wider than a man’s torso. To put a shaft through that opening, strike a moving pine brand, and knock the burning head into the only combustible cargo on the road required the marksmanship of a demon.

He found no footprints in the wet scrub. The thaw had left the limestone scree bare of snow, and whoever had stood in the brush had left as quietly as the fog.

Calpurnius walked back to the road, his stomach turning cold.

Near the ditch, Helius sat in the yellow slush, his knees pulled up to his chin, his hands buried in his soot-stained hair. The brass seal-box hung empty against his chest.

HELIUS
(muttering, rocking back and forth)
Forty thousand bushels. The receipts for the Fourth Cohort. The barge manifests from Aquincum. The winter requisitions for the Emperor’s army. <pause>
(whispering, voice rising into a sharp, thin rasp)
We are two miles inside Pannonia Superior. The stone pillar is behind us. Do you know what the Governor at Carnuntum will say when we march into his headquarters with four empty mules and an iron tire?

CALPURNIUS
He will say Pannonia Inferior never shipped the grain. He will say our procurator embezzled the wheat, sold it to the river merchants, and sent an escort across the provincial border to burn an empty wagon. He will call it fraud! Treason against the imperial treasury!

CALPURNIUS
(opening his fist to show the warped broadhead)
There was an arrow.

HELIUS
(short, hysterical laugh)
An arrow? You think the legatus will accept an arrow? You tell him an invisible archer loosed a shaft through the mist to burn tax rolls, and he will have you flayed alive in the principia yard! Men kill for gold! They kill for horses! They do not shoot arrows into papyrus!

NARRATOR
The clerk clawed at the mud, dragging himself upright against the wheel of a horse-cart.

HELIUS
(weeping)
The border will close. Carnuntum will refuse every barge from Aquincum. Superior will demand an audit of every granary on the lower river. And the Emperor is waiting at Moguntiacum with twenty thousand Rhine legionaries crying out for bread.

NARRATOR
Calpurnius looked east toward the distant boundary pillar, then down at the warped iron broadhead in his hand.

The whispers from the frontier posts along the Danube came back to him with brutal clarity. Stories brought down the river by terrified drovers and bruised beneficiarii. A customs post burned to the stones at Boiodurum. A gatehouse destroyed and an ox stampeded at Namare. An enemy that never took a copper, never kept a sword, and left Roman garrisons strangling in their own administrative wreckage.

The Limesgruppe.

Calpurnius looked at the weeping clerk, then at Rufus clutching his raw palm in the freezing dawn.

CALPURNIUS
(voice steadying through the dread)
Get the mules in hand. Cut the harness from the wreck. We march for the station at Ad Mures.

NARRATOR
He slid the charred broadhead into the leather pouch at his belt.




NARRATOR
18 — The Border War on Paper

The stone-walled compound of Ad Mures sat on a gravel terrace where the military road met the marshes of the Concubia.

It was an imperial mansio, built to service the courier relays and house the road police of Pannonia Superior. Behind a timber palisade stood four whitewashed barracks, an eight-stall stable block, a granary, and the tiled roof of the station tabularium.

Rain fell steadily through the slush.

Through the east gate filed the remnants of the escort. Blescius led the four bare mules, their harness chains clanking against their ribs. Behind them walked the four auxiliary infantrymen, their heads down against the sleet, followed by Rufus holding his swollen hand inside his cloak. At the rear rode Calpurnius, with Helius slumped over the pommel of a spare mount, soot caked across his face and robes.

A dozen auxiliary cavalrymen of the First Thracian Cohort watched from the stable rails.

Decurion Tiberius Claudius Varianus stood beneath the tiled portico of the station office.

He was fifty years old, born to an equestrian family in Savaria, with thirty years of provincial service behind him. He wore an iron scale cuirass under a dry green cloak, his silver signet ring resting on the bone hilt of his spatha. Beside him stood Censorinus, an administrative collector from the procurator’s office at Carnuntum, clutching a pine writing desk under his arm.

Calpurnius swung down from his saddle, his boots sinking into the wet gravel, and climbed the stone steps.

CALPURNIUS
Optio Marcus Calpurnius. Detachment of Legio II Adiutrix, out of Brigetio. Escorting the grain transit records of Pannonia Inferior.

NARRATOR
Varianus looked past Calpurnius at the courtyard. He counted the four mules, the bare pack-saddles, and the empty lead lines.

VARIANUS
You have four mules and ten men. The manifest sent ahead three days ago listed a four-wheeled freight wagon carrying six chests, ten hampers of papyrus, and forty diptychs. Where is the cargo?

CALPURNIUS
Destroyed.

CENSORINUS
Destroyed? Those were the harvest reconciliations for three administrative districts.

CALPURNIUS
The wagon broke an axle half a mile past the boundary stone yesterday afternoon. We wedged the bed on timber and bivouacked on the verge. Two hours past midnight, an arrow struck the cattle-hide tilt. A second arrow struck the sentry’s torch, knocking the burning head into the forward hamper. The papyrus caught. The vehicle burned to the iron tires before the guard could draw water from the ditch.

CENSORINUS
(voice hard)
Pannonia Inferior has been three months late with those vouchers. The procurator at Carnuntum has sent three couriers demanding the delivery tallies. The Emperor sits at Moguntiacum with twenty thousand legionaries demanding bread, and your governor owes forty thousand bushels to the Rhine depots.

NARRATOR
Helius climbed down from his horse, his knees shaking as his boots hit the gravel.

HELIUS
The records were complete. I had the vouchers under the seal of Aquincum.

CENSORINUS
Then produce them. Produce one leaf. Produce one diptych to prove that grain ever left the granaries of the lower Danube.

HELIUS
It is ash. Two miles east, in the ditch.

CENSORINUS
It is ash because you burned an empty wagon. They sold the grain to the river merchants at Aquincum, loaded scrap timber under a hide tilt, and dragged it across our border so the loss would fall on Pannonia Superior.

VARIANUS
An archer in the dark who shoots a torch out of a sentry's hand to burn tax rolls. That is the account you are bringing to Carnuntum?

NARRATOR
Calpurnius reached into the leather pouch at his belt. He pulled out the warped, heat-annealed iron broadhead and laid it on the balustrade.

CALPURNIUS
Found in the ashes of the forward chest. And the sentry’s hand is flayed raw where the wood was wrenched from his grip.

NARRATOR
Varianus picked up the iron head. He turned it over, tested the bent tip with his thumb, and tossed it onto the stones.

VARIANUS
A blacksmith in Brigetio makes ten of those a day. You bring an iron point and a sentry with a blistered thumb, and expect a clearance receipt for forty thousand bushels of wheat.

CALPURNIUS
(keeping voice level)
I command a detachment of the Second Legion under the mandate of the Governor of Pannonia Inferior. You have no authority to detain Roman soldiers on an imperial road.

VARIANUS
You are two miles inside Pannonia Superior. You have crossed our border with stolen mules, a destroyed consignment, and an impossible tale of nocturnal archery. Under the authority of the provincial legate at Carnuntum, I am detaining you, your men, and your clerk on suspicion of grand embezzlement and treason against the imperial annona.

NARRATOR
He looked at the cavalrymen waiting by the stable.

VARIANUS
Disarm them. Take their weapons and belts. Put the soldiers in the granary cellar, and lock the clerk in the irons room.

NARRATOR
Four troopers moved forward with drawn spathas. Calpurnius looked at them, then unbuckled his sword-belt and handed it over without a word.

Varianus turned back to Censorinus.

VARIANUS
How many barges are moving upriver from Aquincum?

CENSORINUS
Twelve. Loaded with winter barley and wheat, clearing customs at Cirpi and Solva.

VARIANUS
They will not clear.

NARRATOR
He stepped inside the tabularium and walked to the writing desk. He took up an iron stylus, dragged a double-leaf pine diptych across the table, and pressed the point into the black wax.

VARIANUS
Send two riders to the river towers at Solva. Drop the iron chains across the navigation channel. Seize every barge flying the flag of Pannonia Inferior. Impound the cargo, arrest the carters, and demand original physical vouchers stamped by the municipal quaestors before a single bushel moves west.

NARRATOR
He smoothed the wax with the flat of his thumb and drafted his operational report for the consular legate at the provincial capital:

To Legatus Augusti pro praetore Titus Fabius Titianus, Headquarters of Legio XIV Gemina, Carnuntum.

From Tiberius Claudius Varianus, Decurion, Station Ad Mures.

A detachment of Legio II Adiutrix out of Brigetio has arrived at this post with four bare mules and an iron wheel tire. Optio Calpurnius reports that the complete grain audit consignment for the imperial army—forty thousand bushels of harvest credits—burned in a roadside ditch two miles inside our border on the night of the third day before the Ides of March.

The escort claims an unseen archer discharged an arrow into the cargo tilt and knocked a sentry's brand into the hampers.

No evidence exists of tribal action or road brigandage. The mules are unharmed. The escort suffered no casualties. Collector Censorinus confirms that Pannonia Inferior has defaulted on three prior requisition deadlines.

I have placed the escort and Clerk Helius under armed arrest on suspicion of deliberate arson executed to conceal systemic grain embezzlement by the procurator of Pannonia Inferior. The navigation boom at Solva is closed to all westbound commercial and military river traffic pending physical verification of cargo.

This report is submitted for immediate transmission to the Praefectus Annonae and the central archives in Rome.

On the Ides of March, two days after the fire, the courier reached the governor’s palace at Aquincum.

Legatus Augusti pro praetore Flavius Marcianus, imperial governor of Pannonia Inferior, read the dispatch in the basilica of Legio II Adiutrix.

Beside his table stood Procurator Valens, the chief financial administrator of the province, his face red with outrage.

VALENS
(voice shaking)
They dropped the river chains at Solva. They seized twelve barges of prime grain. They disarmed an entire century detachment, threw Optio Calpurnius into a cellar, and put my senior clerk in irons.

NARRATOR
Marcianus laid the letter flat on the cedar table.

MARCIANUS
(tone dry)
They accuse you of burning your own wagon, Valens. They say you sold forty thousand bushels to the river merchants and sent Calpurnius over the border to stage an arson so Carnuntum would carry the loss.

VALENS
(shouting)
It is extortion! Superior has been short on its Rhine quotas since the autumn freeze! Their own procurator owes thirty thousand bushels to Moguntiacum! They saw our wagon break down in the mud, seized the opportunity to manufacture an administrative crisis, and impounded our river fleet to feed their own depots!

MARCIANUS
And Calpurnius's report? The arrow through the tilt?

VALENS
(snapping)
A local bandit. Or an off-duty Thracian cavalryman from Varianus’s own garrison! Superior has failed to patrol their stretch of the Danube highway. The axle broke on their road, the escort was attacked on their soil, and now they claim our records were a fraud to cover their own negligence!

NARRATOR
Marcianus rose from his chair, walking to the wall map of the Danube provinces. His finger traced the river from Aquincum north past Brigetio to the Concó stream.

MARCIANUS
Twelve grain barges sit tied to the banks at Solva. The wheat is rotting in the damp, and my soldiers are in a foreign guardhouse.

NARRATOR
He turned to his camp cornicularius.

MARCIUS
Summon two centurions from the Second Legion. Tell them to take sixty infantrymen and twenty cavalry to the boundary stone at the Concubia. They do not cross the line, but they throw up an earthen rampart across the highway. If a single merchant cart or imperial dispatch rider out of Pannonia Superior attempts to cross into my province, turn them back at lance-point.

NARRATOR
He walked back to the desk, pulled a sheet of heavy Egyptian papyrus before him, and took up his reed pen.

MARCIANUS
Now we petition Rome.

NARRATOR
His stylus carved deep into the paper, addressed to the administrative heart of the Empire:

To the Praefectus Annonae and the Urban Prefecture, Tabularium of Rome, with urgent transmittal to Imperator Caesar Marcus Aurelius Severus Alexander Augustus, Imperial Headquarters, Moguntiacum.

From Flavius Marcianus, Legatus Augusti pro praetore, Pannonia Inferior.

I report an act of unlawful military aggression and administrative sabotage committed by Legatus Titus Fabius Titianus of Pannonia Superior.

On the third day before the Ides of March, an official military transit detachment carrying the audited harvest tallies of forty thousand bushels of imperial grain was ambushed two miles west of the Concubia boundary. Due to the total failure of road security within Pannonia Superior, hostile elements fired the vehicle, destroying the duplicate receipts.

Upon seeking refuge at Station Ad Mures, the escort—soldiers of Legio II Adiutrix—was disarmed and imprisoned by Decurion Varianus. The imperial transit clerk was subjected to bodily confinement, and our brass procuratorial seal was confiscated.

Furthermore, the authorities of Superior have illegally dropped the river chains at Solva, seizing twelve military grain barges destined for the Rhine front, holding twenty-four thousand bushels of bread wheat under armed duress.

Pannonia Superior seeks to cover its own systemic supply deficits by pillaging the logistics of this province and leveling slanderous accusations of fiscal fraud against my administration.

I demand the immediate intervention of the Praefectus Annonae, the release of Optio Calpurnius and my soldiers, the return of the imperial seal, the lifting of the river boom at Solva, and a formal senatorial commission of inquiry into the conduct of Legatus Fabius Titianus.

Until imperial arbitration is rendered, the western border of Pannonia Inferior is closed to all transit from Carnuntum.

Four hundred miles to the west, on the Rhine, the imperial army waited in the mud of Moguntiacum for the grain that would never arrive.

Between the two Pannonias, the Danube lay silent. The river chains hung heavy across the water at Solva, forty barges banked against the reed beds, and two Roman armies stood behind earthworks glaring across a four-pace stone pillar.

Fifty miles to the south, eight women rode east along the empty scrub trails of the interior.




NARRATOR
19 — Down the River

Dusk came on cold and blue over the scrub.

They had pulled off the interior trails an hour before sunset, settling the horses into a dry wash choked with dwarf oak and hazel. The mounts stood picketed along the bank, cooling down under coarse blankets after fifty miles of hard riding, while the eight of them sat against their pack-saddles in the growing dark, chewing cold bacon and hard bread.

TORIA
That young recruit was a fool. I wonder what he expected to find in the wagon.

TARAN
(laughing)
He was so frightened of his officer he didn't know which way to jump. The wind made the cattle hide creak, and he thought half the forest was falling on him. When the brand dropped, he looked ready to lie down in the ditch and let the mud take him.

THANA
He couldn't have helped us more if we'd paid him. He put the flame right into the wagon. All you needed to do was make him drop it, and now two governors will spend the next three months blindly accusing each other of fraud and treason.

TIGRATAVA
They won't stay blind forever. Once the dispatch riders start moving between the river and the road, every post on this side of the boundary will have double watches on their horse lines.

TOVE
(chuckling)
Let them double the watch. I can just see the legate at Carnuntum trying to explain it to Rome: The wheat was there, Caesar, but then the night ate the paper, so we must execute the wagons.

NARRATOR
A wave of laughter ran through the group.

TIATA
(dryly)
Rome has survived three civil wars, but it has never learned how to fight its own parchment.

NARRATOR
She paused, letting the amusement settle, before looking across at Tigratavā.

TIATA
So where do we take the horses once we leave this scrub?

TIGRATAVA
We are fifty miles south of the border stone. The Danube runs east, past Brigetio, until it strikes the volcanic ridges above Aquincum. It can't break through the rock, so it takes a hard bend to the right and runs almost dead south. It cuts a wide trench through the plain for two hundred miles.

TORIA
What sits on the other side?

TIGRATAVA
The steppe. The Iazyges plain. Open grass, no Roman stone, and nothing to break the skyline. They are cousins to my father's people. Pannonia Inferior continues down this side. The military road runs along the western bank all the way down to the Drava and the Sava. Beyond the river junctions sits Moesia, where the Danube turns east again toward the limestone gorges.

TORIA
(eyes narrowing, grinning)
Why cross into the grass to hide from herdsmen when the Roman road is right on this bank? If both legates are glaring north at their boundary stone, the whole western side of the river will be wide open behind them.

THANA
She's right. Every post between Aquincum and the south will be watching the crossings or waiting for dispatches about the missing grain. Nobody will be expecting trouble along their own supply lines.

NARRATOR
Tiata nodded, turning it over in her mind.

TIATA
Sticking to this side of the river gives us water, timber for cover, and plenty of Roman infrastructure to bleed. If we bypass Aquincum through the broken hills, we can follow the valley down through Pannonia Inferior, and hit their posts as we go.




NARRATOR
20 — The Legatus at Carnuntum

The fortress of Carnuntum covered sixty acres of high gravel terrace above the south bank of the Danube, its ashlar perimeter wall anchored by eighty stone towers. It was the headquarters of Legio XIV Gemina Martia Victrix and the administrative seat of the consular governor of Pannonia Superior.

Inside the heated record hall of the praetorium, charcoal burned red in two bronze pans set into the stone floor. Snowmelt from the tile roofs dripped into the inner courtyard with a steady, monotonous rattle.

Titus Fabius Titianus, Legatus Augusti pro praetore, sat at a long cedar table strewn with quarterly strength reports, forage returns, and transport accounts. He was fifty-six years old, an experienced patrician administrator who had governed the province for four years. Beside him stood the camp prefect, Gnaeus Domitius Afer, a veteran of thirty-five years with cropped grey hair and thick, scarred hands resting on an iron-fitted belt.

The cornicularius entered and set an auxiliary dispatch cylinder on the cedar boards. The wax cap bore the capricorn seal of Legio II Italica.

CORNICULARIUS
From Lauriacum. Carried by road courier. The river has too much floating ice for boats above the bends.

NARRATOR
Titianus cracked the red wax with an ivory knife, read Licinius's letter through, and passed it across the table without a word.

Afer read it standing. When he finished, his mouth curled.

AFER
Deserters. In Roman mail, shooting steppe bows. What unit? Noricum has only the Second Legion and auxiliary wings that haven't mutinied since the Marcomannic wars.

TITIANUS
He doesn't say. He wants our rampart watches doubled and every unmanifested rider held at the river barriers.

AFER
Bandits take gold and livestock. They don't waste iron shooting oil jars to burn customs ledgers.

NARRATOR
Before Titianus could answer, heavy boot-heels sounded in the outer colonnade. The leather curtain was hauled aside, and an auxiliary cavalry decurion from the gate guard entered, escorting a dispatch rider from the road post at Ad Mures. The rider's cloak was stiff with frozen mud, his cheek cut by wind, and the satchel on his hip was wet with river sleet.

He brought his fist to his breastplate.

RIDER
From Station Ad Mures, Legatus. Decurion Varianus dispatched me at first light yesterday.

NARRATOR
He unbuckled a double-leaf pine diptych and laid it before the governor. Titianus sliced the linen tie-cord, angled the wax leaves to the lamp, and read the sharp Latin twice. He set the tablet beside Licinius's papyrus and did not speak for several moments.

AFER
Forty thousand bushels? The harvest reconciliation for the Rhine?

TITIANUS
(voice dropping into dangerous, icy calm)
Burned in a ditch. Two miles inside our boundary line. The escort commander claims an arrow out of the scrub struck his sentry's torch and knocked the brand into the hampers.

AFER
(with a dry, harsh bark of laughter)
On a wet night in the middle of a muddy road? What kind of fable is that?

TITIANUS
The kind an officer invents when his wagon is empty.

NARRATOR
Titianus rose and walked to the wall map of the Danube provinces. The sheepskin was stretched tight between cedar battens, marked in black and red ink showing every fort, road station, and river post from Raetia to the Black Sea.

TITIANUS
(tapping the ink line at the Concubia stream)
Look at the board, Afer. Pannonia Inferior is three months behind on its grain. The Emperor is at Moguntiacum with twenty thousand Rhine legionaries demanding bread. If those harvest tallies reach Rome, the procurator at Aquincum is exposed for a shortfall of forty thousand bushels. So what does he do?

AFER
He breaks an axle. He drags an iron tire across our boundary stone, stages a midnight fire where the water will wash away the ash, and claims hostile elements in Superior burned his consignment.

TITIANUS
And then he points his finger at me. He tells Rome that Pannonia Superior cannot secure its own highway, and that my administration should absorb the loss.

AFER
Except Varianus didn't let him walk. He dropped the chains at Solva. He has twelve grain barges tied to the bank, and Calpurnius locked in a cellar.

TITIANUS
Varianus acted without orders, but with the spine of a Roman soldier. If those barges had cleared Solva, Marcianus would have claimed the wheat was already delivered to our accounts, while holding back the paperwork.

NARRATOR
Afer crossed his arms and looked from one document to the other. When he spoke again, his voice was quieter.

AFER
Legatus. Look at the dates.

NARRATOR
He laid a thick finger on each document in turn.

AFER
Namare, five days before the Nones: an archer, a window, an oil jar full of burning records. Ad Mures, three days before the Ides: an archer, a torch, a hamper full of burning records. Nine days apart, on the same river, heading east.

NARRATOR
The room fell into heavy, dead silence. For ten heartbeats, the correlation hung in the air between them.

Then Titianus shook his head, his mouth setting like iron.

TITIANUS
(dismissing with a sharp wave)
No. Do not fall into that trap, Afer.

AFER
It matches the method. Archery in the dark. Targeting combustible stores. Hitting transport along the river road.

TITIANUS
(coldly)
It matches because every campfire along the Danube has been trading rumours of the Limesgruppe since midwinter. The boatmen talk. The couriers talk. Calpurnius sat in a ditch with a broken axle and a burned wagon, and he reached for the first ghost that would frighten an inquiry. He stole the story from the river taverns to save his neck.

NARRATOR
He walked back to the desk, his knuckles resting on the wood.

TITIANUS
Consider the consequence. If I admit that armed renegades crossed ninety miles of my province unseen, walked into the willow scrub at the Concubia, and burned forty thousand bushels of imperial grain on my highway, what does that say about my command?

NARRATOR
Afer did not answer.

TITIANUS
It says Legio XIV Gemina cannot police fifty miles of road between its own forts. It says Pannonia Superior is an open sieve. The Senate would demand my recall, and the Emperor would dock my legions' pay to replace the wheat. <pause> Whereas if Pannonia Inferior staged an arson to conceal a deficit and dump the liability on my border, then Marcianus is guilty of grand fraud and treason against the imperial annona.

NARRATOR
He drew the diptych toward him.

He looked at the cornicularius waiting by the door.

TITIANUS
Summon Tribune Lucius Baebius from the First Cohort. He will take two centuries of heavy infantry and forty legionary cavalry from the second decuria to Ad Mures within the hour. Baebius takes formal custody of Optio Calpurnius, the escort, and the clerk. They are held in close confinement in the granary cellar under legionary guard.

AFER
And the boundary?

TITIANUS
Baebius reinforces the river post at Solva. Mount ballistae on the two towers flanking the chains. If Marcianus sends galleys or soldiers upriver to cut the boom, he sinks them in the channel. No grain moves west until Rome answers my dispatch.

NARRATOR
He turned back to the cedar table, drew a large sheet of heavy Egyptian papyrus before him, and took up an iron reed pen. He would not allow an ambitious legate downriver or an incompetent staff officer in Noricum to tarnish four years of administration.

He dipped the pen into the black ink and addressed the imperial court:

To the Praefectus Annonae and the Urban Prefecture, Tabularium of Rome, with urgent transmittal to Imperator Caesar Marcus Aurelius Severus Alexander Augustus, Imperial Headquarters, Moguntiacum.

From Titus Fabius Titianus, Legatus Augusti pro praetore, Pannonia Superior.

An unmanifested detail of Legio II Adiutrix crossed the Concubia boundary without clearance and burned its own freight two miles inside this province, destroying forty thousand bushels of audit vouchers owed to the Rhine army. Its commander, Optio Calpurnius, has offered a transparent fable of an unseen archer, borrowed from river-tavern rumour. The procurator of Pannonia Inferior is in default, and has staged this fire to shift his liability onto my province.

As security against the deficit I hold twelve barges, carrying twenty-four thousand bushels, at the Solva boom, under guard of Legio XIV Gemina. I request an imperial auditor for the accounts of Pannonia Inferior, and authority to confiscate the barges for the Rhine.

Until imperial arbitration is rendered, the eastern border of Pannonia Superior is closed and the boom at Solva remains barred.




NARRATOR
21 — The Pannonian Gridlock

Spring rain had kept the Tiber high for a week, turning the quays below the Aventine into a swamp of yellow silt. At the Porticus Minucia, three thousand citizens stood in the drizzle waiting for the bread dole, while the Praefectus Annonae sat in his office surrounded by delayed barge manifests from Ostia.

In the Tabularium, the intake desk was cold. Vettius worked by the light of a smoking tallow lamp, scraping dried mud from an archive satchel that had come down the Via Flaminia.

The courier from Noricum arrived an hour before noon, his wool trousers stiff with dried salt and sweat. He laid a bronze cylinder on the table, bearing the capricorn seal of Legio II Italica.

COURIER
(voice cracked from twelve days in the saddle)
From Lauriacum. Direct from Tribune Licinius.

NARRATOR
Vettius read the first ten lines, stood up without rolling the paper, and carried it straight to Caecilius's table.

Caecilius read it with the tallow lamp pulled close. Two customs stations burned. A freight wagon stripped in the mountains. An armed band of Roman deserters riding east with legionary iron.

CAECILIUS
The Emperor sits at Moguntiacum waiting for grain and remounts from the Danube road. If armed renegades are cutting that corridor, the southern road to Germania is severed. Draft a transmittal for Moguntiacum. Urgent, under the joint seal of the Urban Prefecture and the treasury.

NARRATOR
It was sealed before the ninth hour. A fresh rider swung into his saddle in the Forum and spurred north along the Via Flaminia toward the Alps.

Ten days passed.

The rain cleared, replaced by a dry, cold wind off the Apennines. The intake at the Tabularium resumed its ordinary rhythm: quarry accounts from Luna, timber tallies from Etruria, municipal petitions from Campania.

Near the end of the month, two couriers reached the Capitol within the hour of each other.

The first wore the bronze badge of Legio XIV Gemina, his cloak caked in Pannonian clay from the Amber Road. He carried a pine diptych bound in green linen cord, with the consular signet of Titus Fabius Titianus. The second was an imperial rider out of Aquincum, his lips split by wind, who had covered seven hundred miles along the southern highway without a day's rest. His papyrus cylinder bore the red wax of Flavius Marcianus.

Caecilius laid the two dispatches side by side on his table and read them in turn.

On the left, Pannonia Superior: forty thousand bushels of audit vouchers burned in a roadside ditch by a transit detail from Inferior, which had invented a tale of an unseen archer. Fraud, Titianus wrote, staged to hide a deficit. As security, he had dropped the iron boom at Solva, impounding twelve grain barges, and set two centuries of Legio XIV Gemina on the river with artillery on the towers.

On the right, Pannonia Inferior: the same fire, the same ditch, and an ambush Superior had failed to prevent on its own highway. Marcianus called the barges at Solva an act of piracy, said his soldiers had been disarmed and thrown into a cellar, and announced that he had raised an earthen rampart across the military road at the boundary stone, with orders to turn back all traffic from Carnuntum at lance-point.

Each accused the other of treason. Each swore he was defending the Emperor's grain.

Vettius looked over his shoulder from one document to the other.

VETTIUS
They are at war.

CAECILIUS
(breath shallow)
They have entrenched against each other. Two armies, Legio XIV Gemina in the west and Legio II Adiutrix in the east, divided by a four-pace boundary stone, pointing spears across an earthen rampart.

NARRATOR
He seized both documents and hurried through the corridors of the Tabularium, down the stone stairs, and out into the Forum.

He went directly to the office of the Praefectus Annonae beside the Porticus Minucia. The grain prefect was an equestrian officer named Pomponius, sitting with two magistrates over bread accounts. Caecilius laid the diptych and the papyrus side by side across his ledgers.

CAECILIUS
(out of breath)
Read them, Prefect. Read them both.

NARRATOR
Pomponius read the diptych from Carnuntum, then the papyrus from Aquincum. As he took in the details, his fingers tightened on the edge of the table until the wood groaned.

POMPONIUS
(voice rising)
Solva is barred? Twelve barges? Twenty-four thousand bushels of bread wheat held under armed duress?

CAECILIUS
And forty thousand burned at the border. Neither province will send a grain west. Superior has closed the river. Inferior has blocked the road.

NARRATOR
Pomponius stood so violently his stool overturned.

POMPONIUS
(shouting, face mottled red)
The Emperor's army sits at Moguntiacum. They have had half-rations of barley since the winter freeze. The Rhine legions are mutinous. If the Pannonian wheat does not reach the Rhine depots before the spring campaign opens, Alexander will have twenty thousand armed legionaries rioting in his camp.

NARRATOR
He paced the room, kicking an empty scroll basket out of his way.

POMPONIUS
We must go to the Curia. The Urban Prefect must convene the Senate. We must order both governors to stand down, lift the boom at Solva, and release the barges.

CAECILIUS
(quietly)
The Senate cannot order consular governors to stand down. Both hold direct imperial mandates. They answer only to the Emperor. Titianus will claim he is preserving provincial security, and Marcianus will claim he is resisting an armed invasion.

NARRATOR
Pomponius stopped, his chest heaving.

POMPONIUS
Then who arbitrates?

CAECILIUS
The Emperor. Only the Emperor.

NARRATOR
They went straight to the Curia Julia to find the Urban Prefect, Marcus Clodius Pupienus Maximus.

Pupienus summoned three senior senators of the consular committee. For two hours, behind locked doors, they debated the crisis. Senatorial authority had withered under the Severan dynasty; none of them had the power to command two armed legions across a distant frontier. The legal apparatus had gridlocked completely: an iron chain across the Danube, and sixty-four thousand bushels of bread wheat lost or impounded.

By late afternoon, only one course remained.

Caecilius sat before a fresh roll of heavy Egyptian papyrus, dipping his iron reed pen into the ink. The dispatch was addressed to the highest authority in the Roman world:

To Imperator Caesar Marcus Aurelius Severus Alexander Augustus, Father of the Fatherland, High Priest, Tribune of the People, Imperial Headquarters, Moguntiacum.

From Marcus Clodius Pupienus Maximus, Praefectus Urbi, and the Senate of Rome.

The governors of Pannonia Superior and Pannonia Inferior accuse each other of fraud and armed provocation over forty thousand bushels of burned harvest tallies. The Danube is closed to all navigation at Solva, the military road is barred at the Concubia, and no grain moves to the Rhine.

We urge Your Imperial Majesty to send a judicial commission to the boundary, order the boom lifted and the grain fleet released, and command the mutual withdrawal of the legions. Without your direct command, an armed clash between the legions of the Danube is imminent.




NARRATOR
22 — The Iron Emperor

The courier from the Rhine reached the Porta Flaminia four days after the Kalends of April. He had ridden from Moguntiacum, changing mounts at the imperial postal stations across the Alps, and delivered his dispatches directly to the Curia Julia.

Urban Prefect Marcus Clodius Pupienus Maximus convened the Senate before noon.

The dispatches carried the seal of Gaius Julius Verus Maximinus. On the fourteenth day before the Kalends of April, the Rhine army had mutinied at Vicus Britannicus. Severus Alexander had been killed in his tent, together with his mother, Julia Mamaea, and the soldiers had acclaimed Maximinus as Augustus. The letter promised to uphold the laws and respect the senatorial order, requesting formal confirmation of the army's choice.

Severus Alexander had governed for thirteen years, maintaining civilian courts and treating the senatorial families with respect. Maximinus was an equestrian soldier from Thrace who had risen from the ranks and had never held a civilian magistracy. However, Maximinus commanded forty thousand veteran legionaries on the Rhine, while Rome held only the urban cohorts and the Praetorians. Without discussion, the Senate voted the decree confirming Maximinus as Augustus, conferring the proconsular imperium, the tribunician power, and the office of Pontifex Maximus.

Caecilius returned to the Tabularium after the session. The regular business of the office had halted; carters and petitioners had cleared from the Forum, and the clerks sat at empty tables.

Pupienus sent for Caecilius an hour later.

The Urban Prefect was at his table in the administrative offices behind the Curia, reviewing the courier logs.

PUPIENUS
The dispatch we sent north for Alexander. Where is it now?

CAECILIUS
Past Mediolanum, Prefect. It should reach the Rhine within six days.

NARRATOR
Pupienus looked at the map of the northern provinces on the wall.

PUPIENUS
Alexander would have sent an arbiter. He would have ordered an inquiry at the Concubia, audited the accounts, and told both governors to stand down.

CAECILIUS
Maximinus will break the seal on that petition instead.

PUPIENUS
Yes. And he was made emperor because his soldiers were short of grain and pay. Our petition informs him that forty thousand bushels of wheat records have been burned in a ditch, twelve grain barges are chained at Solva, and the governors of Pannonia Superior and Inferior are entrenching troops against each other across the military highway.

CAECILIUS
Titianus and Marcianus expect an imperial legal hearing.

PUPIENUS
They won't get one. Maximinus holds his position at the pleasure of the Rhine legions. If his troops are short of bread, he will not send commissioners to argue over duplicate vouchers. He will march the army down the Danube to take the grain by force.




NARRATOR
23 — The Blue Banshee

They made camp in a deep ravine a mile back from the highway, tucked well into the limestone breaks.

A fire of seasoned birch threw a good, steady heat against the stone overhang, the smoke lost in the canopy above. The horses were picketed along the creek bank, while Tove and Thilda lounged by the fire paring bacon into a bubbling kettle.

The brush cracked up the gully, loud and careless, long before the three scouts came into view.

THANA
Did you bring a parade back with you?

NARRATOR
Tigratavā came through the scrub first. Toria and Taran were not far behind and neither was making the slightest effort to keep quiet.

Toria was doubled over, laughing so hard her face was crimson, while Taran came strutting into the firelight with a wild, infectious grin plastered across her face.

TOVE
(tossing a chunk of bread across)
Sit down before you fall down. What's got into the two of you?

NARRATOR
Toria dropped onto a pack-frame by the hearth, threw her head back, and let out a great, ringing shout of laughter.

TORIA
(chortling)
Alexander is dead!

TIATA
The Emperor? Truly?

TIGRATAVA
(dropping her cloak by the fire with a broad grin)
Dead as mutton. Slaughtered in his tent on the Rhine. His mother with him. We had it straight from the imperial courier at the road post.

TORIA
(wiping tears from her cheeks)
His own men did it! The Rhine legions mutinied! They'd been sitting in the mud on half-rations all winter, waiting for the Danube grain that never showed up. Alexander tried to pay off the barbarians instead of fighting, so the soldiers dragged him out and put an end to him.

THILDA
(with a slow smile)
And who's the purple princeps now?

TORIA
(slapping her thigh, roaring)
A Thracian giant! Maximinus! A common soldier who came up from the ranks! The courier said he doesn't know Greek, drinks wine by the bucket, and he’s already planning to march forty thousand starving legionaries east to rip the grain out of every granary in the province by the roots!

THANA
(bursting into fits of laughter)
We broke a couple of wagons and the empire cuts its own head off!

TARAN
(shouting, waving both arms)
Wait, wait. That's not even the best of it! Tell them what they call us!

TORIA
(bellowing)
The Limesgruppe!

TOVE
(frowning, looking around the fire)
The what?

TORIA
The Limesgruppe! Fifty men! Fifty heavily armed Roman renegades! Traitors from the auxiliary wings, riding great big stallions and shooting composite bows that punch clean through iron mail! The officers swear on their mothers' graves that fifty mutineers are tearing the frontier apart from behind!

NARRATOR
Thana guffawed so hard she fell off her saddle-pack, rolling on the turf.

THANA
(rolling on the turf, guffawing)
Fifty men! Oh, that's rich! Those pomatum-scented legates would rather invent fifty traitorous Romans out of thin air than admit eight women stripped their border bare!

TIGRATAVA
(deep, open laugh)
It gets better. The tavern gossip down on the river has completely lost its mind. They swear the deserters have summoned demons out of the northern woods to fight for them.

NARRATOR
She turned her grin straight on Tiata.

TIGRATAVA
They say a giantess seven feet tall walks right through stone walls with an underworld reaper's scythe, slaughtering four Roman watchmen at Aalen and melting into the river mist!

NARRATOR
Tove let out an enormous, booming roar of laughter that set the horses whinnying along the creek. Toria was pounding the dirt with both fists, helpless with mirth, while Thilda leaned back against the rock face, laughing until she had to hold her ribs.

Tiata looked down at the massive, curved blade resting across her knees, her shoulders shaking, and let out a rich, booming laugh that carried straight into the night.

TIATA
(chuckling, thoroughly delighted)
A seven-foot giantess. I'll have to stretch my legs to keep up with myself.

TIGRATAVA
(grin turning toward the shadows)
And she’s not the only spirit they’ve got us conjuring. The river posts swear another demon has possessed a Roman gladius. They say the blade moves through locked gates on its own—floats in the dark without a footstep, opens a sentry's throat from ear to ear, and vanishes back into the walls.

NARRATOR
Tacita glanced down at her sword and smiled, letting out a quiet chuckle.

TORIA
(shouting over the din, pointing at Taran)
And what about Buch? Tell them what the patrol said about the culvert!

NARRATOR
Every eye turned to the Caledonian woman.

Taran planted her boots wide by the fire, tossing her red hair back with pure, swaggering defiance, and grinned ear-to-ear at the lot of them.

TARAN
(laughing, voice ringing loud and clear)
A blue-painted, screaming banshee! I like their imagination! It was just a girl in a tree with a little self-applied lapis lazuli—and a tuagh.

Historical Note

The termination of the Raetian stone wall at Hienheim is historical. Near the modern town of Kelheim and the auxiliary fort of Abusina (Eining), the plastered limestone wall met the north bank of the Danube and ceased entirely. From this point eastward to the Black Sea, Rome abandoned artificial land barriers and relied on the river itself—the ripa Danuvii—patrolled by the river flotillas of the classis and guarded by watchtowers and bridgehead fortifications.

The three-river junction at Boiodurum sits beneath modern Passau, Germany, at the confluence of the Danube, the Inn (Aenus), and the Ilz. The river Inn formed the provincial frontier between Raetia to the west and Noricum to the east. Excavations in the Passau-Innstadt district have uncovered Kastell Boiodurum, an auxiliary cohort fort on the Norican bank, alongside the Statio Boiodurensis. This post was a verified checkpoint of the Publicum Portorium Illyrici—the imperial customs district that collected transit duties across the Danube and Balkan provinces. Roman inscriptions discovered at Passau (including CIL III, 5691) explicitly document customs staff stationed here, including a vilicus (station manager) named Faustinianus. While official military transport and state remounts were exempt from customs tolls, Roman law required convoys to present valid travel warrants (diplomata, evectiones, and descriptive manifests such as notae equorum) for rigorous verification to prevent civilian merchants from smuggling commercial stock under the guise of imperial business.

Lauriacum (modern Enns/Lorch in Upper Austria, distinct from Fort Lorch on the Upper Germanic limes) served as the legionary headquarters of Legio II Italica. Outposts situated along the narrow defiles of the Norican Danube road, including Namare (near Melk) and Arelape (Pöchlarn), were routinely garrisoned by beneficiarii consulares—experienced legionary non-commissioned officers seconded to the provincial governor’s staff to serve as road police, customs inspectors, and dispatch supervisors.

Mons Cetius corresponds to the Wienerwald (Vienna Woods), which ancient geographers recorded as the natural and administrative dividing line between Noricum and Pannonia Superior. Downriver lay the major legionary strongholds guarding the Danube: Carnuntum (headquarters of Legio XIV Gemina and seat of the governor of Pannonia Superior), Vindobona (modern Vienna, base of Legio X Gemina), Brigetio (modern Szőny, base of Legio I Adiutrix), and Aquincum (modern Budapest, capital of Pannonia Inferior and base of Legio II Adiutrix). In 214 AD, Emperor Caracalla reorganized the Pannonian border, moving the boundary of Pannonia Inferior westward to the Concó stream (Concubia, near Komárom) to assign Brigetio to the lower province, balancing both Pannonias with two legions apiece. Solva was an auxiliary fort controlling the strategically vital Danube Bend at modern Esztergom.

The administrative gridlock depicted in the story reflects the genuine logistical strain placed on the annona militaris (the state military supply system). Vexillations from the Rhine and Danube legions had been transferred east since 231 AD for Severus Alexander’s war against the Sassanid king Ardashir I. That campaign concluded without a decisive victory by 233 AD, just as major Alemannic incursions breached the Rhine and Upper German frontiers. In 234 AD, the imperial court marched from Syria to Moguntiacum (Mainz) to assemble a counter-offensive, leaving the western frontier depots depleted and forcing the army to rely on long, vulnerable supply lines stretching up the Danube corridor to feed and remount the legions.

The climax of the book depicts the historic mutiny of mid-March 235 AD at Vicus Britannicus (modern Mainz-Bretzenheim). Reluctant to risk an offensive across the Rhine, Severus Alexander opened diplomatic negotiations with the Alemanni, offering cash subsidies to secure peace. Enraged by supply failures, winter hardships, and the humiliation of buying off an enemy they had assembled to fight, the Rhine legions mutinied under the direction of Gaius Julius Verus Maximinus (Maximinus Thrax), a veteran equestrian officer from Thrace. Roman soldiers broke into the imperial tent and assassinated the twenty-six-year-old emperor alongside his mother, Julia Mamaea. Maximinus was acclaimed Augustus, extinguishing the Severan dynasty (193–235 AD) and inaugurating the fifty-year period of military anarchy, civil war, and economic fragmentation known to historians as the Crisis of the Third Century.
