1 — The Same Fire. <pause> Brocchus took the morning watch on the western rampart at Aalen. The sun was already clear of the hills behind him, and the smoke was still rising in the west. It had first been reported the previous forenoon, a thin grey line on the horizon. By noon it was a column, and by dusk it was a wall of smoke. This morning it was rising from the same place, leaning north on the wind. Decurion Senecio came up the stair a short time later. He looked west for a while before he spoke.

SENECIO
Is that the same fire we saw yesterday?

BROCCHUS
It's the same one, sir. <pause> The night watch say it glowed all night, and they couldn't see any change in it towards dawn.

SENECIO
Did anything come in overnight? A rider, a lamp from any of the towers?

BROCCHUS
Nothing at all, sir. I had it from the night watch just now. They woke the beacon watch twice to be certain nothing had been missed.

SENECIO
What about Böbingen or Schirenhof?

BROCCHUS
Nothing from either, sir. Both reports last evening said all quiet, and we haven't heard from them since.

SENECIO
And the other towers along the road?

BROCCHUS
The same, sir. Every one of them reported all quiet at dusk.

NARRATOR
Senecio was silent for a moment.

SENECIO
Every one of them can see that smoke, <pause> and not one has thought it worth mentioning. Well, I'll take it to the Praefectus and see what he makes of it.

NARRATOR
Praefectus Alae Marcus Aurelius Antoninus Raeticus had been raised in Raetia under the emperor Marcus Aurelius Antoninus Augustus, known in the latter years of his reign as Caracalla. His father had named him in the emperor's honour, and he had always felt a personal attachment to the Limes Gate at Dalkingen, where a bronze statue of his emperor stood above the portal. When he received his posting to Aalen, he knew that the favour of the gods was with him, for it made him guardian of the monument to his namesake. Praefectus Raeticus was seated at his table when Senecio was admitted to make his report. He asked how long the fire had been burning and what had been done the previous day when the smoke was first sighted. Senecio had not been idle the previous day. He had discussed the smoke with one of the Praefectus's aides, and they had decided not to bother him with it until they had answers back. They had sent to every post on the Remstal road, and the answers had all come back by dusk with nothing to report.

RAETICUS
A haystack doesn't burn for a whole day. <pause> A farmstead goes up in an hour and leaves nothing much to smoke afterwards. So what does burn for a day, Senecio?

SENECIO
A fort, sir, or a vicus. Something with a great deal of timber in it, and probably a great deal of grain.

RAETICUS
And every post between here and it has nothing to report. That is the part I find hardest to accept. A fire that size can be seen from every one of them, and not one of them has anything to say about it.

NARRATOR
He considered it. It was not enough to justify calling out the ala. If he turned out a wing of cavalry for a fire he couldn't name, the whole province would want to know why, and he might be wrong. But he could not sit and watch it either.

RAETICUS
I want eight riders. <pause> Yours, since you've seen it for yourself. You choose the other seven, and I want them fast and light, with no remounts. Find out where the fire is and what is burning, then bring word back as quick as you can.

SENECIO
And if there's something out there to fight, sir?

RAETICUS
Then you'll have found something I need to hear about. You find it, you report to me, and I'll decide what we do about it. <pause> And Senecio, no heroics.

NARRATOR
They left within the hour, riding west along the ridge road. It was a good twenty miles to Lorch, and the going was slow — the road hugged the high ground rather than running straight — and Senecio kept to a pace the horses could hold without arriving spent. The smoke stayed ahead of them the whole way and never seemed to come any nearer. Böbingen looked ordinary. The officer of the day came out to meet them and said all was well.

SENECIO
(pointing west)
You can see that from here.

OFFICER
I can see it well enough, Decurion. But that's Germania, that is. Nothing to do with us. We've had a quiet week, we sent in the usual report, and there's been nobody through from that direction and no word of trouble.

SENECIO
Has anyone from this fort gone to look?

NARRATOR
The officer looked at the smoke, then at Senecio.

OFFICER
Look at it, Decurion? Whatever for? It isn't ours.

NARRATOR
It was plain the question hadn't occurred to him. Senecio thanked him and rode on. Schirenhof was much the same, although the officer there seemed less comfortable about it. Beyond Schirenhof the road climbed. At the top of the rise the smoke was no longer on the horizon but rose from behind the next ridge, and the wind brought the smell of it up the valley. It was wood smoke, with burnt grain in it, and scorched hide, and something else none of them wanted to name. Senecio halted, and the others closed up around him.

BASSUS
That's Lorch. <pause> It has to be. It's the last fort on the line, right at the bend, and there's nothing else out there big enough to make a fire like that.

URSIO
Then it isn't ours. <pause> Not from here on. Lorch belongs to the Germanic command, not to Raetia.

BASSUS
The Praefectus told us to find out where the fire is and what is burning. <pause> Well, now we know. I'd say that's the end of our errand, and we can go back and tell him so.

SENECIO
He said to find the source of it. <pause> He didn't say to stop at the province boundary.

BASSUS
And if the source turns out to be somebody else's fort?

SENECIO
Then somebody else's fort has been burning since yesterday morning, and nobody at Aalen or Böbingen or Schirenhof has been told a word about it. If it were ours, I'd want to be told. I don't see why that should be any different because it isn't.

URSIO
Perhaps they have been told. <pause> Perhaps everyone's been told except us.

SENECIO
Then I'd like to know why. Orders are orders — we go on to confirm, and then we go home.

NARRATOR
They came out of the trees to the east of Lorch, and Senecio halted them at three hundred paces. Beyond the wall, the column of smoke was continuing to rise, though less than earlier in the day. The gate was open and they could see the yard was deserted. The stables were a heap of charred timber with the roof fallen in, still glowing in places. The granary had lost its roof, and the grain inside it smouldered. A long shed near the wall had burnt down to embers, which flared up whenever the wind caught them. There was no sign of life anywhere. Ursio called out and his voice echoed off the wall. There was no response. A beam fell somewhere inside, and sparks went up over the rampart.

BASSUS
Decurion, there might be wounded in there. Men trapped, or lying somewhere they can't call out from. We ought to at least go up to the gate.

SENECIO
Look at the roofs, Bassus, and look at that gate. It's standing wide open and nobody is closing it. If there were a living man in that fort he'd be at the gate waving at us, and I'm not taking eight men into a place where the roofs are still coming down.

BASSUS
Then where have they all gone?

SENECIO
I don't know. <pause> That's what we're here to find out for the Praefectus.

NARRATOR
They circled the fort at three hundred paces, keeping it on their left. There were no dead men, which was the strangest thing about it. Three dead horses lay on the approach to the gate, stripped of saddle and bridle. Arrows stood in the turf below the wall, forty or fifty of them, some of them broken, and at that distance Senecio couldn't tell whose they were. There were cart-ruts on the road, many of them and deep, all running the same way. Senecio dismounted and inspected the tracks.

SENECIO
They've simply abandoned the fort. All of them. <pause> Judging by the tracks, it was the entire company, not a reduced force.

URSIO
So no large losses from the raid.

SENECIO
I don't think it was a raid. <pause> It's peculiar. An unraided, abandoned, burning fort.

NARRATOR
Senecio stood and returned to his mount.

SENECIO
Let's head back.

NARRATOR
Gripping the pommel to swing himself into the saddle, there was a sharp crack, and Senecio's saddle lurched under his hand, sending him tumbling backwards into the mud. The bronze buckle of his girth had shattered, the strap fell away, and the saddle slid off the horse's back to land beside him in the mud. He got to his feet and took hold of the reins to calm his rearing horse.

URSIO
Sir, are you injured?

SENECIO
I'm fine. <pause> Did any of you see where that shaft came from?

BASSUS
I'd say from the south, sir, judging by where it lodged itself.

NARRATOR
He pointed to Senecio's left, where the arrow was buried in the mud up to the fletching. Senecio walked across and pulled it out.

SENECIO
It's a trilobate head, <pause> and a very heavy shaft.

NARRATOR
A second arrow struck the cheek strap of Bassus's bridle at the bit ring, cutting it clean through, and the left side of the headstall dropped. The rein went slack in his hand. The horse tossed its head, free of the bit, and Bassus threw both arms around its neck to keep his seat.

URSIO
(shouting)
That was from the south, too! <pause> Both of them from the south, out of those trees! I saw the second one go past me, someone is in those trees!

NARRATOR
Then an arrow struck his own saddle with a thump. It stood in the saddle horn a hand's breadth from his thigh, buried a thumb's length in the wood. He stared at it, then took hold of it with both hands. It took him two heaves to pull it free.

BASSUS
That one didn't come from the south. <pause> There's no man in those hazels who put that into Ursio's saddle from where he's sitting. It came from the north.

URSIO
It's a completely different head, too! <pause> This one's a broadhead.

NARRATOR
They turned in their saddles. To the north there was open ground, a line of scrub, and the wall of the fort. Nothing moved there either.

SENECIO
We've seen what we need to here. <pause> It's time we were heading back with our report.

NARRATOR
He climbed onto the bare back of his mount and dug his knees in to keep his seat.

SENECIO
Let's go. Close order, at a canter, and nobody breaks the line for any reason. Bassus, can you hold that horse without a bit?

BASSUS
I can hold it, Decurion, though it'll be my knees and my prayers doing the work and not the reins.

NARRATOR
They rode east, leaving the smouldering fort behind them. More arrows came at intervals along the road, each one just when the men had begun to relax. The first went through a water-skin hanging from one of the saddles. The man swore, looked at his leg, and noted that the arrow had missed him by a couple of fingers. The second was driven into the road at a stream crossing, exactly where the lead horse would have put its forehoof. The horse shied, the one behind shied into it, and for a moment the column was a tangle of legs.

URSIO
(when Senecio had got it straightened out)
They're toying with us. <pause> That's what it is. Every arrow so close it could have killed one of us, and every one of them just missing.

SENECIO
They know what they're doing. <pause> And I don't like it.

TROOPER
I saw one. <pause> Back there in the birches on the left. Something pale, moving. I'd swear to it.

TROOPER 2
That was a bird.

TROOPER
It wasn't a bird.

TROOPER 2
I was looking right at that spot, and there was nothing there.

TROOPER
You were looking at the road, that's what you were doing.

SENECIO
That's enough, both of you.

BASSUS
(from the back of his unruly horse)
Sarmatians. <pause> Who else shoots like that? Who else would waste an arrow on a buckle just to make a point?

URSIO
Sarmatians? Here? <pause> We're a month's ride from the nearest one, Bassus, and you know it.

BASSUS
Then you tell me what they are.

SENECIO
Men who can shoot, <pause> and who want us to know it. That's all we know, and I'll thank you both to keep quiet.

NARRATOR
Further back another man muttered that the road was cursed and they should never have come. Senecio wheeled his bareback horse on him and said he would flog the next man who spoke. They came out of the woods with the horses blowing and the light going. Aalen was in front of them now, and figures on the rampart were turning to look. The last arrow came within sight of the sentries. It took the crest off Senecio's own helmet, close enough that he felt it pass. The Praefectus heard them in the principia with the lamps lit. Senecio stood with Bassus beside him, still holding his horse's cut headstall. Ursio told the story of the saddle horn. Raeticus let them speak. Lorch was empty and burning. The gate was open, and there was nobody at it. There were no dead men, but there were dead horses, and arrows in the turf below the wall, and cart-ruts on the road.

RAETICUS
Which way did those ruts run, Senecio?

SENECIO
West, sir, or north-west.

NARRATOR
Raeticus said nothing for a moment. The garrison had left, and nobody had sent word east. He put that aside for the time being.

RAETICUS
Now the arrows. Where were you when the first one struck, and where do you think the man who loosed it was standing?

NARRATOR
They told him. The south twice, and the north once. Then several more arrows at intervals along the road, and not one of them into a man.

RAETICUS
Not one.

SENECIO
Not one, sir.

NARRATOR
He looked at the lamp for a while.

RAETICUS
(at last)
I have seen this done before. <pause> On the lower Danube, when I was a young officer. Two Sarmatian horse-archers took a patrol apart over the course of a day, and they didn't kill a single man. They wanted the officer to understand that he was vulnerable, and that they were choosing not to kill. Two men, mind you, on their own ground, on horses bred for exactly that work.

BASSUS
Sarmatians, sir? That's what I said on the road.

RAETICUS
(turning to Senecio)
I know it's what you'd think, Bassus, but no. The nearest Sarmatians are on the Danube, hundreds of miles east of here. It isn't them. <pause> How far apart were the shots from the south and the shot from the north?

SENECIO
It was about a hundred and fifty paces from the south trees to where we stood, sir, perhaps two hundred. And the north shot came only a few heartbeats after the second one from the south.

RAETICUS
And the shot at the stream, and the last one outside the gate?

SENECIO
The stream was a mile or so down the road, sir, and the last one was here at our own gate, the better part of twenty miles from the first, near enough.

RAETICUS
And you were riding hard the whole way, at a canter, and you never once saw a man.

SENECIO
Not one, sir.

NARRATOR
Raeticus laid both hands flat on the table.

RAETICUS
Then consider it. One archer might account for the first three shots, or two if they were very good. But nobody does that and is also waiting at a stream miles down the road, and again outside my own walls, ahead of a column riding at a canter. It isn't two men. Nor is it a war-party, or every one of you would be dead. It's a band, twenty or thirty of them, spread out along the road and placed there deliberately, every one of them able to do that with a bow.

2 — The Archers. <pause> It was well past dark when Tigratavā, Taran, and Thilda rode in.

THANA
(from beside the fire)
Well? <pause> We've had all night to invent our own version. In mine you took the garrison single-handed.

NARRATOR
Tigratavā took the food Tove passed her before she said anything else.

TIGRATAVA
Nothing so dramatic. <pause> Eight riders came out of Aalen. I picked them up on the road well before they came in sight of the fort. They held at three hundred paces, circled it once, dismounted to look at the tracks and the arrows in the turf, called out at the gate — and rode off without setting foot past the tree line.

TIATA
They didn't go in.

TIGRATAVA
Not past the tree line. Whatever they came to learn, they decided they already had it.

TIATA
And you let them ride home with that. <pause> And nothing else to carry.

NARRATOR
Tigratavā looked at her. Tiata's mouth moved, close to a smile, and she let it go.

TIGRATAVA
Two arrows caught them before they'd got back into the saddle to leave. <pause> One took a girth buckle off clean. He went down in the mud reaching for the pommel. The second went through a bridle strap on the man beside him.

THILDA
I had the north, with Taran.

TARAN
(with satisfaction)
One shot. <pause> I put a broadhead into his saddle horn close enough that he'd have felt it pass his leg. What he said afterwards carried further than the arrow did.

NARRATOR
Thilda's mouth twitched, not quite a laugh.

THILDA
He wasn't quiet about it.

TARAN
Thilda took me ahead to Aalen after that. <pause> We wanted to be in position before they got home.

THILDA
There's a cut east of here that saves what the road loses going round the ridge. <pause> It's rough going if you don't know it.

TIATA
(to Tigratavā)
You stayed with the column the whole way.

TIGRATAVA
Yes, I stuck to the trees alongside the road. My mare is bred for open ground, not trees, but she never faltered. I gave them a few more arrows. One went through a waterskin — missed the man's leg by a hair's breadth. I put one into the ground just where the lead horse was about to put his forefoot. That one had several horses stumbling over each other.

TARAN
We were waiting for them outside Aalen. <pause> Close to the wall.

THILDA
That last shot was great. <pause> You took the crest clean off the decurion's helmet.

TARAN
(laughing)
Right outside his own gate, <pause> in front of however many men watching from the wall.

TOVE
The rooster lost his tail. <pause> He didn't have much left to crow about, then.

TORIA
You had all the excitement today. <pause> I want to be closer to the action. Even if I can't fight till my arm heals, I can at least watch the fun.

NARRATOR
Tove pressed a hand to her chest.

TOVE
I found you a nice little hollow with good water and thick cover, and this is the thanks I get?

TORIA
That's it. <pause> Too quiet for me.




NARRATOR
3 — Raetian Camp. <pause> They left at daybreak and made good time on the road, and by midday the land ahead had opened out enough that Aalen showed as a smudge of roofs on the horizon. Tiata reined in.

TIATA
This'll do for a rendezvous. We split from here.

TARAN
Thilda, with me. <pause> We'll take the east.

THANA
Tigratavā and I'll go southeast.

TIATA
(nodding at Tove and Toria)
Tacita and I take southwest. <pause> That leaves north for you two. <pause> Back here before sundown, whether you've found anything or not. That way we've still got light to make camp, wherever we end up.

NARRATOR
They each took their chosen bearings, and the four groups were soon out of sight of each other. Tove and Toria worked the high ground northeast of the town, keeping well back from the road. It wasn't long before Toria pointed out a break in the timber where the land fell away towards Aalen.

TORIA
This gives us a good view of Aalen.

NARRATOR
Tove reined in, and they both dismounted.

TOVE
You stay here with the horse, and I'll see if there's water nearby. Can't have you taking a tumble with your arm still like it is.

NARRATOR
She worked her way down through the brush and came back a few minutes later, wiping her hands on her leggings.

TOVE
There's a beautiful little stream just down there. <pause> Crystal clear, and a reasonable depth.

TORIA
What about cover?

TOVE
Plenty of that too. It's thick foliage right up to the ridge. I couldn't see any paths or woodsmoke anywhere close.

TORIA
That sounds perfect. <pause> And the view from here couldn't be much better either.

TOVE
(pointing through another gap in the trees)
That looks like another fort to the right.

TORIA
I think you're right. <pause> This has got to be the spot. Let's head back and tell the others.

Historical Note. <pause> The fort at Aalen housed the largest cavalry garrison on the Raetian limes, covering roughly fifteen acres as the base for the Ala II Flavia milliaria — an elite regiment of one thousand heavy cavalry. It stood on the military highway connecting Augusta Vindelicum (modern Augsburg) across the Danube to the frontier. The nearby fort at Buch, home to an auxiliary unit generally identified as Cohors III Thracum (a 500-strong part-mounted cohort), lay a little over a mile from the frontier and guarded the same approach. The Limes Gate at Dalkingen is the only monument of its kind discovered along the entire Upper Germanic-Raetian frontier. Following Emperor Caracalla's Germanic campaign around 213 AD, an existing border gatehouse was transformed into an opulent triumphal arch of dressed sandstone. Over 140 fragments of a larger-than-life gilded bronze statue of an emperor in military cuirass — widely believed to depict Caracalla — were recovered from the site and are now preserved in the Limesmuseum in Aalen. Excavations confirm that the gate and its timber guardhouse were destroyed by fire and abandoned during third-century frontier turmoil, traditionally associated with the Alemannic invasion of 233–234 AD. Archaeological evidence also shows that the portal itself had been walled off and blocked prior to its destruction, ending its function as a gateway. Since 2010, the conserved ruins have stood protected under a striking glass-and-steel enclosure. Moguntiacum (modern Mainz) was the provincial capital of Germania Superior and long-term base of Legio XXII Primigenia. Its legionary fortress, established under Augustus, remained in military use into the fourth century. Although the official provincial capital of Raetia was Augusta Vindelicum, the effective seat of military command lay at Castra Regina (modern Regensburg). Built in 179 AD as the base of Legio III Italica — the only legion stationed in the province — the fortress served as the headquarters of the governor, who held both military and civil authority. Its northern portal, the Porta Praetoria, remains partially preserved in the old town — surviving alongside Trier's Porta Nigra as one of the few standing Roman stone gate structures in Germany. The wider collapse of the frontier depicted across this book is historical, known as the Limesfall. The fort and palisade at Murrhardt sat along the Outer Limes of Upper Germania, where an earthen rampart, a split-oak palisade, and timber watchtowers guarded the frontier line. Although the permanent abandonment of the trans-Rhenish frontier territory (the Agri Decumates) culminated around 259–260 AD, massive Alemannic incursions began in earnest in 233 AD, burning and destroying outposts, watchtowers, and supply routes across Raetia and Upper Germany. That unrest did not stay contained to the frontier. Emperor Severus Alexander’s war against the Sassanid king Ardashir I had kept him, his mother Julia Mamaea, and much of the Rhine and Danube legions stationed in the East since 231 AD. That Persian campaign had wound down without a decisive victory by late 233 AD, against the backdrop of alarming dispatches arriving from the northern governors. In 234 AD, the crisis compelled the imperial court to abandon Syria and march north to establish field headquarters at Moguntiacum to confront the Germanic threat. The logistical unraveling of this expedition and the final collapse of the Severan dynasty are detailed in the Historical Note of the sequel to this book. Today, the Upper Germanic-Raetian frontier is preserved as part of the UNESCO World Heritage "Frontiers of the Roman Empire".
