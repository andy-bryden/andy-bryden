# 9 — The First Target

They broke camp before sunrise, and Tiata had already decided how the morning's riding would be spent.

"You asked me for a plan," she said, bringing her horse alongside Toria's. "I have the start of one."

"There's an outpost northwest of here — a watch-station, the kind Rome scatters along the frontier. A dozen men, perhaps fewer by now, forgotten by whoever's supposed to be rotating them out. That's not an accident. Rome doesn't forget things by mistake. It forgets them because remembering costs coin it would rather spend elsewhere."

"And that makes it worth our time because—" Thana prompted.

"Because a message needs a place to land," Tiata said. "You've been cutting patrols and supply trains for months. Rome absorbs that — it doesn't like it, but it barely slows down. A watch-station is different. It's a fixed point. It reports in on a schedule. When it stops reporting, someone has to ask why, and whoever answers that question has to explain it to someone above them, and so on, up a chain that doesn't like admitting a dozen of its own simply vanished off a frontier it swore was secure."

"You want them to notice," Toria said.

"I want them to have to explain it," Tiata said. "There's a difference. A raided supply train is bad luck. A watch-station gone silent, with nobody left to say what happened to it, is a question nobody in command wants asked — because someone has to answer."

Taya turned that over without comment for a while before she spoke. "How many men have actually manned it, that you know of?"

"I don't know precisely. That's the first thing we need to find out before anything else." Tiata glanced her way. "You'll want eyes on it before we're anywhere near close enough to be seen."

"I shall," Taya said.

"And after?" Toria asked. "Once it's fallen?"

"After, Rome sends someone to ask questions nobody can answer," Tiata said. "And somewhere far south of here, in a room none of us will ever see, someone with far too much authority and far too little information starts wondering whether the frontier he's been assured is secure actually is." She paused. "That's the plan for now. Not to win a war. Just to make sure the people who think they're winning one start doubting it."

They rode on into country that grew wilder with every mile — the road thinning to a track, trees closing in from both sides until the sky narrowed to a strip overhead. Before long the ground grew too broken and close-set with timber for the horses to press further, and they went the last stretch on foot, leading the animals into cover thick enough to hide both.
