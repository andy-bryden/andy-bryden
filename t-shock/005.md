# 5 — The Falx

Winter found them on the road into Dacia, a hundred and thirty years a Roman province.

Taya found the woman from a rise, downwind, unseen. She watched for longer than she usual, then came back to the others with her report.

"One woman. a local, not Roman — the falx makes that plain enough, and the fact that she's standing over a dead surveyor and isn't hiding the body." A pause, while she turned the rest of it over. "She's not hiding herself either. Looks like she wants to be found. By the right people."

"And are we the right people?" Thana asked, already amused by the question before she'd finished asking it.

"I think," Taya said, "she will know the answer to that better than we do."

They found her exactly as Taya had described, as unbothered by their arrival as Taya had suspected. The falx was still in her hand, as comfortably as if the possibility of violence were no more remarkable to her than the weather.

Toria spoke first. "You're a long way from anywhere that wants Romans dead badly enough to leave one lying in the open."

"He's not lying in the open for want of caring," the woman said. "He's lying in the open because I want the next one to see him."

It was a bold enough answer that Toria's mouth twitched toward something sharper — an edge she rarely bothered to leash when a stranger tested her — and it might have gone further than words if Tacita hadn't simply looked at her. Toria exhaled through her nose and let it go.

"I'm Toria," she said instead, and if her voice was a shade cooler than it had been a moment ago, the stranger didn't seem to notice, or didn't care to. "We're headed north. You're welcome to ride with us, if you've a mind to."

"I have a camp a half hour from here," the woman said, with a glance that took in the two tired horses and the four women. "You'll want a rest before going too much further." She turned toward a picket line half-hidden in the brush, where a horse of her own stood already saddled, and swung up onto it without waiting to see if they'd follow.

Her name was Tiata, and her camp was better kept than four fugitives expected from one woman alone — dry wood stacked, water close, the fire built low and smokeless in a hollow. She moved through her own space with the same flat certainty she'd shown standing over the body, and soon the five of them were settled around the fire.

It was Thana who broke the quiet first, because something about Tiata's stillness was needling her.

"You kill Roman surveyors for sport, or is there a cause behind it somewhere?"

"There's a province behind it," Tiata said, without heat. "A hundred and thirty years of one. You'd know something about causes, I'd think, given how loudly you don't have one."

Thana's smile didn't move, but her eyes narrowed. "I have plenty of cause. I simply don't feel the need to announce it to strangers with strange swords."

"Falx," Tiata said.

"I know what it is."

"Then you'll know it's not for show either." Tiata turned the blade slightly where it lay across her knees. "You wear your skill the way some men wear rank — like it's owed to you rather than earned. I've known officers like that. Rome's full of them. They tend not to survive contact with people who actually had to earn what they carry."

The fire cracked. Nobody else spoke.

Thana's jaw tightened, just slightly — the first crack any of them had seen in that permanent, easy smirk since the arena. "You don't know anything about what I earned." She hissed. 

"Then tell me," Tiata said flatly.

Thana looked at the fire for a long moment before she answered, and when she did, her face had become sober.

"I wasn't born to a cage," she said. "My family had standing — not wealth, but standing, in a town on the coast that many thought too small for pirates to bother with. We were wrong about that." She turned a piece of kindling over in her hands. "I was fourteen. They didn't take the town. A dozen men came ashore at night, took what they could carry that breathed, and were gone before anyone could raise an alarm."

"The market at Delos, or somewhere smaller?" Tiata asked, not unkindly.

"Smaller. It doesn't matter. What matters is the man who bought me didn't buy me for my arms or my back. He watched me for one day and he told his overseer I'd last longer than the fighters twice my size, because I wasn't going to make the mistake of fighting fair." Something almost like the old smirk flickered back, thin and bitter now. "He was right. Eleven bouts. Eleven women who thought strength was the only thing that mattered in that sand, and eleven times I let them believe it right up until it killed them."

"And the arrogance?" Tiata asked.

"It's not arrogance," Thana said. "It's arithmetic. I already know how most fights end before they start. I've simply never found it useful to pretend otherwise."

Tiata studied her for a moment longer, then nodded once. "Good," she said. "I can use arithmetic."

It wasn't exactly warmth that passed between them after that, but it was something neither of them had offered a stranger before. Toria let the last of her earlier temper go now that she understood there was nothing in the woman's bluntness aimed at any of them personally.

They sat around the fire long after the wood had burned down to nothing, and for the first time since Byzantium, the silence between them was truly comfortable.
