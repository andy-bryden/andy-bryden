# 27 — The Ambush
 
Marcellus had known his cousin since they were boys, and he had never seen him the way he'd looked that morning — armor half-fastened, giving orders that changed shape halfway through the giving of them.
 
That was why Marcellus hadn't argued when the command fell to him. Someone needed to ride out with a clear head, and his cousin's wasn't one this morning.
 
"Take what you need," the Praefectus told him, gripping his forearm a moment too long. "Bring back the dead. Bring back the truth of it, whatever it turns out to be. I'd rather know than go on not knowing."
 
Marcellus said he would. He'd have gone regardless of what his cousin asked of him. It happened his cousin had asked.
 
He rode out with a full century, foot and horse both — more men than the errand strictly needed, and exactly as many as the uncertainty did. Half the column had heard some version of the boy's story by the time they mounted. He didn't correct any of it. Wary men made fewer mistakes than confident ones, and this morning called for fewer mistakes than most.
 
They found the site inside the hour. Marcellus knew it before anyone told him — the stillness that settles over ground where men have died and nobody has come yet to argue with the fact of it.
 
Four bodies, roughly where the boy had said. Marcellus dismounted himself rather than send a man ahead.
 
He knelt by the nearest of them a long while.
 
The wounds were clean. Not hacked at in panic — placed, each of them, and placed by more than one hand working with the same discipline. Whoever had done this hadn't been afraid while they did it.
 
"Recover them," he said, straightening. "Gently. They rode out as my men. They come home as my men."
 
The century went to the work of it. Horses stood. Weapons went, for a while, out of most men's hands.
 
He walked the ground himself while they worked — drag marks half-swallowed by grass nobody had disturbed further, churned earth where more than four had clearly stood and fought, and then nothing. No trail leading off in any one direction. Whoever had done this had come from everywhere and gone back to it the same way.
 
A shout went up from the treeline to the north. Not panic yet — just a man's voice pitched to say *something's here.*
 
Marcellus was moving before he'd decided to move, four infantrymen falling in around him. "Hold the recovery," he called back to his optio. "Cavalry stays with the dead. I'll see what that is."
 
He didn't get thirty paces into the trees.
 
The man leading his file went down without a sound — a short, choked exhale, already falling before Marcellus had registered what he was seeing. An arrow buried to the fletching in the side of his throat.
 
"Down! Cover!" His own sword was clear before the second man's shout finished, four infantrymen scattering for whatever the ground offered, eyes on trees that gave nothing back.
 
Nothing came. No second shot found any of them.
 
From the south — too far to be the same hand, too close to be chance — a second shout went up, and behind it the sound of another man falling wrong.
 
Marcellus understood, before the sound had fully reached him, that this wasn't one threat in one place. His century was already coming apart at seams he hadn't accounted for.
 
He turned back toward the horses. Toward the dead. Toward the one part of the morning he'd trusted enough to leave lightly guarded.
 
He didn't reach them in time to matter.
 
"Cavalry, mount!" he shouted, already running for his own horse, the recovery abandoned where it stood. Two threats, maybe more, in ground his men didn't know — the only answer left was to get the century moving as one body instead of scattered pieces, and that started with getting every rider up.
 
Men were already swinging into saddles around him, horses shifting, unsettled by the shouting and the smell of blood already thick from the four dead being loaded. Marcellus reached his own mount and had one hand on the pommel, one foot already leaving the ground, when the horse reared under him.
 
It went up hard and sudden, no warning in it, no cause he could see — and he was thrown back off it before he'd gotten his weight over the saddle, hitting the ground with the wind knocked clean out of him.
 
He didn't see the dart. He only understood, lying on his back with the sky swimming and his own horse screaming somewhere above him, that whatever had put it there hadn't been aiming for him at all.
 
Then the rest of it came down at once.
 
A woman broke from the treeline to his left, a spear already leveled, and put it into the chest of the nearest mount before the rider had finished cursing his own horse's panic. Another came from the right with an axe, short and heavy, swinging low and taking a second horse's legs out from under it in a single stroke — the animal screaming, going down sideways, the rider caught half under it.
 
Behind him, at the rear of the line, two more shapes moved through the tangle with blades already working, and Marcellus, still fighting to get air back into his lungs, understood with a cold, distant clarity that this hadn't been aimed at his men at all. It was aimed at the horses. Every one of them.
 
He rolled, got a hand under himself, and looked up in time to see her.
 
She came through the chaos without hurrying, a curved blade in one hand, and went straight for his own horse where it still reared and screamed above him — one swing, low and hard, and the animal's foreleg buckled under it and it went down in a heap that missed him by less than an arm's length. She didn't stop to watch it fall. She was already moving to the next.
 
Around him the whole cavalry line was coming apart into exactly the kind of ruin he'd have called impossible an hour before — horses down, horses bolting into each other, riders thrown or trapped or simply gone, the ordered shape of a century dissolving into noise and blood and animals dying in ways no discipline could answer.
 
He tried to get to his feet. He didn't get far.
 
A hand closed over his shoulder from behind, and a blade found his throat before he'd finished turning into whoever held it — close, controlled, no wasted motion in it at all. He went still. Whoever this was had done this before, and knew exactly how much pressure it took to make a man understand that stillness was the only thing keeping him alive.
 
"Don't," a voice said, low, near his ear. Not shouted. It didn't need to be.
 
He didn't.
 
A second woman appeared at his other side, matching pace as he was hauled to his feet and moved, half-walking and half-dragged, away from the wreckage of his own command. She gripped his arm with a power he would never have expected from a woman, and a faint smirk lifted one corner of her mouth.
 
"You shaved well this morning, centurion," she said, close to his ear. "Don't make us do it again now."
 
The blade against his throat remained as steady as a plumb line.
 
Around him, the screaming of horses and men swallowed whatever sound he might have made if he'd been fool enough to make one.
 
He caught fragments of it as they moved him — a third shape, an axe, a fourth with a spear, all of it already withdrawing as quickly as it had come, the whole assault folding itself back into the trees the way it had come out of them, leaving behind a heaving, dying mass of horseflesh and men too disoriented to know yet what had actually happened to them.
 
Nobody was looking at him. That was the thing he understood last and worst of all, dragged backward into cover with a blade still resting light against his skin — in all that noise and blood, not one man of his own century so much as glanced his way to see that their commander was being taken.
