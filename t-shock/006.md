# Chapter Six — A Shape for the Anger

Morning came grey and close to the ground, the fire down to embers, and Tiata was the first of them awake — though none of them had actually seen her sleep, which by now surprised no one.

She didn't ease into the day the way the others had learned to. She simply asked.

"Where are you headed?"

It landed differently than it might have from anyone else. Taya would have asked it to read them. Thana would have asked it to needle. Tiata asked it the way she asked everything — flatly, expecting an actual answer, and it was that expectation, more than the question itself, that seemed to catch Toria off guard.

She opened her mouth to say *north*, or *away*, the answer that had been true enough for months now to stop requiring thought. What came out instead surprised her as much as anyone.

"We don't entirely know," Toria admitted. "We started out running. That's simple enough to understand — Byzantium wasn't going to let three escaped gladiatrices become an inconvenience it lived with." She glanced at Thana, who picked it up without missing the handoff, the way they'd learned to do without discussing it.

"But running stops being the whole of it, eventually," Thana said. "There's only so far you can go before *away* isn't a direction anymore, just a habit. We've killed enough patrols by now, taken enough supply, put enough dead officers back on Roman desks as a problem someone has to explain — it's occurred to more than one of us that we're not just avoiding the empire anymore. We're already hurting it. Small cuts. Nothing it can't absorb."

"But cuts add up," Toria said, and there was something in her voice now that hadn't been there a moment ago — not the habitual quickness, something closer to conviction finding its own shape as she spoke it aloud for the first time. "If it were just cuts forever, fine. But there are garrisons out past where anyone bothers to look — frontier postings, undermanned, half-forgotten by whoever's supposed to be minding them. Hit the right one, hard enough, in the right place, and it isn't a supply raid anymore. It's a message that travels further than we do. Rome doesn't fear losing men. It fears looking like it's losing control of what it already holds."

"You mean to make the empire flinch," Tiata said. Not a question.

"I mean to make it bleed somewhere it can't hide the wound," Toria said. "Whether that's the same thing or not, I haven't decided yet."

Tiata was quiet for a while after that, turning the falx over where it lay across her knees, the same idle-looking gesture that never actually looked idle on her. When she finally spoke again, it wasn't to Toria at all.

"That's not a plan," she said. "That's an appetite with a direction. Which isn't nothing — appetite is more than most people manage. But it isn't a plan yet either."

Nobody argued with her. It didn't occur to any of them to.

"I'm not ready to move on this morning," Tiata went on, standing, banking the last of the fire with the flat of her boot without seeming to think about the motion at all. "There's a matter or two I haven't finished settling in this province, and I'd rather not leave it half done. But I find I don't mind the company while I finish it."

It wasn't an offer, exactly. It wasn't phrased as one. But all four of them understood it for what it was, and none of them mistook it for anything less than what it actually meant — that whatever this had been until now, a coincidence of four women who happened to move well together, was becoming something with a shape to it. Something that would need more than appetite and a direction to survive contact with an empire that could afford to lose entire legions and barely notice.

Nobody said any of that aloud. There was no need to. Taya caught Toria's eye across the dead fire, and Toria caught Thana's, and even Tacita, watching all of it with her usual unreadable stillness, allowed something in her expression to settle that hadn't been settled before.

They had been four women surviving by their own separate wits, tolerating each other's company because it happened, so far, to work. What stood in front of them now, banking a fire with the same unhurried certainty she did everything else, was the first among them who didn't survive by wits alone. She survived by design. And design was the one thing none of the rest of them, for all their skill, had ever quite managed to bring to what they were doing.

Whatever came next — and something would, they all understood that now, whether it was one more garrison or an empire's worth of them — it was no longer going to happen to them.

It was going to be planned.
