# 18 — Blind to the West

Taya left before the others had finished breaking camp. No goodbyes, no last-minute cautions — she preferred it that way. She took the mare and nothing that could rattle or catch light, and by the time the sun cleared the trees she was three ridges west of where the frontier bent north.

Alemanni farmsteads first, scattered and new, the wood still pale and unweathered. She noted two homesteads, the distance between them, which way the smoke leaned in the wind — nothing useful today, but worth remembering.

Past the farmsteads the land roughened into forest. She slowed the mare to a walk and read the ground at that pace instead.

She found the trail by midmorning: old but used, running the direction they meant to go, worn by feet rather than hooves. People without horses to spare. Poorer country, and likely the loose, unwatched ground Thilda had promised. She followed it from the saddle, further west than she'd meant to go on the first day, because it kept giving her reason to keep going.

By early afternoon she understood why.

The trail crossed a ford — shallow, old, worn smooth by long use, with nothing standing guard over it. No palisade. No watchtower. Every Roman crossing she'd ever scouted had one, even a poorly manned one. This had none. She sat above it a while, working out what Thilda had meant back at the fire — not ground Rome had forgotten, ground Rome had never bothered learning about in the first place.

That alone would have been enough for the day.

She followed the far bank north a while instead, out of habit, and that was when she saw it — straight lines against the treeline, where nothing straight grew on its own.

A fort. Not a watch-station. Stone footings, from what she could make out, and enough of a footprint to house a real garrison. She spent the rest of the afternoon working the treeline in a slow half-circle, getting close enough to be sure without being seen.

It sat at the exact point where the frontier turned north to follow the river and stopped watching west. The walls faced east and south, back the way the frontier came from. On the west side, behind the fort itself, a supply track ran wide and open — wagons coming and going on a plain schedule, because nobody expected anyone to be watching from that direction.

She counted what she could see. Enough men to matter, not a skeleton crew. Walls too strong to take head-on with seven, the way they'd taken the wooden station months before. But that supply road was undefended, and the whole fort was built facing the wrong way.

This was the target Toria had described, back before any of this had a plan behind it — a fixed point, not a scratch Rome could shrug off. Nobody had known then where to find one. Now there was one, sitting exposed on its blind side.

She could have moved on it herself that night. A courier on the road, a careless watch — she'd done that kind of work alone for three winters and still knew how. One woman could have taken something small from that fort tonight.

She didn't. A raid like that would cost the fort one man and put the rest on alert for nothing. Seven women, working from what she'd learned, could hit that supply road hard enough to spook the whole garrison — enough dead couriers, enough silence where reports should be, that the men inside started doubting the ground they thought was safe behind them. She marked the road, the blind approach, and the two places a rider could cross the ford unseen, then turned back before the light went.

It was full dusk by the time she reached the hollow, later than she'd said she'd be. Tiata was on her feet before Taya had cleared the treeline.

"You found something," Tiata said. Not a question.

"I found the garrison," Taya said.

The others gathered without being called. Taya laid it out plainly — the ford, the fort, the supply road nobody was watching.

"Stone-built. Real numbers, not a skeleton crew. We're not walking in and clearing it the way we cleared the watch-station." She looked at Toria. "But it's the one you asked for. Back before Tiata, before any of this had a plan. That road is wide open. We can't take the fort. We can make the men inside it stop trusting it."

Toria was quiet a moment. "How long to plan it properly?"

"Longer than tonight," Taya said. "But it can be done. I saw how."

Tiata looked around the fire once — Toria still, Thana already turning the shape of it over, Tacita watching, Tove's hand resting on the axe across her knees, Thilda leaning in.

"Then that's where we're going," Tiata said.

Nobody argued.
