# 3 — The Wrong Wind

Taya had been alone for three winters, which was not, by itself, unusual. What was unusual was that she intended to stay that way.

She had been born into the confederation of the Roxolani, one of the great Sarmatian peoples, who had ruled this region for longer than anyone could recall, horse and bow and a discipline that had challenged Rome — but a generation of being ground down changed a people, slowly at first and then all at once. What was left of her clan by the time she came of age wasn't a nation. It was a handful of families arguing over which direction to run, and a few old men who wanted to make peace with an empire that had no intention of keeping any peace it made.

She had tried, for a while, to be what a daughter of that line was supposed to be. She had ridden with her clan's last riders, taken her share of Roman scouts and Roman grain, watched the elders trade away pieces of their land for promises that never held. And when the clan finally split — half toward a peace that would cost them everything, half toward fight — she had simply ridden away.

A woman alone on the steppe was not expected to survive, by most estimations, but Taya had made a study, in the years since, of challenging expectations.

She tracked Roman patrols now the way other women kept house — not for any clan, but because it was work she understood and Rome kept supplying more of it. She travelled everywhere on horseback, the way Roxolani had been doing it for centuries, her mare as much a part of how she read the ground as her eyes. This patrol, six men and a junior officer, she'd been shadowing for three days, learning their rhythm, waiting for the ground to offer her something worth the risk.

That was when she found the other three.

They were tracking the same patrol. Badly.

She watched them for the better part of a day before she made up her mind about them. Three women, moving like fighters, not like hunters, standing where a gust of wind would give them away to anyone with a reasonable sense of smell. One carried herself like a blade in motion. One moved like she was already three steps into a plan she hadn't shared. The third had said nothing all day, and somehow told the most.

She came down off the ridge, leading the mare  and didn't bother with a greeting.

"You're standing in the wrong wind," she said. "They'll smell you before they see you."

Three heads turned as one, but none of them flinched, which told her something too — these weren't women who startled, they were women who assessed, and she watched all three of them do exactly that in the space of a breath.

"You've been tracking us," the sharp-eyed one said.

"I've been tracking them," Taya said, with a tilt of her head toward the distant patrol. "You've been getting in my way doing it badly. Figured I'd fix that before it got one of you killed."

She didn't explain further — she'd watched them, decided they were worth the trouble of correcting, and that judgment was enough. Whatever came next between the four of them, it would be built on that: a hunter's plain accounting of what something was worth.

It would turn out, in the years after, to be exactly how she read every person she ever came close to. She saw what they were before they knew she was watching.
