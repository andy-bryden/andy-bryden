# 4 — Remembering

Weeks on the steppe had taught four women to move as a unit. Taya still scouted alone, still vanished for hours at a stretch and came back with information about the richest pickings, but the other three had also learned to read her silences almost as well as they read each other.

They needed supplies. Winter was close and four women moving through open country couldn't easily carry what they'd need to outlast it. Taya had made that plain enough without saying it outright: fewer arrows spent, more grain taken.

This time she'd spotted a small supplies convoy, consisting of 5 soldiers and one young officer, accompanying 3 waggons of supplies.

Taya opened the attack from a distance, unseen. An arrow into the throat of the officer was all it took to throw them all into confusion. The column broke formation instantly. Thana was was already moving before the officer had slidden from his horse, xiphos flying. Tacita moved behind her, silencing the struggling.

That left Toria the wagons, and the four men still standing between her and them.

The falcata came up and she was already past the first man, already turning into the second, and somewhere in the space between them, something in the depth of the fight — the dust, the shouting in a language that wasn't familiar, the particular quality of fear on a soldier's face — took her mind, for only a brief flash, back in time to a hillside and smoke that didn't belong there. Her mother's voice, cut off mid-word.

She had been small enough, then, that she couldn't understand what the men who came through her village were doing. Rome did not explain the putting down trouble in Iberia to the daughters of the people who'd caused it; Rome simply took what was useful and left the rest behinand To the Romans, Toria had been useful. Young, quick, and worth more alive than dead.

She didn't remember the ship. She remembered the smoke, and her mother's voice, and then nothing at all until years later, in a different country, when a wooden sword put into her hands by a man who called it training.

The fourth soldier's blade caught her across the forearm — not deep, but enough to snap her back to the present.

Taya found her a moment later, blood on her hands and a brief, lost look in her eye. She recognized immediately the particular stillness that came over a person reliving a flash of the past.

"You're bleeding," Taya said.

"It's nothing," Toria said, which was true of the wound.

They took what the wagons carried — grain, salted meat, a cache of coin. They took the officer's horse as well, the only mount worth the trouble among the column's tired draft animals. Winter would be survivable now, and the ground ahead of them would be shorter under hoof than it had been under four sets of tired feet.

Nobody asked Toria what she'd seen in that half-second she'd gone somewhere else. Tacita, who understood better than any of them what it cost to carry something silently, rode with her on the way back to camp — just near enough to convey an understanding.
