# Chapter Thirty-Three — Nobody There

Six days, and no patrol had crossed the western ground.

Taya reported it each evening, back from whatever ridge had held her that day. North, twice. East, three times, once close enough to hear the horses. South, once, a foraging party more than a patrol, gone before it had covered any real distance. West, nothing. Not a boot print laid fresh over the old ones. Not a rider sent to test what had swallowed a centurion.

"They've stopped believing that ground is theirs," Tiata said, the third evening, and nobody argued it.

They made sure the fort didn't forget why.

An arrow went into the gatepost on the fourth night, close enough to the watchman's shoulder to take the color out of his face, gone before anyone could name a direction to answer it in. On the fifth, a shape stood at the treeline in full daylight long enough for three separate men on the wall to swear to it, and was gone by the time any of them had found the nerve to point.

By then Marcellus had stopped watching much of anything.

*

The first day, he'd flinched at every shadow that crossed near the birch, tracked every pair of boots that passed within reach, tested the give in the tether at his ankles when he thought no one was looking.

By the fourth, he'd stopped testing it. It held. It had always held.

He ate what was left in front of him — bread, a strip of dried meat, whatever the fire had produced that night — without looking up to see whose hand had set it down. He drank when the waterskin appeared near his knee and didn't wonder anymore who'd carried it. His wrists had been unbound since the second day. He'd stopped raising them to check.

Toria passed within a foot of him twice a day, on her way to the horses, and he'd stopped turning his head to follow her.

Thana had stopped needling him somewhere around the fifth night, not out of any mercy, but because a man who no longer flinched wasn't worth the breath it cost to bait him.

He answered Tiata once, on the sixth morning, when she crouched in front of him and asked, flatly, whether he was still capable of hearing her. He said something. None of them could afterward agree on what it had been, only that it hadn't been a sentence a Roman officer would have recognized as his own.

He had stopped being watched, in the sense that mattered to a man. He had simply become a fixture of the camp — tethered, fed, and otherwise unremarked.

*

Taya caught the muster leaving the fort before it had cleared the gate properly, and was back at camp with the word before the column had covered a third of the ground between.

"They're coming," she said. "Full strength again, and the Praefectus riding at the head of it this time. Not a patrol. A rescue."

No one scrambled. There was nothing to scramble for — the horses stayed tethered exactly where they stood, saddled and waiting, because a camp stripped down to nothing said the same thing a fled one would. This one needed to say the opposite: that whoever lived here hadn't gone anywhere.

They went to ground at the treeline in good order and watched the column close the distance. Tacita stayed last, at the birch, and didn't move until the column was near enough that a man at its front could have named the color of the leaves behind her.

She cut the tether at Marcellus's ankles with one stroke, unhurried even then, and was gone into the foliage before the sound of it had finished reaching him.

*

Draco had argued against the Praefectus riding out himself, the night before, and lost. Rufinus hadn't bothered arguing at all, only asked how many men he wanted at his own back, and set about finding that many.

They went in with more caution than the strike that had cost them a century — smaller numbers, slower pace, scouts thrown well ahead and well wide, every man of them watching the trees without needing to be told why. None of them expected an easy day. Many of them, by the look on their faces, expected not to come back to the fort alive.

They found the camp by midafternoon, close by the ford on the fort's blind side — a fire still smoking where it had been kicked over rather than banked, bedding laid out around it in a rough circle, tack and gear stacked with the particular order of people who'd meant, until a few minutes before, to come back to it. Horses stood tethered at the treeline's edge, saddled, waiting on riders who plainly hadn't gone far.

A shape lay near the birch, half-curled on the ground rather than sitting against it. The grey wool dress Tove had thrown at his feet days before hung off one shoulder now. No rope on the wrists. No rope at the ankles. Nothing was holding him there at all.

The Praefectus was first to the clearing, ahead of his own scouts, and first to the shape on the ground. He went down on both knees beside it and turned the face up out of the dirt, and knew him before he'd finished the motion — the same man whose forearm he'd gripped at his own gate, the same Marcellus he'd grown up alongside.

Marcellus's eyes found the Praefectus's and held there a moment, flat and still — no relief in them, no fear, no recognition of the man who'd raised half a fort to come and find him. He said nothing. He made no sound at all when the Praefectus said his name, once, then again, closer.

No one had stopped him from being found.

Draco bent down and took the Praefectus by the arm. "Come, sir."

He rose quietly, and the men behind him stooped and lifted Marcellus up between them and carried him out of the clearing.

*

Nothing came at them.

That was the part that undid them more than the fire pit or the bedding or the man who no longer answered to his own name.

Every man in that clearing had spent the walk in braced for an arrow out of the trees, a dart from somewhere unseen, a blade rising out of ground that had already proven it could hide exactly that. They had come ready to fight for him, to buy him back at whatever price was asked, to beg if begging was what it took. None of it had been required. The camp had simply opened itself and let them walk in and take what they'd come for.

The gear was real. The horses were real, tethered and saddled as if their riders had only just stepped away from them. The fire was still catching air where it had been kicked apart, not yet gone to embers. Whoever had lived here hadn't gone far, and hadn't gone long ago. And not one soul stood anywhere in sight to account for any of it.

They didn't linger to search the treeline. They left the way they'd come, faster than caution strictly allowed, every man among them certain — without one shred of anything to prove it — that eight pairs of eyes were counting them out of that clearing the same way they'd been counted in.

Nothing made a sound. Nothing moved. Nothing.
