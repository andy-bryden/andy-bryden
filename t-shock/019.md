# 19 — Released

They crossed the ford at first light, single file, five horses and two riding double, and said nothing until the fort's shape rose clear through the trees ahead of them.

Taya split them to their work without needing to be asked twice. She and Thana went to ground on the west side, within sight of the supply road and the blind stretch of wall behind it — the side Taya had already read as open, and the side that mattered most to confirm before anything else. Tacita and Toria worked around to the east, where the wall stood higher and better watched, reading what a real approach from that direction would cost, in case it was ever needed. Tove and Thilda ranged the ground behind the western treeline, marking cover, water, and a line the horses could hold without giving anyone away.

What the west side gave up matched everything Taya had reported. Wagons rolled through the gate on that side on a loose, trusting schedule, waved through without a challenge called down from the wall. No patrol crossed that ground all morning. Every patrol that did go out swung east and south instead, walking the line the fort had always watched, the line the frontier had trained every man inside it to think of as the danger.

"They still don't look this way," Taya said, low, watching a wagon roll through unchallenged. "Not once, all morning."

Tove found ground worth using not far back from the road, on the blind side — a hollow, water close, cover thick on three sides. Thilda walked its edges herself before she'd trust it.

"There's a patrol runs somewhere near this ground," she said, once she'd finished. "Small. Four men, maybe five. Not part of the eastern loop — a stray from it, most likely. If we hold here quiet, we take it clean tomorrow, well clear of where we're sitting."

Tiata turned that over, the falx across her knees. "Tomorrow, then. Taya first light, eyes on their—"

She didn't finish it.

Taya was already on her feet, one hand raised flat for quiet, head turned toward the trees south of the hollow. A moment later the others heard what she'd caught first — voices, unhurried, far closer than anything had a right to be.

"That's them," she said, low. "The same patrol. Come further off their line than they should have, and headed straight at us." She looked to Thilda. "This was meant to be clear ground."

"It should have been," Thilda said, already moving. "They don't stray this far from the eastern loop. Not on anything I've read."

There was no time to settle whose read had failed. Tiata was up, falx in hand.

"Positions," she said. "The ones we marked for tomorrow. We use them now."

They moved fast and silent, each of them already knowing where Thilda's ground had placed her, where Tove's strength would matter, where Taya's bow would open it. The patrol walked into it exactly as Taya had read them — unhurried, unaware — and the ambush took four of them down within a dozen heartbeats of the first arrow.

The fifth was young, younger than the armor suggested he had any business wearing, and when Toria's blade came level with his throat he didn't raise his own to meet it. He dropped it instead, both hands open, shaking hard enough that it carried up into his shoulders.

"Hold," Tiata said, before Toria's arm had finished its swing.

Toria held. It cost her something — that much was plain on her face — but she held.

Tiata studied the boy a long moment, the same flat, weighing look she gave everything.

"How long have you served here?" she asked, in workable Latin.

"Six months," he managed. "Less."

"Then you're young enough to still remember what fear feels like, before it wears down into habit," Tiata said. "That's useful to me today." She nodded to Toria, who lowered the blade without lowering her guard. "You're going to walk back to that fort. Not run — walk, so you have time to remember all of it. Tell them four of your patrol are dead and you're the one left to say so. Tell them seven women did it, in less time than it takes to explain, on the side of that fort nobody thought needed watching."

The boy said nothing. He didn't look capable of it.

"Tell them exactly that," Tiata said. "Don't soften it for your own sake, and don't guess at anything you didn't see. The truth, plain, is worth more to me than anything you could invent to shrink it."

They stripped him of blade and shield, anything that might let him claim afterward he'd fought rather than survived, and let him go. He didn't look back once.

Taya watched him go until the trees took him, then turned to the others.

"That wasn't silence," she said. "That's the opposite of the watch-station."

"It was meant to be," Tiata said. "A forgotten station gets erased quietly, and nobody above it ever has to explain the gap. A garrison this size, with men left who'll ask questions when four of their own don't come back — that fort needs a story it can't control, told by someone who was there and can't be accused of exaggerating it." She looked toward the fort, still standing exactly as it had that morning, not yet knowing what it had lost. "Let them wonder what's coming next. Wondering costs more than four men ever could."

They broke the ambush site clean and moved deeper into cover before the fort could send anyone out looking. Behind them, a boy who would carry the whole of it walked alone toward a gate that would soon be a great deal less certain of what stood outside it.
