1 — The Lion
↑ Back to top

Byzantium, 232 AD.

Sand kicked up under bronze-shod feet as Thana and Toria closed the distance at a dead sprint, blades already rising, the roar of the crowd folding into a single wall of noise meant to drown out whichever of them screamed first.

Then a second roar cut under it — deeper, guttural, rolling in off the far side of the arena — and both women broke stride at once, blades still up, eyes cutting toward the sound instead of each other.

A lion came through the gate at a dead run, mane low, claws tearing sand, and every eye in the stands went with it.

The owner had grown tired of Thana's arrogance. Tired of the smirk she wore into every bout, the one that said she already knew how the other woman would die before the first blow landed. Eleven fights, eleven corpses that weren't hers, and still that same insufferable calm — as if the arena were beneath her rather than the whole of her world.

So he paired her against Toria.

He had never matched two of his own against each other before. It wasn't done, not when both women drew a crowd and both crowds paid. But the sun-bronzed Iberian was fast in a way Thana wasn't, vicious in a way the Greek's cold precision had never had to answer for, and he told himself the novelty alone would cover the loss. Two gladiatrices, one blade each, armor same as any other bout. Let the smirk answer that.

What he hadn't accounted for was the third woman.

Tacita was difficult to manage in ways that had nothing to do with defiance. She never argued, never resisted, never gave the guards a reason to raise a hand — which was, in its way, worse, because there was nothing to correct. She moved through the compound the way water moves through a cracked jar: quietly, and always somewhere she wasn't supposed to be. Three years without her voice, and in that time she had learned to move without sound in bronze armor that should have announced her with every step. No one ever worked out how. No one ever caught her at it either, which amounted to the same thing.

While Thana and Toria were led into the tunnel that opened onto the sand, Tacita was already gone — not fled, not hiding, simply elsewhere, drifting into the low stone corridor where the compound kept its beasts.

She had a plan. It was not a subtle one.

Thana and Toria didn't need to speak to know what to do. The falcata came up first, catching the lion's shoulder as it closed; the Greek was already moving to flank, cutting off the angle before the beast could turn. It was over in under a minute — messy, efficient, the kind of kill that looks effortless only because both women had killed things far more dangerous than a starved arena cat. The crowd didn't see a jailbreak. They saw a miracle. They saw two gladiatrices turn a death-match into a shared triumph, and they roared for it, and Thana and Toria walked out of that arena arm in arm to the kind of adulation neither had ever earned separately.

In the tunnels beneath the arena, the animal handlers were already unraveling. A lion loose mid-bout wasn't bad luck — it was a failure in a system that wasn't supposed to fail, and they knew exactly whose neck that failure would land on. Word went out fast, hands short-staffed and voices raised, calling down every man they could pull from elsewhere in the compound to help account for what had gone wrong. Guards who should have been at their posts were suddenly needed somewhere else entirely, and nobody thought to ask who'd engineered that.

When Thana and Toria came back in off the sand, still catching their breath, Tacita was waiting for them in the passage — and simply beckoned.

Neither of them asked where, or why, or how she'd known the moment to move. They already understood there'd be no answer if they did. They knew, too, without needing it said, whose silent hand had rearranged the whole board while the crowd was busy cheering a miracle.

They followed.

2 — Out of Byzantium
↑ Back to top

The tunnels beneath the arena were built for animals and the men who managed them, low stone and narrow turns, and in the confusion nobody had thought to watch the three women who didn't belong there.

Thana and Toria still carried what they'd fought with. No one had thought to disarm them — why would they, in the scramble to explain a loose lion, to account for a failure that would cost someone his hide — and so a falcata and whatever blade Thana had carried onto the sand came with them, unremarked, into the dark.

Tacita already had hers. Whatever she'd taken in the beast cages, on her way to the lion's gate, she'd taken quietly and without asking, the way she took everything. She didn't explain it. She never did.

She led them without hesitating, through turns that suggested she'd walked them before — maybe that same afternoon, maybe over weeks nobody had noticed. Handlers' voices carried from side passages, sharp with panic, all of it aimed at each other and none of it aimed at three gladiatrices who should have been back in a cell by now. Tacita moved through the noise like it belonged to someone else's building.

They came out through a low service door into the cool dark beyond the compound walls, the city's noise a different register out here — market close, temple bells, someone singing badly two streets over.

They had perhaps a hundred paces before the horn went up behind them.

A headcount, a door found unbarred, someone finally asking the right question at the wrong time — it didn't matter which. What mattered was the shout that followed it, and the second shout answering the first, and the sound of hobnailed sandals breaking into a run on stone.

"Move," Thana said, and they moved.

Byzantium at that hour was a maze of narrow lanes and blind corners, walls close enough on both sides to touch, and Tacita took it like she'd already memorized every wrong turn in it. She cut left where the street looked like it dead-ended, and didn't, ducked under a hanging line of drying fish, vaulted a low wall into a courtyard that spat them out three streets further than any pursuer would guess.

It wasn't enough. Byzantium's watch knew their own city too.

Two men came out of a side alley ahead of them, blades already drawn, shouting for them to halt — and Toria didn't slow down to consider it. The falcata came up and around in the same motion as her stride, catching the first man across the throat before he'd finished the word, and she was past him and into the second before he'd registered what had happened to his partner. He didn't get his blade up in time. Neither of them would be standing watch again, that night or any other.

Behind them, a third pursuer rounded the corner at a dead run and met Thana instead. She didn't have Toria's speed, but she'd never needed it — she stepped inside his reach, took the strike on her forearm instead of her chest, and put her blade in under his ribs with the same cold economy she brought to every bout she'd ever fought clean. He went down without a sound. She was already moving again before he finished falling.

More voices behind them now, closer than before, torchlight starting to bounce off the walls at the mouth of the lane. Tacita caught Toria's sleeve and hauled her sideways through a gap between two buildings barely wide enough for a shoulder, and Thana followed without asking why — there wasn't time to ask why, there was only time to run and trust that the woman who'd gotten a lion loose in a guarded arena knew how to get three women out of a guarded city.

They lost the torches in the tanners' quarter, where the smell was bad enough that even a determined watch didn't want to linger, cutting through standing water and worse until the shouting behind them thinned into confusion and then, finally, into nothing they could hear at all.

By the time Byzantium's outer wall rose up ahead of them, dark against a darker sky, the pursuit had scattered into search parties combing the wrong streets, calling to each other across distances that told all three women exactly how lost their hunters already were.

They didn't go through a gate. Tacita found a stretch of wall where the stonework had crumbled enough for a foothold, and they went over it instead, dropping into the scrub beyond breathing hard, blood on two blades that wasn't theirs.

They didn't put distance between themselves and the wall — not that first night. Distance was what a frightened person bought, and none of them mistook fear for sense. They stayed close enough to watch the gates, close enough to hear which direction the horns went out in, and used the dark to learn the ground instead of running blind across it. They slept well that night, knowing their pursuers would be expecting them to be running, not resting.

At first light, a search party came out from the city gate — four men, moving the way men move when they expect to find nothing.

Tacita spotted them before they came anywhere near spotting her. She watched them long enough to learn their spacing, then went back for the other two.

What happened after wasn't a fight so much as a correction. Toria and Thana had killed men in the dark of a city, quick and necessary; this was neither quick nor, strictly, necessary — it was a message, delivered in the same coin Byzantium had sent out to collect them. Four men went into that stretch of scrub looking for three fugitives. None of them walked back out to report what they'd found.

By the time anyone in Byzantium worked out that a search party had gone missing rather than simply failed, the three of them were already gone north, leaving no one behind who could say which way.

3 — The Wrong Wind
↑ Back to top

Taya had been alone for three winters, which was not, by itself, unusual. What was unusual was that she meant to stay that way.

She had been born into a confederation that no longer behaved like one. The Roxolani, one of the great Sarmatian peoples, had ruled this grass for longer than anyone could put a number to, horse and bow and a discipline that had made Rome itself pay tribute rather than fight — but a generation of being ground down changed a people the way water changed stone, slowly and then all at once. What was left of her clan by the time she came of age wasn't a nation. It was a handful of families arguing over which direction to run, and old men who wanted to make peace with an empire that had no intention of keeping any peace it made.

She had tried, for a while, to be what a daughter of that line was supposed to be. She had ridden with her clan's last riders, taken her share of Roman scouts and Roman grain, watched the elders trade away pieces of themselves for promises that never held. And when the clan finally split — half toward a peace that would cost them everything anyway, half toward a fight none of them had the numbers left to win — she had simply ridden away from both halves, because neither was worth what they were asking her to become.

A woman alone on the steppe was supposed to die quickly. Taya had made a study, in the years since, of not doing what she was supposed to do.

She tracked Roman patrols now the way other women kept house — not for any clan, not for any cause anyone living could name, but because it was work she understood and Rome kept supplying more of it. She did it from horseback, the way every Roxolani did everything worth doing, her mare as much a part of how she read the ground as her own eyes were. This patrol, six men and a junior officer too pleased with himself, she'd been shadowing for three days, learning their rhythm, waiting for the ground to offer her something worth the risk.

That was when she found the other three.

They were tracking the same patrol. Badly.

She watched them for the better part of a day before she made up her mind about them, because that was how she did everything now — from distance, from height, from wherever the wind wouldn't carry her scent to whatever she was studying. Three women, moving like fighters and not like hunters, standing where the air would give them away to anyone with a nose worth trusting. One carried herself like a blade already in motion. One moved like she was already three steps into a plan the other two hadn't been told yet. The third said nothing at all, the whole day, and somehow said the most.

Taya had spent three winters deciding that nothing on this steppe was worth attaching herself to. She watched these three for one day and started to wonder if she'd been wrong.

She came down off the ridge only once she'd decided — not before, because deciding first was the only way she'd survived this long — leading the mare rather than riding her in, so as not to announce herself with hoofbeats before she'd chosen her moment. She didn't announce herself with a greeting either. Greetings were for people who hadn't already made up their minds.

"You're standing in the wrong wind," she said. "They'll smell you before they see you."

Three heads turned as one. None of them reached for a blade, which told her something too — these weren't women who startled, they were women who assessed, and she watched all three of them do exactly that in the space of a breath, weighing her the same way she'd already weighed them.

Nobody asked how long she'd been there. They already knew the answer wouldn't help them.

"You've been tracking us," the sharp-eyed one said — not a question.

"I've been tracking them," Taya said, with a tilt of her head toward the distant patrol. "You've been getting in my way doing it badly. Figured I'd fix that before it got one of you killed."

She didn't explain further. She didn't join because anyone asked her to, and she made sure they understood that plainly enough — she'd watched them, decided they were worth the trouble of correcting, and that judgment was the whole of her invitation. Whatever came next between the four of them, it would be built on that: not need, not loyalty owed in advance, but a hunter's plain accounting of what something was worth before she spent any part of herself on it.

It would turn out, in the years after, to be exactly how she read every person she ever let close. She saw what they were before they knew she was looking.

4 — Remembering
↑ Back to top

Weeks on the steppe had taught four women to move like one, mostly. Taya still scouted alone, still vanished for hours at a stretch and came back with information instead of company, but by the time she found this patrol — eight men, a supply train, slower and fatter than the last one — the other three had learned to read her silences almost as well as they read each other.

They needed the supplies more than they needed the kill. Winter was close enough to smell, and four women moving fast through open country couldn't carry what they'd need to outlast it. Taya had made that plain enough without saying it outright: fewer arrows spent, more grain taken, and nobody dies who doesn't have to.

It didn't go that cleanly. It rarely did.

Taya opened it the way she opened everything — from a distance, unseen, an arrow into the throat of the man walking rearguard before anyone else in the column understood the day had turned. The column broke formation exactly the way undisciplined men break formation: badly, all at once, shouting orders that contradicted each other. Thana was already moving before the echo of the first arrow died, sliding into the gap the panic had opened, finding the seams the way she always did. Tacita took the officer alive and silent, the way she took everything, walking him out of the chaos before he'd fully understood he was a prisoner.

That left Toria the wagons, and the four men still standing between her and them.

She didn't remember deciding to move. She rarely did, in the middle of a fight — there was before, and there was after, and the part in between happened to her hands more than it happened to her thinking. The falcata came up and she was already past the first man, already turning into the second, and somewhere in the space between one cut and the next, something in the shape of the fight — the dust, the shouting in a language that wasn't quite Latin and wasn't quite anything else, the particular quality of fear on a soldier's face when he realizes the day has gone wrong — dropped her, for one half of one heartbeat, somewhere she hadn't been in a very long time.

A hillside. Smoke that didn't belong there. Her mother's voice, cut off mid-word.

She had been small enough, then, that the men who came through her village hadn't bothered explaining themselves to a child. There had been no need to. Rome did not explain the aftermath of putting down trouble in Iberia to the daughters of the people who'd caused it; Rome simply took what was useful and left the rest behind. Toria had been useful. Young, and quick even then, and worth more standing than dead, which was the only kind of mercy an empire like that one ever handed out.

She didn't remember the ship. She remembered the smoke, and her mother's voice, and then nothing at all until years later, a different country, a wooden sword put into her hands by a man who called it training.

The fourth soldier's blade caught her across the forearm — not deep, but enough. Enough to snap her back into the wagons, the dust, the falcata still warm in her grip. She finished him without thinking about it twice, the way she'd finished the others, and stood alone among the dead with her breath loud in her own ears.

Thana found her like that a moment later, blood on her hands that wasn't hers either. She didn't ask what had happened. She'd fought beside Toria long enough to recognize the particular stillness that came over her sometimes, after — not shock, exactly, and not grief either, just a woman standing very still in the wreckage of something that had happened to her twice.

"You're bleeding," Thana said, which was easier than anything else she might have said instead.

"It's nothing," Toria said, which was true of the wound and not of anything else, and neither of them pushed past that.

They took what the wagons carried — grain, salted meat, a cache of coin nobody bothered to explain to the officer Tacita marched south a day later, disarmed and pointed toward whatever remained of his career. They took the officer's horse as well, the only mount worth the trouble among the column's tired draft animals, and Taya spent longer choosing it than she'd spent on anything else that day, running a hand down its foreleg and into its mouth before she pronounced it sound enough to keep. Winter would be survivable now, and the ground ahead of them would be shorter under hoof than it had been under four sets of tired feet. That had been the point, twice over.

Nobody asked Toria what she'd seen in that half-second she'd gone somewhere else. Tacita, who understood better than any of them what it cost to carry something silently, simply rode close beside her on the way back to camp — near enough to matter, not so near as to ask.

Some things, on the steppe, were easier carried than spoken. Toria had been doing both for a very long time.

5 — The Falx
↑ Back to top

Winter found them on the road into Dacia, a hundred and thirty years a Roman province and still, in places, not fully convinced of it.

Taya found the woman before any of them, the way she found everything — from a rise, downwind, unseen. She watched for longer than she usually bothered to, then came back to the others with her report instead of her opinion, because she'd learned by now that the others valued the difference.

"One woman. Local, not Roman — the falx makes that plain enough, nobody issues those. She's standing over a dead surveyor and she isn't hiding the body." A pause, while she turned the rest of it over. "She's not hiding herself either. She wants to be found. By the right people."

"And are we the right people?" Thana asked, already amused by the shape of the question before she'd finished asking it.

"I think," Taya said, "she already knows the answer to that better than we do."

They found her exactly where Taya said she'd be, exactly as unbothered by their arrival as Taya had promised. The falx was still in her hand, not raised, not lowered — simply held, the way another woman might hold a walking stick, as if the possibility of violence were no more remarkable to her than the weather.

Toria spoke first, because Toria generally did. "You're a long way from anywhere that wants Romans dead badly enough to leave one lying in the open."

"He's not lying in the open for want of caring," the woman said. "He's lying in the open because I want the next one to see him."

It was a bold enough answer that Toria's mouth twitched toward something sharper — an edge she rarely bothered to leash when a stranger tested her — and it might have gone further than words if Tacita hadn't simply looked at her. Not a warning, exactly. Just a look, brief and level, the kind that had stopped worse impulses in worse company than this. Toria exhaled through her nose and let it go.

"I'm Toria," she said instead, and if her voice was a shade cooler than it had been a moment ago, the stranger didn't seem to notice, or didn't care to. "We're headed north. You're welcome to ride with us, if you've a mind to."

"I have a camp a half hour from here," the woman said, with a glance that took in the two tired horses and the four women doubled up across them. "You'll want the fire more than you want the riding." She turned toward a picket line half-hidden in the brush, where a horse of her own stood already saddled, and swung up onto it without waiting to see if they'd follow — which, Taya noted, privately, filing it away, was its own kind of answer.

They followed.

Her name was Tiata, and her camp was better kept than four fugitives expected from one woman alone — dry wood stacked ahead of need, water close, the fire built low and smokeless in a hollow that wouldn't carry light past the treeline. She moved through her own space with the same flat certainty she'd shown standing over the body, and by the time the five of them were settled around the fire, nobody had yet had to ask her to explain herself. She simply occupied the space as though she'd always been part of filling it.

It was Thana who broke the quiet first, because Thana always did, and because something about Tiata's stillness seemed to invite exactly the kind of needling Thana couldn't resist offering.

"You kill Roman surveyors for sport, or is there a cause behind it somewhere?"

"There's a province behind it," Tiata said, without heat. "A hundred and thirty years of one. You'd know something about causes, I'd think, given how loudly you don't have one."

Thana's smile didn't move, but something behind it sharpened. "I have plenty of cause. I simply don't feel the need to announce it to strangers with strange swords."

"Falx," Tiata said.

"I know what it is."

"Then you'll know it's not for show either." Tiata turned the blade slightly where it lay across her knees, an idle gesture that somehow read as anything but idle. "You wear your skill the way some men wear rank — like it's owed to you rather than earned. I've known officers like that. Rome's full of them. They tend not to survive contact with people who actually had to earn what they carry."

The fire cracked. Nobody else spoke.

Thana's jaw tightened, just slightly — the first crack any of them had seen in that permanent, easy smirk since the arena. "You don't know anything about what I earned."

"Then tell me," Tiata said. Not a challenge. Worse than a challenge — genuine invitation, offered as though she already suspected the answer and simply wanted to see if Thana would give it honestly.

Thana looked at the fire for a long moment before she answered, and when she did, the smirk was entirely gone.

"I wasn't born to a cage," she said. "My family had standing — not wealth, not much of it, but standing, in a town on the coast that thought itself too small for pirates to bother with. We were wrong about that." She turned a piece of kindling over in her hands, not looking at any of them. "I was fourteen. They didn't take the town. They didn't need to. A dozen men came ashore at night, took what they could carry that breathed, and were gone before anyone thought to raise an alarm that would have done nothing anyway."

"The market at Delos, or somewhere smaller?" Tiata asked — not unkindly, simply precise, the way she seemed to ask everything.

"Smaller. It doesn't matter. What matters is the man who bought me didn't buy me for my arms or my back. He watched me for one day, the way he watched all the new stock, and he told his overseer I'd last longer than the fighters twice my size, because I wasn't going to make the mistake of fighting fair." Something almost like the old smirk flickered back, thin and bitter now instead of easy. "He was right. Eleven bouts. Eleven women who thought strength was the only thing that mattered in that sand, and eleven times I let them believe it right up until it killed them."

"And the arrogance?" Tiata asked.

"Isn't arrogance," Thana said. "It's arithmetic. I already know how most fights end before they start. I've simply never found it useful to pretend otherwise for anyone's comfort."

Tiata studied her for a moment longer, then nodded once, as if some private accounting had come out even. "Good," she said. "Arithmetic, I can use. Comfort, I have no patience for."

It wasn't warmth, exactly, whatever passed between them after that. But it was something neither of them had offered a stranger before, and the other three noticed it land — Taya filing it away the way she filed everything, Tacita watching without a word, Toria letting the last of her earlier temper go now that she understood there'd been nothing in the woman's bluntness aimed at any of them specifically, only at whatever in Thana still needed saying.

They stayed at that fire long after the wood should have burned down to nothing, and for the first time since Byzantium, the silence between them wasn't the comfortable kind born of not needing words. It was the kind that came after finally using some.

6 — An Appetite with Direction
↑ Back to top

Morning came grey and close to the ground, the fire down to embers, and Tiata was the first of them awake — though none of them had actually seen her sleep, which by now surprised no one.

She didn't ease into the day the way the others had learned to. She simply asked.

"Where are you headed?"

It landed differently than it might have from anyone else. Taya would have asked it to read them. Thana would have asked it to needle. Tiata asked it the way she asked everything — flatly, expecting an actual answer, and it was that expectation, more than the question itself, that seemed to catch Toria off guard.

She opened her mouth to say north, or away, the answer that had been true enough for months now to stop requiring thought. What came out instead surprised her as much as anyone.

"We don't entirely know," Toria admitted. "We started out running. That's simple enough to understand — Byzantium wasn't going to let three escaped gladiatrices become an inconvenience it lived with." She glanced at Thana, who picked it up without missing the handoff, the way they'd learned to do without discussing it.

"But running stops being the whole of it, eventually," Thana said. "There's only so far you can go before away isn't a direction anymore, just a habit. We've killed enough patrols by now, taken enough supply, put enough dead officers back on Roman desks as a problem someone has to explain — it's occurred to more than one of us that we're not just avoiding the empire anymore. We're already hurting it. Small cuts. Nothing it can't absorb."

"But cuts add up," Toria said, and there was something in her voice now that hadn't been there a moment ago — not the habitual quickness, something closer to conviction finding its own shape as she spoke it aloud for the first time. "If it were just cuts forever, fine. But there are garrisons out past where anyone bothers to look — frontier postings, undermanned, half-forgotten by whoever's supposed to be minding them. Hit the right one, hard enough, in the right place, and it isn't a supply raid anymore. It's a message that travels further than we do. Rome doesn't fear losing men. It fears looking like it's losing control of what it already holds."

"You mean to make the empire flinch," Tiata said. Not a question.

"I mean to make it bleed somewhere it can't hide the wound," Toria said. "Whether that's the same thing or not, I haven't decided yet."

Tiata was quiet for a while after that, turning the falx over where it lay across her knees, the same idle-looking gesture that never actually looked idle on her. When she finally spoke again, it wasn't to Toria at all.

"That's not a plan," she said. "That's an appetite with a direction. Which isn't nothing — appetite is more than most people manage. But it isn't a plan yet either."

Nobody argued with her. It didn't occur to any of them to.

"I'm not ready to move on this morning," Tiata went on, standing, banking the last of the fire with the flat of her boot without seeming to think about the motion at all. "There's a matter or two I haven't finished settling in this province, and I'd rather not leave it half done. But I find I don't mind the company while I finish it."

It wasn't an offer, exactly. It wasn't phrased as one. But all four of them understood it for what it was, and none of them mistook it for anything less than what it actually meant — that whatever this had been until now, a coincidence of four women who happened to move well together, was becoming something with a shape to it. Something that would need more than appetite and a direction to survive contact with an empire that could afford to lose entire legions and barely notice.

Nobody said any of that aloud. There was no need to. Taya caught Toria's eye across the dead fire, and Toria caught Thana's, and even Tacita, watching all of it with her usual unreadable stillness, allowed something in her expression to settle that hadn't been settled before.

They had been four women surviving by their own separate wits, tolerating each other's company because it happened, so far, to work. What stood in front of them now, banking a fire with the same unhurried certainty she did everything else, was the first among them who didn't survive by wits alone. She survived by design. And design was the one thing none of the rest of them, for all their skill, had ever quite managed to bring to what they were doing.

Whatever came next — and something would, they all understood that now, whether it was one more garrison or an empire's worth of them — it was no longer going to happen to them.

It was going to be planned.

7 — Old Ground
↑ Back to top

The matter Tiata hadn't finished turned out not to be a man at all, but a process.

"A survey team," she told them, as they broke camp. "Six men, an escort of four, working their way along the old boundary stones. They're not measuring the land for its own sake. They're measuring it so it can be given away — parceled out to men who've never set foot on it, veterans owed land for service, officials owed favors. Once the stones are moved and the record's made in Rome, it stops being anyone's but theirs. That's how a province disappears. Not all at once. Stone by stone, until one day the name on the map is the only Dacian thing left standing."

Nobody asked why that mattered enough to delay everything else. By now, none of them needed to.

They moved together — all five, for the first time since Tiata had joined them, rather than splitting off in pairs the way old habit still sometimes pulled them. It was Tiata who called for it, and it was Tiata, scouting the ground an hour ahead of the survey party's expected line, who turned to Taya rather than to any of the others when the terrain finally opened up in front of them.

"You read ground better than anyone I've fought beside," Tiata said. "Where would you put us?"

It wasn't flattery. Tiata didn't deal in flattery, any more than she dealt in comfort, and Taya understood the question for exactly what it was — not a courtesy extended to the newest useful hand, but a genuine ceding of judgment on the one thing Taya had always done better than anyone watching.

She studied the tree line, the fall of the ground, the place where the old boundary stones sat half-swallowed by grass that didn't care whose empire it grew under.

"There," Taya said, pointing to a shallow rise choked with scrub, upwind of the stones and low enough to stay unseen until it mattered. "I open from here — they won't hear it coming, and the escort will break toward the noise instead of toward the surveyors, which buys you the six that actually matter. Thana and I can pin the escort from two angles once they scatter. Tacita takes whoever's left standing when it's done. You and Toria go straight for the stones and the men measuring them — that's the part that actually needs killing quietly, before anyone thinks to run with the record they've already made."

Tiata looked at the ground Taya had marked out for a long moment, turning the plan over the way she turned everything over, and nodded once. "That'll do."

It did.

The escort broke exactly as Taya had said it would, chasing sound instead of sense, and by the time any of them understood which direction the real threat had come from, three were already down and the fourth was running in a direction that wouldn't save him. Thana finished what Taya started with the same cold economy she brought to everything, and Tacita moved through what remained of the confusion like she always did — quiet, thorough, leaving nothing behind that could carry a warning south.

That left the stones.

Toria and Tiata came on the surveyors from the tree line together, and the falx and the falcata did their work without needing to discuss which of them took which man. It was fast. It was, by the standards of everything they'd done since Byzantium, almost clean.

Almost.

One of the surveyors — older, gray at the temple, a man who'd clearly done this work in more provinces than one — didn't run and didn't fight either. He simply stood over the half-driven stake he'd been setting, hands empty, and said something in Latin too fast for most of them to catch before Toria's blade ended it.

She stood over him afterward longer than the moment required. Long enough that Tiata, wiping the falx clean on a fold of the dead man's cloak, noticed.

"You understood him," Tiata said. Not a question.

"Enough of it." Toria didn't look up. "He said it wasn't personal. That he was only doing the measuring — someone else decides where the lines go, he just walks them." Her jaw worked once, twice, before the rest came. "Someone said something very like that to me once. Different words. Same shape."

Tiata didn't press. She simply waited, the way she'd waited with Thana at the fire, offering the silence as its own kind of invitation.

"I was small when Rome came through my village," Toria said, at last, to the stake still half in the ground rather than to Tiata directly. "Small enough that nobody explained anything to me. There'd been trouble — I never learned what kind, or whether it was even trouble my own people had caused, or just trouble that happened near enough to us that it didn't matter. Rome doesn't sort that out before it burns something down. It just puts down what's in front of it and calls the job finished." She finally looked up. "I remember smoke. I remember my mother's voice stopping in the middle of a word. I don't remember the ship, or the market, or most of the first year after. Just that I was young, and quick, and worth more walking than dead, and that was the whole of the mercy anyone showed me."

"And the surveyor," Tiata said quietly.

"Was measuring the same kind of nothing that got done to me," Toria said. "Somebody's already decided where this land goes. He's just walking the lines so Rome doesn't have to admit that's what happened. I know that walk. I've been the thing at the end of it."

Tiata was quiet for a moment, then reached down and pulled the half-driven stake free of the ground herself, tossing it into the brush where no one would ever set it plumb again.

"Then let's make sure this stone stays exactly where it was," she said. "Whatever else changes."

It wasn't comfort, not the way Tacita's silences offered comfort, or the way Thana's bluntness sometimes did despite itself. But Toria understood it as its own kind of answer all the same — the first time since her mother's voice had stopped mid-word that anyone had looked at what Rome had done to her and offered, instead of pity, something that looked almost like restitution.

They buried nothing. They left the bodies for whoever came looking, the same message Toria's blade had already started writing months ago in a different province, under a different sky.

Then they moved on, together, toward whatever ground Taya would read for them next.

8 — The Cost of Silence
↑ Back to top

They made camp that night within sight of the boundary stone Tiata had thrown back into the brush, close enough that none of them had needed to discuss why. It felt, without anyone saying so, like a place worth finishing a day at rather than merely stopping in.

Tacita built the fire, as she often did — quickly, without wasted motion, the kind of competence that had never once needed a word attached to it. She had been the quietest of them since Byzantium, quieter even than the silence itself sometimes required, and by now the others had stopped noticing it as absence. It was simply how she took up space.

Tiata noticed it differently. Tiata noticed everything differently.

"You haven't spoken since I joined you," she said, once the fire had caught and the others had settled into the loose, unhurried quiet that followed a day like the one behind them. It wasn't an accusation. It landed the same way everything from Tiata landed — flat, exact, entirely without softness, and somehow more disarming for it than sympathy would have been.

Tacita didn't look up from the fire.

"Either you don't speak because you can't," Tiata went on, "or because you've decided against it. I've met both kinds. You're not the first kind."

Still nothing. But something in the set of Tacita's shoulders had changed, minutely, the way still water changes when something moves beneath it.

Thana shifted, half-ready to step in the way she sometimes did when a silence ran too long for her comfort, but Tiata lifted one hand, barely, and Thana settled again. This wasn't hers to interrupt.

"I don't ask to be cruel," Tiata said. "I ask because I've noticed you're the one among all of us who never once has to be corrected. Taya reads people. Thana reads fights. Toria acts before she thinks and is usually right anyway. But you—" she paused, choosing the words with the same care she chose everything, "—you never do anything that needs undoing. That's not nature. That's discipline. And discipline like that is built, not born, whatever quiet you started with."

The fire cracked. Nobody else breathed too loudly.

"I was already quiet," Tacita said.

The words came out low, and rough with disuse, like a door that hadn't been opened in years finally giving on its hinges. It was the first full sentence any of them had heard from her since the night they'd followed her out of Byzantium, and the shock of it moved around the fire like a current — Taya's eyes flicking up sharp, Toria going very still, Thana, for once, saying nothing at all.

Tiata simply nodded, once, as if she'd expected exactly that answer and no other. "I imagine you were. But quiet isn't silence. Not this kind." She let that sit a moment before she asked the rest of it, gently for her, which was still not gentle at all. "What made the difference?"

Tacita was quiet again for a long while. Long enough that the others thought, perhaps, that was all there would be — one sentence, hard-won, and nothing further owed to anyone. But she was still looking at the fire, not at any of them, when she finally spoke again, each word coming slower than the last, as though she were paying some private cost for every one of them.

"My father," she said. "Owed a debt he could not pay honestly." A pause, her jaw working once before the rest came. "He found a way to pay it that did not cost him anything he valued."

"Rome bought what he sold," she said, at last, flat and final, as if she were closing a door she had only opened wide enough to prove it could still move. "That is all there is."

It plainly wasn't all there was. But Tiata, who had pushed exactly as far as the question required and not one word further, simply nodded again and let it stand.

"That's enough," she said. Not dismissive. Something closer to respect.

Tacita went back to the fire, feeding it a piece of wood that didn't need feeding yet, and said nothing more that night. But something had shifted at that fire all the same — not healed, none of them were foolish enough to mistake it for healed, but witnessed, for the first time, by people who understood exactly what it cost her to let them.

Taya caught Toria's eye across the flame, the same way she had the morning Tiata asked where they were headed. Neither of them said anything either.

Some things, it turned out, only needed saying once.

9 — The First Target
↑ Back to top

They broke camp before the sun had fully cleared the tree line, and by the time the fire was cold and buried, Tiata had already decided how the morning's riding would be spent.

"You asked me for a plan," she said, bringing her horse alongside Toria's without waiting to be invited into the lead. "I have the start of one."

Nobody slowed. Nobody needed to. By now, when Tiata spoke like that, the whole line simply listened while it rode.

"There's an outpost northwest of here — not a fortress, not even a proper garrison. A watch-station, the kind Rome scatters along a frontier to say it's watching without spending what real watching costs. A dozen men, perhaps fewer by now, forgotten by whoever's supposed to be rotating them out. That's not an accident. Rome doesn't forget things by mistake this far from anywhere that matters to it. It forgets them because remembering costs coin it would rather spend elsewhere."

"And that makes it worth our time because—" Thana prompted, already ahead of the answer, the way she usually was.

"Because a message needs a place to land," Tiata said. "We've been cutting patrols and supply trains for months. Rome absorbs that the way a body absorbs a scratch — it doesn't like it, but it barely slows down. A watch-station is different. It's a fixed point. It reports in, on a schedule someone expects. When it stops reporting, someone has to ask why, and whoever answers that question has to explain it to someone above them, and so on, up a chain that doesn't like admitting a dozen of its own simply vanished off a frontier it swore was secure."

"You want them to notice," Toria said.

"I want them to have to explain it," Tiata said. "There's a difference. A raided supply train is bad luck. A watch-station gone silent, with nothing left to say what happened to it, is a question nobody in command wants asked twice — because the second time, someone has to answer for why the first one wasn't enough warning."

Taya, riding a little apart as she always did, turned that over without comment for a while before she spoke. "How many men have actually manned it, that you know of?"

"I don't know precisely. That's the first thing that needs learning before anything else." Tiata glanced her way — not quite a question, but an opening left for one. "You'll want eyes on it before we're anywhere near close enough to be seen ourselves."

"I generally do," Taya said, and left it there.

"And after?" Toria asked. "Once it's fallen?"

"After, Rome sends someone to ask questions nobody left alive can answer," Tiata said. "And somewhere far south of here, in a room none of us will ever see, someone with far too much authority and far too little information starts wondering whether the frontier he's been assured is secure actually is." She let that sit, watching the ground ahead rather than any of their faces. "That's the plan for now. Not to win a war. Just to make sure the people who think they're winning one start doubting it."

Nobody argued. By now, none of them expected to need to.

They rode on into country that grew wilder with every mile — the road thinning to a track, the track thinning to something that was barely a suggestion of one, trees closing in from both sides until the sky narrowed to a strip overhead. Before long the ground grew too broken and close-set with timber for the horses to press further, and they went the last stretch on foot, leading the animals rather than riding them, into cover thick enough to hide both.

10 — A Silent Station
↑ Back to top

Taya found it two days before the others reached it — a timber watch-tower and a low palisade wall, set on a rise where the old frontier road bent north, manned by fewer men than even Tiata had guessed. Nine, by her count. No more than that, and likely fewer once the night watch was subtracted from the count that mattered.

"Wood, not stone," she reported, back at the treeline where the others waited. "One gate, badly hung — I heard it complain in the wind from two hundred paces out. A watchtower at the northeast corner, one man in it most hours, sometimes none. They've gone soft here. Nobody's tested them in longer than any of them can remember."

"Good," Tiata said. "Soft is what we came for."

This was not a patrol, strung out and vulnerable on open ground, and all of them understood without needing to discuss it that the shape of what they'd done a dozen times already wouldn't simply transfer here. A watch-station had walls, however poor, and a gate, however badly hung, and men inside it who could bar a door and buy time even in their panic.

"No one gets out to raise anything," Tiata said, laying it out plainly once they'd gathered close enough to speak low. "Not a rider, not a runner, not a shout carried far enough to matter. This isn't a message we're sending with a survivor. This is a message we're sending with silence. Rome doesn't get an account of what happened here. It gets an absence, and absences are worse than accounts — a man can explain a raid. Nobody can explain nothing."

It was Taya who took the tower, going up and over the palisade in the dead hour before dawn, silent in a way that made even Tacita's quiet look effortful by comparison. The man on watch never called out. He simply stopped being a threat, and the tower's height became theirs instead of his — which mattered more than any single kill, because it meant nothing that happened next inside those walls would be seen by anyone outside them.

Thana and Tacita took the gate from the inside, quietly enough that the two men posted there never raised a sound worth hearing. Toria and Tiata went through the barracks door together, and what followed inside wasn't the kind of fighting any of them had done in open ground — no ambush to spring, no formation to shatter, just close, fast, brutal work in cramped quarters, men waking into their own deaths before they'd fully understood they were awake at all.

It was over before the sky had properly lightened. Nine men, and not one of them had gotten so much as a shout past the walls.

They didn't burn it. Fire drew eyes for miles, and eyes were exactly what this needed to avoid. Instead they left it standing — gate shut again, palisade intact, everything in its place except the men who should have been walking it. From a distance, riding past on the frontier road, it would look, for a while yet, like nothing was wrong at all. That delay was the whole of the point. The longer it looked normal, the longer before anyone thought to ask why it had stopped answering.

They took two horses from the station's small paddock before they left — sturdy, unremarkable frontier stock, nothing that would draw a second look on any road in the empire. Toria claimed one without discussion, running a hand down its neck the same unhurried way Taya always did, and Thana took the other, muttering something about finally not having to share a saddle with anyone's elbows. It meant, for the first time since Byzantium, that no one among them had to ride double. Small enough a thing, but none of them treated it as small.

"How long before someone notices?" Toria asked, wiping her blade clean on a dead man's cloak.

"Depends how badly Rome wants to admit it's stopped watching its own frontier," Tiata said. "Days, if we're fortunate. Longer, if the men meant to check on it are as soft as the men we just buried." She looked out over the quiet palisade, the closed gate, the ordinary morning settling over what had, an hour before, been nine lives. "Either way, by the time anyone works out what happened here, we'll be somewhere they've stopped thinking to look."

They left before full light, filing out through the same badly-hung gate Taya had heard complaining two days before, and struck north again into the deep timber, leaving the station standing silent behind them — a fixed point on Rome's maps that would, for a good while yet, keep lying about what it had become.

11 — Out of Place
↑ Back to top

The country north of Dacia thickened into forest by degrees, oak and pine crowding closer with every mile, until the line they rode between the old Iazyges pastures to the west and the Hasdingi holdings to the east had narrowed to something barely wide enough for the horses to pass single file. It was Gothic country now in all but name — had been for longer than any of the tribes still fighting over it cared to admit — and none of them expected to find much this deep in it beyond game trails and old grudges.

Taya found her first, as she found everything.

She lay along a fallen trunk for the better part of an hour before she came back to report it, which told the others something before she'd said a word — Taya didn't usually need an hour to decide what she was looking at.

"There's a woman half a mile ahead, dressing a boar over a low fire," she said. "Alone. No camp markers, no signs of kin nearby, nothing that says she's expecting company or minding a herd for someone else." She paused, choosing the rest of it with more care than she usually bothered with. "She doesn't belong here. Not the way anyone else out here belongs."

"Meaning what?" Tiata asked.

"Meaning I've seen Gothic dress and Gothic weapons the whole way through this stretch of forest, and hers isn't either. Fair enough hair for it, but the cut of her armor, the way she's built her fire, the way she's carrying herself — none of it reads local. She's a long way from wherever she started, and whatever brought her that far, it wasn't a trade caravan."

Tiata considered that for a moment, the same unhurried weighing she gave everything. "You think she's dangerous."

"I think she's competent," Taya said. "There's a difference, though it doesn't always work in our favor. She hasn't looked up once in an hour, which either means she hasn't noticed me — unlikely — or she has, and doesn't consider one watcher in the trees worth breaking stride over." A faint, private note of respect crept into that last part, the kind Taya rarely extended to strangers. "I'd want your read on her before we get any closer. This isn't ground I know as well as you do."

It was, in its way, its own kind of compliment — Taya conceding a judgment to someone else's territory rather than trusting her own instinct alone, which by now the others understood as rare enough to be worth noticing.

Tiata studied the tree line for a long moment, though there was nothing to see from here but green and shadow. "If she's been taken from somewhere and dragged this far south, there are only a few ways that happens," she said, half to Taya, half to herself. "Gothic raiding parties push north against the tribes along the Baltic coast more than Rome ever bothers to. If she's not Gothic and not local, that's the likeliest story — captured somewhere up past where any of us have been, brought south, and somewhere along the way, she stopped being anyone's prisoner."

"You've seen this before," Toria said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I've seen enough of it," Tiata said. "Rome isn't the only power on this continent that profits from moving people who don't belong to it." She straightened, decision settling over her the way it always did — quick, once made, final. "Let's go and find out for ourselves, rather than guess at it from a hundred paces out."

Taya led them in, quiet as ever, and the woman by the fire didn't so much as glance up until they were within a dozen paces of her — at which point she looked up all at once, entirely unsurprised, as though she'd been tracking their approach for longer than any of them would have liked to think.

She was built like something the forest itself might have put together on a slow afternoon — tall, broad through the shoulder in a way none of the rest of them quite matched, an axe within easy reach of one hand and a boar half-dressed in front of her, as unbothered by five armed strangers as she'd apparently been by Taya's hour on the fallen trunk.

"You've been standing in my treeline since before the sun moved a hand's width," she said, in accented but serviceable Latin, nodding once toward where Taya had been. "I was starting to wonder if you were ever going to come down and say something, or if I'd have to finish this boar and eat alone."

Nobody answered right away. It was, all of them realized in the same instant, the first time since Byzantium that someone had spotted Taya before Taya had finished deciding what to make of them.

"Sit, if you're staying," the woman added, turning back to her work without waiting for a reply. "There's more boar than I need for one."

They sat.

12 — Rugii
↑ Back to top

The boar was good, and Tove made sure everyone said so before she let the conversation move anywhere near her.

She fed the fire like she did everything else — generously, without waste, tearing off the better cuts of meat and passing them along before anyone thought to ask, laughing easily at Thana's dry remarks in a way that made even Thana's smirk soften into something like genuine amusement. By the time the meal was down to bones and embers, she'd made herself, without seeming to try, the most immediately likable person any of them had met since Byzantium.

It was Tiata who finally set the warmth aside long enough to ask what the rest of them had been too well-mannered, or too wary, to ask outright.

"You're not Gothic," she said. "You're not local to any tribe within a hundred miles of this fire. And you speak Latin better than most of the Romans I've killed for the privilege of practicing mine." She said it without accusation, the same flat directness she gave everything. "I'd like to know how you came to be sitting alone in this forest, that far from wherever you started."

Tove didn't flinch from it. If anything, something in her seemed almost relieved to finally have the question sitting plainly on the ground between them instead of circling unspoken.

"Rugii," she said. "Though I doubt the name means much to any of you this far south. My people held the coast a long way north of here — cold water, colder winters, the kind of place Rome's never once bothered sending a legion, because there's nothing there Rome wants badly enough to freeze for it." A faint, wry twist at the corner of her mouth. "The Goths wanted it badly enough, though. Or wanted us gone from it, which amounts to the same thing when you're the one being pushed."

"Gothic raiders took you," Tiata said. Not a question — a confirmation, matching what she'd already guessed back at the tree line.

"Took quite a few of us, over a few bad years. I wasn't the only one, and I wasn't the last." Tove turned a piece of bone over in her hands, not quite looking at any of them now. "They didn't keep me for themselves, not for long. I was traded on, the way anyone useful and unclaimed gets traded on — south, mostly, toward people who had use for hands that could work and backs that could carry. Somewhere in all that moving, I ended up in the keeping of Vandal traders. Hasdingi, if the distinction matters to you."

"It does," Tiata said, quietly.

"Then you'll know they'd made themselves useful to Rome by then. Land for loyalty, or something close enough to it — I never heard the whole of the arrangement, only enough to understand that whatever I was worth to them, some part of that worth was owed, one way or another, to keeping Rome content." The wryness in her voice hardened, just slightly, into something with more edge. "So you could say I have two grudges instead of one. The tribe that took me from my own people, and the empire that made itself comfortable enough in the taking that nobody involved saw any particular need to ask what I wanted."

"How did you get free of them?" Toria asked, leaning in despite herself.

"Badly, and slowly, and mostly by being underestimated," Tove said, and something like her earlier warmth came back into her voice, threaded now with real satisfaction. "People who move captives for profit get careless about which of us they think capable of anything beyond carrying what we're told to carry. I waited until careless turned into an actual mistake, and then I made sure it cost them more than they'd bargained losing." She shrugged, as if the details weren't worth the breath. "I've been on my own since. Long enough now that I'd stopped expecting company worth keeping — until an hour of watching five strangers not quite manage to sneak up on me."

That drew something close to a real laugh around the fire, Taya included, which was itself no small thing.

"Any interest in trading one kind of alone for a different kind?" Toria asked, when the laughter had settled.

Tove looked at each of them in turn, unhurried, the same frank assessment she'd apparently been making since before any of them knew she was watching.

"I've been waiting for something worth my time since I stopped being anyone's captive and started being nobody's anything," she said. "I don't know yet if that's you. But I know I'd rather find out than spend another season alone in this forest wondering." She picked her axe back up, resting it easy across her knees the way Tiata rested her falx. "So. Where are we headed?"

13 — West
↑ Back to top

Nobody answered right away. It was Tacita, in the end, who did.

She rose from where she'd been sitting at the edge of the firelight, unhurried, and drew her sword — not to threaten, not to explain, simply to have something in her hand worth pointing with. She held it out, arm level, blade steady, and turned until it settled due west, into the dark beyond the trees.

That was the whole of her answer. She didn't sheath the blade again right away, and she didn't look to Tiata, or to anyone, for confirmation that the gesture had landed the way she meant it.

It landed.

Tove studied the line of the blade for a moment, then looked back at the fire, at all of them, with something that might have been the beginning of real respect. "West," she said, testing the shape of the word in her own mouth as much as answering it. "Away from the Hasdingi. Away from the people who sold me."

"Away from what took you," Tiata said, "but not away from what it means. West is still a direction, not a target. What we do with it is ours to decide."

Nobody corrected her. Nobody needed to.

They moved before the fire had fully died, west and further west, the ground rising and falling under old growth that had never once heard a Roman axe. Tove fell into the line easily, matching pace without being asked, reading the ground the way the rest of them had long since learned to read each other.

It was Taya, again, who found the trouble before the trouble found them.

"Party ahead," she reported, dropping back from a rise just wide enough to see through the trees. "Six, maybe seven. Moving east — toward us, not away. Loaded down, from the look of their pace. Not hunting. Coming home from somewhere."

Tove's jaw tightened at the word. "Hasdingi carry themselves a particular way when they're heavy with plunder," she said. "Loud enough to matter, careless enough to notice. That's them."

"Then they're a long way from home to be careless," Tiata said, already reading the ground the way she read everything — not for the fight itself, but for the shape it should take. "Taya, the same as always. Tove—" she paused, weighing the offer before she made it, "—this one's yours to lead, if you want it."

Tove's answer wasn't in words. She simply hefted the axe, already moving toward the treeline that would put her closest to whatever came first.

The Hasdingi party walked into the ambush the way careless men always did — strung out, talking too loud for men who should have known better this far from any territory they could call safe. Taya's arrow took the first before he'd finished his sentence, and the rest broke the way broken lines always broke, chaos before sense, exactly the gap the vanguard had learned to live inside.

Tove didn't wait for the formation to fully collapse. She went in low and fast for someone her size, axe finding the gap between one man's shield and his ribs before he'd registered the threat had a face. There was nothing showy in it — no flourish, no cruelty beyond what the moment required — but there was a raw, focused violence in her that hadn't been visible at all around the fire where they'd found her, and Toria, working the opposite flank, found herself briefly grateful not to be standing where Tove was standing.

Thana and Tacita closed the rest of it the way they always did — fast, economical, no wasted motion — and within minutes the party that had walked west so carelessly wasn't walking anywhere again.

Tove stood over the last of them a moment longer than the fight required, breathing hard, something unreadable moving behind her eyes.

"Feel any different?" Toria asked, not unkindly.

"No," Tove said, after a moment, sounding almost surprised by her own answer. "I thought it might. It doesn't." She wiped the axe clean, matter-of-fact again, the warmth already creeping back into her voice. "Turns out revenge isn't really what I wanted. Just this." She gestured, loosely, at all of them. "Something worth standing beside."

They took what the party carried — enough to matter, nothing worth slowing for — and pushed on until the light had nearly gone, making camp in a hollow Taya chose for exactly the reasons Taya always chose a camp: wind, sightlines, water close enough to reach without exposing anyone to find it.

The fire that night burned easy, for a first night. Tove told the story of the boar — short, well-shaped, not a word in it that didn't earn its place — and even Tacita's mouth moved in something that might, in poor light, have been mistaken for a smile.

14 — Along the Frontier
↑ Back to top

They moved west for the better part of a month. One territory gave way to the next without any line drawn to mark it, except the ones Rome insisted existed on the far side of a river that didn't much care what anyone called it.

Through Iazyges country the going was easiest, open plain and old grazing ground, and Taya moved through it like she'd been born to exactly this kind of horizon — which, in every way that mattered, she had. Tove rode behind Tiata, whose horse was easily the sturdiest of the five and bore the both of them without complaint, trading some dry, unhurried remark with her often enough that the rest of the line had learned to let the two of them have it. They crossed paths with a handful of Iazyges riders twice, and both times the riders simply watched them go, unbothered, six women on five horses being a curiosity rather than a threat to people who measured everything in horses first.

Where the plain gave out, they picked up the line of the frontier itself and followed it west — close enough to see the watchtowers on a clear day, far enough that no patrol ever had cause to look their direction twice. It was strange country to ride, Roman order on one hand and everything else on the other, and more than once Tiata pointed out some fresh scar on the land — a burned granary, a hastily rebuilt stretch of palisade — that told its own story of a frontier under more pressure than the maps in any governor's tent would admit to.

Quadi territory took them longer than any of them expected. The Quadi kept close to Rome by treaty and old habit, client kings and Roman garrisons sitting near enough to each other that the vanguard spent most of that stretch moving at night, sleeping through the worst of the daylight in whatever cover the ground offered. Twice they came near enough to a Roman supply column to have taken it, and twice Tiata judged the risk not worth what little it would buy them this far from anywhere the empire would feel the loss. "We're not here to bleed every vein we cross," she said, the second time, when Toria's hand had strayed to the falcata a moment too long. "We're here to reach the one that matters."

Marcomanni land came next, rougher and less watched, and it was here that Tove came into her own in a way none of them had yet seen — she knew this stretch of country, or knew people who'd known it, secondhand paths and half-remembered river crossings passed along by others who'd been moved through it the way she once had. More than once her instinct for the ground saved them a hard day's travel, and more than once she caught herself, mid-sentence, describing a landmark with the flat, practiced ease of someone who'd once been marched past it as cargo rather than a traveler in her own right. Nobody remarked on it. They'd learned, by now, when to let a silence sit unfilled.

There was a skirmish, one grey morning in the Marcomanni hills — not planned, not chosen, simply bad luck meeting bad luck. A hunting party stumbled onto their camp before dawn, more startled than hostile, and it ended fast and ugly the way surprised violence always did, three of the hunters dead before either side had fully understood what was happening, the rest scattering back into the treeline rather than press a fight they hadn't wanted in the first place. Nobody in the vanguard was hurt beyond a graze Thana barely acknowledged. It left all of them quieter than usual for a day afterward, the particular unease of a fight that had cost lives for no reason either side could have named.

By the time the ground softened into the river valleys of Alemanni territory, autumn had turned the whole frontier the color of old bronze. Six women who'd started this stretch as five plus a stranger around a fire now moved and thought more like one animal than six.

The Alemanni themselves were newer to this ground than almost anyone else along the frontier — a confederation still finding its own shape, drawn together out of smaller peoples the way the vanguard itself had been, though for entirely different reasons. Tiata, watching the first Alemanni farmstead they passed at a careful distance, said as much herself.

"They're young, as peoples go," she said. "Still deciding what they are. That makes them dangerous in ways older tribes aren't — nothing to lose yet that they haven't already lost once, getting here."

"Sounds familiar," Toria said.

Tiata didn't disagree.

15 — In Full View
↑ Back to top

Thilda had been walking north for three days when she found the smoke.

Not battle-smoke — she knew that smell too well by now to mistake it for anything gentler — but a cook-fire, banked low and careful, the kind of fire built by people who understood exactly how far its scent would carry and had decided to risk it anyway. That alone was enough to slow her step. Careless fires got built by careless people, and careless people didn't survive long in the country between Raetia and Upper Germania, not anymore, not with Rome pressing from the south and every tribe along this stretch of border spending as much blood on each other as on the legions.

She had been walking that particular tightrope for longer than she cared to count. Her own people held ground that mattered to nobody who mattered — no gold in it, no grain worth an emperor's attention, just forest and hard winters and a stubborn refusal to disappear quietly the way so much of this frontier had already been persuaded to. That refusal had cost them. It had cost her, specifically, more than she let herself total up on any single night, and somewhere in the losing of it she had stopped waiting for anyone else to stand the line beside her and simply learned to hold it alone.

She scouted for herself now. For whatever was left standing that still needed scouting. It amounted to the same work either way.

The fire, when she finally worked close enough to study it properly from a fold in the ridge above, belonged to six women — which was itself strange enough to hold her attention past the point good sense would have moved her on. Armed, all of them, and not armed the way farmers carried a blade for show. She watched the way they sat, the way their eyes never all rested in the same direction at once, the easy economy in how one of them fed the fire without looking away from the tree line while she did it.

Soldiers didn't move like that. Bandits didn't either, not for long. This was something else — something that had survived long enough to stop needing to prove it to each other.

She watched for the better part of an hour, weighing it the way she weighed everything now, cost against use, before she made the only decision that had ever made sense to her in situations like this one.

She stood, in full view, and walked down toward the fire without hiding a single step of it.

They were on their feet before she'd covered half the distance — fast, controlled, no wasted motion, six weapons finding six angles without a word passing between any of them. She stopped well short of striking range and simply waited, both hands visible, spear planted upright in the dirt beside her rather than leveled at anyone.

"I'm alone," she said, in workable if accented Latin. "I've been alone a long while. I saw your fire three ridges back and decided I'd rather find out what it was than spend another night wondering."

A tall woman with a falx stepped forward from among them — the one, Thilda judged quickly, whose stillness carried more weight than anyone else's movement. "That's a long way to walk on a guess."

"I've walked further on worse," Thilda said.

They didn't lower their weapons yet. But nobody moved to close the distance either, and after a long, measuring moment, the falx-bearer nodded once — not welcome, not yet, but a door left open rather than shut.

"Sit, if you're going to," she said. "We'll decide the rest after."

Thilda sat. She didn't tell them everything that had brought her to this ridge, this fire, this particular late point in a war she'd been fighting alone for longer than she wanted to admit out loud. That would keep. Some of it would keep for a long time yet, the way it always had.

But she told them enough — that Upper Germania was her home and had been her people's for longer than Rome had bothered learning its name, that the losing of ground here had been slow and then sudden the way it always was, that she had buried more of her own than she had patience left to speak of in a single sitting, to strangers, on a first night.

It was enough for now. She could see, watching the tall one's face, that it had landed as exactly that — enough, not everything, and understood as such without needing to be told.

Someone passed her a share of whatever was left over the fire — a smaller woman, quick-moving, who set it down beside her without ceremony and went back to watching the tree line as if she'd never stopped. Thilda ate without hurrying, the first meal in longer than she wanted to admit that she hadn't had to eat standing, weapon in hand, watching her own back while she chewed.

"You've fought Rome directly," the falx-bearer said, at length. Not a question.

"More than I've fought anyone else, this past year," Thilda said. "Though the tribes pressing from the north give me nearly as much work. Rome, at least, I understand the shape of. It wants land, and it's patient about taking it. The others just want what's left when Rome's finished, and they're not patient at all."

"That's most of this frontier, in one sentence," the falx-bearer said, and something in her tone suggested she meant it as something close to approval.

"You're not from here," Thilda said. It wasn't quite a question either.

"None of us are from any one place," said the one with the axe, older sounding than she looked, amusement threaded through it. "That's rather the point."

Thilda looked around the fire once more, slower this time — the falx, the twin blades resting easy against one woman's leg, the round shield with its worn boss propped near another's shoulder, the stillness of the one who hadn't spoken a single word since she'd walked into the light. Six women who by every reasonable account should have had nothing holding them together, and did, plainly, all the same.

"Good," she said. "I've had enough of understanding a shape by myself."

Nobody answered that directly. But the fire burned a while longer before anyone moved to bank it for the night, and nobody suggested she ought to be moving on.

16 — Roxolani
↑ Back to top

Morning came grey and mild, and Thilda woke to find the camp already stirring around her in the unhurried, practiced way of people who'd done this exact routine more mornings than they could easily count.

It was Tiata who made the introductions, moving around the dead fire with the same flat economy she brought to everything else.

"Toria," she said, nodding to the one with the falcata, already checking the edge of her blade out of habit rather than need. "Iberian-born. Taken as a girl when Rome put down trouble in her province — she doesn't remember whether her own people caused it. Gladiatrix out of Byzantium before any of the rest of this started."

Toria didn't look up from the blade, but something in her shoulders acknowledged it all the same.

"Thana," Tiata went on, nodding to the one with the easy, sharp smile. "Greek. Taken by pirates off her own coast at fourteen, sold into the same arena. Eleven bouts survived on nothing but reading a fight faster than the woman across from her could."

"Flattering, when you say it like that," Thana said, though she didn't disagree with any of it.

"Tacita." A level glance from the quiet one was the whole of her greeting. "The breakout that got the three of them out of Byzantium was hers, start to finish. She usually lets her gladius speak for her."

Tacita's only reply was the faintest incline of her head — not denial, not modesty, just confirmation, offered and withdrawn in the same breath.

"Tove." The broad-shouldered one with the axe, who grinned outright and passed over the last of the previous night's meat before anyone had asked. "Rugii, from a long way north of anywhere on this frontier. Taken by Gothic raiders, traded on to Hasdingi hands, freed herself the same patient way she does everything else."

"And Taya," Tiata finished, nodding to the one already half a distance apart from the rest, watching the tree line the way she seemed to watch everything.

Tiata paused there, longer than the introduction required.

"Which is, I realize, a name I've said to strangers a dozen times without once hearing the whole of what sits behind it," she said, half to Thilda, half to herself, an admission that seemed to genuinely surprise her as she made it. "You joined before I did. I've never actually asked."

Taya's mouth curved, faint and dry. "You've been busy asking everyone else's."

"That's not an answer."

"No," Taya agreed. "It isn't."

She was quiet a moment, weighing it the way she weighed everything, and when she finally spoke it came the way most things came from her — brief, exact, nothing wasted.

"Roxolani," she said. "One of the Sarmatian peoples, though by the time I was old enough to matter to anyone, 'people' was generous. A generation of Rome grinding at us had turned a confederation into a handful of families who couldn't agree which direction to die running. Some wanted peace with an empire that had never once kept a peace it made. Some wanted a fight we no longer had the numbers to win. I rode with the second half long enough to bury most of them, and when what was left of my clan finally split for good, I didn't go with either side." She looked out at the trees, not at any of them. "I went alone. It was the only option on offer that didn't require becoming something I'd already decided I didn't want to be."

"And you've been alone since," Tiata said.

"Alone by choice, which isn't the same thing as alone by loss, though I understand most people can't always tell the difference from outside it." A faint, private note entered her voice — not quite warmth, but close enough to it. "I watched these three for the better part of a day before I ever spoke to them. Decided they were worth the trouble before they knew I existed. That's simply how I do things. It served me well enough on my own. It's served better since."

Tiata studied her for a long moment, the same weighing look she gave everyone, and nodded once — the closest thing to a verdict she ever handed down.

"Good," she said. "Arithmetic, again. I can use that."

"You say that to everyone," Thana called over, not quite hiding a smile.

"I say it to everyone because it's true of everyone worth having," Tiata said, entirely unbothered. "That's rather the point of saying it more than once."

Thilda, watching all of it unfold around a fire she'd been sitting at for less than a full day, found herself, for the first time in longer than she wanted to name, in no particular hurry to be anywhere else.

17 — Away from the Frontier
↑ Back to top

They didn't break camp with the morning, and nobody suggested they should.

Tiata sat with the fire still smoking between them, the falx across her knees the way it always rested when she meant to think rather than move, and looked at Thilda with the same flat attention she gave any question worth actually answering.

"You know this ground better than any of us," she said. "Better than me, and I've spent longer reading frontiers than most. Before we decide where we're riding next, I'd rather hear where you'd take us."

It wasn't a small thing, and Thilda understood that plainly enough not to waste it. She'd spent a year listening to men who gave orders without ever once asking what the ground actually permitted, and the difference sat with her for a moment before she answered.

"North along the border, the way you've been moving, you'll keep finding what you've already found," she said. "Watch-posts, patrols, the occasional supply line worth bleeding. Useful. Steady. It won't ever be more than steady, though — Rome expects losses along that stretch, budgets for them, barely blinks." She nodded, slowly, westward, away from the frontier entirely. "West, the ground changes. Fewer eyes, less discipline, tribes still working out who they are and who they owe anything to. Harder country to move through safely. But anything you do there, nobody's expecting, and nobody Roman has to explain losing it — because officially, Rome isn't watching that ground closely enough to have lost anything there at all."

"An absence, rather than an account," Tiata said, echoing her own words from the watch-station without seeming to notice she'd done it.

"If that's how you put it, yes." Thilda's mouth twitched, not quite a smile. "I've spent a year learning exactly how much Rome doesn't watch what it doesn't think it needs to. It's more than you'd guess."

Taya, listening from the edge of the fire, spoke up without being asked, which was itself its own small signal of how far the morning had already shifted. "If we go west, I'll need days ahead of the rest of you to read it properly. Unfamiliar ground, unfamiliar people — I won't move us blind into either."

"Take what you need," Tiata said, without hesitation.

Tove leaned back, arms folded, something considering in her expression. "West also puts more distance between us and everyone who might still be looking east for us," she offered. "No harm in that, this far along."

"And less distance from whatever's waiting for us once we get there," Toria said — not an objection, simply the other half of the same coin, laid out plainly the way she always laid things out.

Tiata let all of it settle before she spoke again, the same unhurried weighing she gave everything, each voice folded into the decision rather than argued past.

"West, then," she said, at last. "Not blindly. Taya goes ahead alone, same as always — she'll move faster and quieter without the rest of us slowing her. The rest of us hold somewhere safe until she's read enough of it to bring us in behind her."

Nobody argued. By now, none of them expected to need to — not because Tiata's word was law, but because everyone at that fire had just watched her actually listen before she gave it.

"There's a hollow half a day south of here," Thilda said, into the pause that followed. "Good water, better cover, nothing on the approach that isn't visible a long way off. If Taya needs days to read what's west of us properly, that's where I'd sit and wait for her word, rather than push the rest of us blind into ground even she hasn't seen yet."

Taya nodded once, already turning the shape of it over. "That works. I'll move faster alone than any of you could keep pace with regardless."

"Then that's settled," Tiata said.

They broke camp within the hour — not west yet, but south, toward the hollow Thilda had named, seven of them moving together while Taya struck out alone ahead, into ground none of the rest of them had seen before.

18 — Blind to the West
↑ Back to top

Taya left before the others had finished breaking camp. No goodbyes, no last-minute cautions — she preferred it that way. She took the mare and nothing that could rattle or catch light, and by the time the sun cleared the trees she was three ridges west of where the frontier bent north.

Alemanni farmsteads first, scattered and new, the wood still pale and unweathered. She noted two homesteads, the distance between them, which way the smoke leaned in the wind — nothing useful today, but worth remembering.

Past the farmsteads the land roughened into forest. She slowed the mare to a walk and read the ground at that pace instead.

She found the trail by midmorning: old but used, running the direction they meant to go, worn by feet rather than hooves. People without horses to spare. Poorer country, and likely the loose, unwatched ground Thilda had promised. She followed it from the saddle, further west than she'd meant to go on the first day, because it kept giving her reason to keep going.

By early afternoon she understood why.

The trail crossed a ford — shallow, old, worn smooth by long use, with nothing standing guard over it. No palisade. No watchtower. Every Roman crossing she'd ever scouted had one, even a poorly manned one. This had none. She sat above it a while, working out what Thilda had meant back at the fire — not ground Rome had forgotten, ground Rome had never bothered learning about in the first place.

That alone would have been enough for the day.

She followed the far bank north a while instead, out of habit, and that was when she saw it — straight lines against the treeline, where nothing straight grew on its own.

A fort. Not a watch-station. Stone footings, from what she could make out, and enough of a footprint to house a real garrison. She spent the rest of the afternoon working the treeline in a slow half-circle, getting close enough to be sure without being seen.

It sat at the exact point where the frontier turned north to follow the river and stopped watching west. The walls faced east and south, back the way the frontier came from. On the west side, behind the fort itself, a supply track ran wide and open — wagons coming and going on a plain schedule, because nobody expected anyone to be watching from that direction.

She counted what she could see. Enough men to matter, not a skeleton crew. Walls too strong to take head-on with seven, the way they'd taken the wooden station months before. But that supply road was undefended, and the whole fort was built facing the wrong way.

This was the target Toria had described, back before any of this had a plan behind it — a fixed point, not a scratch Rome could shrug off. Nobody had known then where to find one. Now there was one, sitting exposed on its blind side.

She could have moved on it herself that night. A courier on the road, a careless watch — she'd done that kind of work alone for three winters and still knew how. One woman could have taken something small from that fort tonight.

She didn't. A raid like that would cost the fort one man and put the rest on alert for nothing. Seven women, working from what she'd learned, could hit that supply road hard enough to spook the whole garrison — enough dead couriers, enough silence where reports should be, that the men inside started doubting the ground they thought was safe behind them. She marked the road, the blind approach, and the two places a rider could cross the ford unseen, then turned back before the light went.

It was full dusk by the time she reached the hollow, later than she'd said she'd be. Tiata was on her feet before Taya had cleared the treeline.

"You found something," Tiata said. Not a question.

"I found the garrison," Taya said.

The others gathered without being called. Taya laid it out plainly — the ford, the fort, the supply road nobody was watching.

"Stone-built. Real numbers, not a skeleton crew. We're not walking in and clearing it the way we cleared the watch-station." She looked at Toria. "But it's the one you asked for. Back before Tiata, before any of this had a plan. That road is wide open. We can't take the fort. We can make the men inside it stop trusting it."

Toria was quiet a moment. "How long to plan it properly?"

"Longer than tonight," Taya said. "But it can be done. I saw how."

Tiata looked around the fire once — Toria still, Thana already turning the shape of it over, Tacita watching, Tove's hand resting on the axe across her knees, Thilda leaning in.

"Then that's where we're going," Tiata said.

Nobody argued.

19 — Released
↑ Back to top

They crossed the ford at first light, single file, five horses and two riding double, and said nothing until the fort's shape rose clear through the trees ahead of them.

Taya split them to their work without needing to be asked twice. She and Thana went to ground on the west side, within sight of the supply road and the blind stretch of wall behind it — the side Taya had already read as open, and the side that mattered most to confirm before anything else. Tacita and Toria worked around to the east, where the wall stood higher and better watched, reading what a real approach from that direction would cost, in case it was ever needed. Tove and Thilda ranged the ground behind the western treeline, marking cover, water, and a line the horses could hold without giving anyone away.

What the west side gave up matched everything Taya had reported. Wagons rolled through the gate on that side on a loose, trusting schedule, waved through without a challenge called down from the wall. No patrol crossed that ground all morning. Every patrol that did go out swung east and south instead, walking the line the fort had always watched, the line the frontier had trained every man inside it to think of as the danger.

"They still don't look this way," Taya said, low, watching a wagon roll through unchallenged. "Not once, all morning."

Tove found ground worth using not far back from the road, on the blind side — a hollow, water close, cover thick on three sides. Thilda walked its edges herself before she'd trust it.

"There's a patrol runs somewhere near this ground," she said, once she'd finished. "Small. Four men, maybe five. Not part of the eastern loop — a stray from it, most likely. If we hold here quiet, we take it clean tomorrow, well clear of where we're sitting."

Tiata turned that over, the falx across her knees. "Tomorrow, then. Taya first light, eyes on their—"

She didn't finish it.

Taya was already on her feet, one hand raised flat for quiet, head turned toward the trees south of the hollow. A moment later the others heard what she'd caught first — voices, unhurried, far closer than anything had a right to be.

"That's them," she said, low. "The same patrol. Come further off their line than they should have, and headed straight at us." She looked to Thilda. "This was meant to be clear ground."

"It should have been," Thilda said, already moving. "They don't stray this far from the eastern loop. Not on anything I've read."

There was no time to settle whose read had failed. Tiata was up, falx in hand.

"Positions," she said. "The ones we marked for tomorrow. We use them now."

They moved fast and silent, each of them already knowing where Thilda's ground had placed her, where Tove's strength would matter, where Taya's bow would open it. The patrol walked into it exactly as Taya had read them — unhurried, unaware — and the ambush took four of them down within a dozen heartbeats of the first arrow.

The fifth was young, younger than the armor suggested he had any business wearing, and when Toria's blade came level with his throat he didn't raise his own to meet it. He dropped it instead, both hands open, shaking hard enough that it carried up into his shoulders.

"Hold," Tiata said, before Toria's arm had finished its swing.

Toria held. It cost her something — that much was plain on her face — but she held.

Tiata studied the boy a long moment, the same flat, weighing look she gave everything.

"How long have you served here?" she asked, in workable Latin.

"Six months," he managed. "Less."

"Then you're young enough to still remember what fear feels like, before it wears down into habit," Tiata said. "That's useful to me today." She nodded to Toria, who lowered the blade without lowering her guard. "You're going to walk back to that fort. Not run — walk, so you have time to remember all of it. Tell them four of your patrol are dead and you're the one left to say so. Tell them seven women did it, in less time than it takes to explain, on the side of that fort nobody thought needed watching."

The boy said nothing. He didn't look capable of it.

"Tell them exactly that," Tiata said. "Don't soften it for your own sake, and don't guess at anything you didn't see. The truth, plain, is worth more to me than anything you could invent to shrink it."

They stripped him of blade and shield, anything that might let him claim afterward he'd fought rather than survived, and let him go. He didn't look back once.

Taya watched him go until the trees took him, then turned to the others.

"That wasn't silence," she said. "That's the opposite of the watch-station."

"It was meant to be," Tiata said. "A forgotten station gets erased quietly, and nobody above it ever has to explain the gap. A garrison this size, with men left who'll ask questions when four of their own don't come back — that fort needs a story it can't control, told by someone who was there and can't be accused of exaggerating it." She looked toward the fort, still standing exactly as it had that morning, not yet knowing what it had lost. "Let them wonder what's coming next. Wondering costs more than four men ever could."

They broke the ambush site clean and moved deeper into cover before the fort could send anyone out looking. Behind them, a boy who would carry the whole of it walked alone toward a gate that would soon be a great deal less certain of what stood outside it.

20 — Movement in the Dark
↑ Back to top

They moved twice more before dark, each time a short distance, each time Thilda's choice — always toward better cover, never far enough to lose sight of the fort itself. By the third stop even Tove had stopped asking whether it was necessary. After a boy walked home with a story like that one, necessary wasn't a question anyone needed answered twice.

The fort sat low against the last light, a shape more felt than seen once the sun went fully down, and they made no fire near it that night either.

Watch fell to three, in the order it always fell when the ground itself might be the danger rather than what walked on it — Taya, Tove, then Thilda, the two best readers of a treeline bracketing the one who trusted her ears over her eyes. Taya took the last third, the grey hour before dawn that asked the most of whoever held it and forgave the least.

She set herself further out than Tove or Thilda had bothered to, out past the edge where a sleeper's turning or a horse's shift in its footing could still reach her. She wanted nothing between herself and the fort but ground.

For a long while there was nothing to read. The wall held its shape against a sky just beginning to consider grey. No torches moved along it. No gate opened.

Then something changed, and she couldn't have said what.

Not sound — nothing had reached her ears that hadn't been there a moment before. Not sight either, not yet. Something under both, the way ground told her things before her eyes caught up to confirm them, the way she'd learned to trust years before she'd ever had a name for what she was trusting. She went still all the way through, the stillness that cost nothing and gave everything, and narrowed everything she had down onto the base of that wall.

There.

A shape, low, there and gone in less time than it took to be sure of it — a single movement at the foot of the stone, the kind a body makes crossing from one kind of dark into another. Then nothing. The wall held its shape again, patient as it had been all night, as if it hadn't given anything away at all.

She didn't move. She didn't call out. She held the same narrow focus and waited to be wrong.

A few breaths later, north of where she'd caught the first of it — a flicker at the treeline, gone before it had properly arrived, the kind of motion that belonged to something that knew exactly how much darkness it needed and took no more than that. Then stillness, complete and ordinary, as though the forest had never held anything else.

That was enough.

Taya came off her position without hurry and without sound, back toward the sleepers, and touched Tiata's shoulder once.

Tiata was awake before the touch had finished landing — she always was.

"Something came off that wall," Taya said, low enough to die a foot from her own mouth. "One shape, then a second further out, moving for the trees. I didn't see it clean either time. I felt it before I saw it."

Tiata didn't ask her to swear to it. She never did, not with Taya.

"Down," she said, already turning to the others. "All of you. Now."

It took no more than the time it took to say it. Seven bodies went from sleep to stillness without a sound wasted between the two states, low against the ground, weapons already found by hands that hadn't needed to look for them.

They lay low, and they waited.

21 — The Tigress of Lorch
↑ Back to top

Taran had learned, three years ago, that a cage didn't need bars to be a cage. It only needed everyone around it to agree on where the walls were.

She awoke that morning, as she did every morning, in the small chamber off the Praefectus's own quarters, and dressed in the clothes the household had settled on for her — Roman enough at the collar to satisfy anyone who might object, loose enough through the shoulder that she could still move as she'd been built to move, red hair left loose and uncut because he liked it that way, liked the color of it against the grey stone of his house, liked the story it told before she'd ever said a word.

She had stopped hating the clothes years ago. Hating something that changed nothing was a waste of the only thing they hadn't managed to take from her, which was time to think.

Breakfast she took standing, near the door, as it had always been done since the first month, when someone had decided a woman like her sitting at table looked wrong to guests who hadn't yet been told what to expect from her. She didn't mind it. Standing let her see the room. Sitting never had.

The morning brought two officers up from Raetia, minor men on some minor errand, and the Praefectus did what he always did when new eyes came through his gate — found a reason to have her brought into the hall, found a reason to mention, in the particular tone he used only for this, that she'd been taken in Britannia three years past, that she'd fought three of his own men to a standstill before they'd finally worn her down, that even now, three years tamed, she could still put a blade through a man's throat before he'd finished drawing his own.

"Show them," he said, as he always said it, and held out the practice blade himself, because he never let her hold a live one in that hall, not with guests present, not with anyone but him close enough to take it back if her hand ever forgot its training.

She showed them. She always showed them. It cost her nothing that mattered to move well for an audience that had already decided what her movement meant — proof of Rome's reach, proof that even the wildest thing dragged out of the furthest edge of the map could be brought to heel and kept, years from home, still red-haired, still dangerous-looking, still exactly the kind of trophy that made lesser men lean forward at dinner. She'd learned to give them exactly that and nothing more. The part of her that actually knew how to kill a man, she kept for herself, same as everything else that still belonged only to her.

The two officers left before midday, suitably impressed, and the household settled back into its ordinary rhythm — one she'd had three years to learn in every particular. She knew which guard drank in the evenings and which one didn't. She knew the armory was checked at dawn and rarely again before dusk. She knew the Praefectus's moods as a farmer learns the sky — not because she could change what was coming, but because knowing gave her time to prepare for it — when he wanted her silent, when he wanted her shown off, when a bad week made him crueler and a good one made him almost forget, for an hour, that she wasn't simply his.

She had spent three years watching all of it, cataloguing without a plan yet to spend the knowledge on, because a woman with nowhere to run didn't waste herself dreaming of running. She had made herself patient instead. Patient as stone. She had decided, somewhere in the second year, that she would know the moment when it came, because everything else in her life since Britannia had at least had the decency to announce itself plainly once it arrived.

Dusk came grey and early that day, cook-fires already lit, and she was standing where she always stood at that hour — near the Praefectus, in the long room where he took his reports, present because she was always present, because nobody in that house had ever once thought to ask whether she should be.

The boy came in still shaking.

She knew before he'd said a word that something real had happened, because nothing about the household's ordinary rhythm produced a soldier who walked like that — stripped of blade and shield, dust and worse on him, eyes that hadn't yet decided whether they were done being afraid.

"Speak," the Praefectus said, more sharply than he needed to, the particular sharpness of a man who already suspected the news wasn't going to flatter him.

The boy spoke.

Four of his patrol, dead. Killed inside a dozen heartbeats, on the western ground nobody watched, by seven women who'd known exactly where to wait and exactly when to move. He hadn't fought, because there had been nothing left to fight by the time anyone understood what was happening. They'd let him go. They'd told him to walk, not run, so he'd remember it right. They'd told him to tell it plain.

The room went very still.

Taran watched the Praefectus's face do the thing faces did when a man's whole understanding of his own safety came apart in front of witnesses — the flush, the too-quick anger reaching for someone to land on, the fear underneath it he was already trying to smother before anyone noticed it was there. He shouted something at an aide. He demanded numbers, demanded a search, demanded answers from a boy who had none beyond what he'd already given. Nobody moved with any real purpose. Everyone in that room, Taran understood watching them, had spent so long believing the western ground didn't matter that not one of them had the first idea what to actually do now that it had proven otherwise.

She stood very still herself, near the wall, exactly where she always stood, and felt something settle in her chest that she hadn't felt in three years — not hope, which she'd stopped trusting as a feeling that led anywhere, but certainty, plain and total, the kind she used to feel reading ground back in Caledonia before a fight she already knew she'd win.

Seven women. On the western ground. Waiting, watching, moving exactly when the moment offered itself and not a heartbeat before.

She didn't know their names. She didn't need to. She knew it as she'd known very few things in her life, with this little room for doubt, that whoever they were, they were the same shape of thing she was — women who had been taken and measured and put on display for what other people needed them to be, who had decided, at some point nobody else had witnessed, to stop being anyone's trophy and start being something with teeth of its own.

She had waited three years for a door to open that she could actually walk through, rather than one she'd have to break herself bloody against for nothing. This was not yet that door. But for the first time since Britannia, she could see roughly where it stood.

The Praefectus was still shouting when she slipped out unremarked. A woman always in a room eventually becomes furniture nobody watches closely; she'd been furniture long enough now to use it. She took the passage that led, two turns and a stair down, to the armory nobody checked again before morning.

22 — Between the Watchmen
↑ Back to top

Getting out of the praetorium was the easy part. She took a pair of blades, not the good ones — the good ones would be missed by morning, and she needed the household believing, for as many hours as she could buy, that nothing had happened yet.

The garrison itself was another matter. The praetorium sat at its center, walls in every direction, and center-to-wall was ground she'd only ever crossed under guard or under watching eyes, never alone, never at an hour that mattered. She knew its shape thoroughly and uselessly, a prisoner's knowledge of a courtyard, never once tested for whether it held weight.

She didn't test it that night either. Not yet.

She went to ground instead, in a gap between the granary and the wall it backed onto, a space too narrow to be worth a patrol's attention. She had marked it years before, as she marked everything, without a plan yet to spend the knowledge on. Tonight was the plan.

She waited.

The garrison took its time settling. Voices carried a while longer than she'd have liked — the Praefectus's fear hadn't burned itself out quickly, and it took the household with it, lamps staying lit in rooms that should have gone dark an hour before they did. She held still through all of it, the patience she'd spent three years telling herself she had, and let the fort talk itself out.

By the deep hours it was quiet in the particular manner of a frightened garrison — not the ordinary quiet of men asleep, but the thinner quiet of men who'd been told to watch harder and had already, within a few hours, started trusting the dark to do the watching for them. She'd seen it before, in smaller ways. Fear was a poor substitute for discipline, and it never lasted as long as the men relying on it hoped it would.

When she moved again, it was along the wall itself, keeping its shadow rather than the open ground, counting the intervals between the two men she could place on the rampart above her — not by sight, but by the rhythm of their footsteps, unhurried, bored, walking a line they'd walked a thousand nights without anything ever coming of it. That was the gift the fort's own routine gave her. Nothing had ever come of it. So nothing, tonight, was exactly what they expected to keep finding.

She found her place at the wall's lowest stretch, where old repair work had left the stone rougher than the engineers had likely intended, and went up it hand over hand, patient with every hold, the same economy of motion she'd spent three years pretending she didn't have.

She stopped short of the top and hung there, flat against the stone, and waited for the two rhythms above her to meet.

She'd chosen her spot for exactly this — the point on the rampart where the two sentries' paths crossed, the furthest either man would be from her once they turned back the other way. When their footsteps met and passed, she went up and over in the gap it bought her, flat on the walkway for less than a breath, and dropped to the outer face before either rhythm had broken.

She hit the ground at the base of the wall and went still, folded small against the stone, and did not move again until both rhythms had passed a second time, out and back, unbroken.

Then she slid along the base of the wall, keeping its shadow, until she stood opposite the stretch of forest she meant to use.

She waited once more. The footsteps met, passed, met again. On the second passing she crossed the open ground in a handful of strides and went to ground just inside the treeline, and froze there, flat against the dark, listening for anything that said she'd been wrong about any of it.

Nothing came.

She held there a long moment, Fort Lorch behind her for the first time in three years.

23 — Unfamiliar Ground
↑ Back to top

Three years she had spent reading a household most women never saw the inside of, and she had read it well enough to walk out of it clean. Tonight asked something else of her. She had never set foot on this ground. She did not know these trees, or this dark, or what waited at the end of them — only what one frightened soldier's account had told her: seven women, and four Roman dead inside a dozen heartbeats, on ground nobody had thought worth watching until it proved them wrong.

When she judged the distance enough, she turned west, and held that line a long while, counting nothing but her own steps and the black shapes of trunks passing on either side.

The sky began its slow turn from black to grey behind her as she went, the fort's shape rising out of the dark by degrees rather than all at once — which was worse, not better. A clean silhouette she could have read. This half-light gave her only edges, uncertain and shifting, and she couldn't have said with any confidence whether a shadow along the wall was stone or a man walking it. She kept moving anyway, trusting distance over certainty.

When she judged she'd gone far enough west, she turned again, south this time, the fort held deliberately to her left where she could keep half an eye on it without giving it the whole of her attention.

She hadn't gone far on the new bearing when she was stopped in her tracks by a blade at her throat.

She hadn't heard it come. Hadn't felt the air change, hadn't caught the weight of a step behind her — nothing had told her anything until the edge was already there, cold and exact, and by then telling herself anything would have been too late to matter. She went perfectly still. She didn't reach for either sword. She didn't so much as let her breath change rhythm, because whoever held that blade had earned it fair, and a woman who'd survived three years on reading rooms correctly knew better than to misread this one.

Silence answered silence.

Then a shape came out of the dark ahead of her, closing without a sound the ground itself should have made someone pay for — larger than her, carrying something she'd never seen the like of, a curved blade near the length of her own arm.

The shape spoke, though not to her.

"Tacita. Thank you."

The blade left her throat as quietly as it had arrived.

Taran didn't move. Whoever this was had not lowered her guard along with the blade — that much was plain in how she stood, weight settled, the curved weapon held loose but ready, a woman deciding something rather than a woman finished deciding.

"You came from the fort," the woman said. Not a question.

"I did."

"Alone."

"Yes."

"Why."

Taran had turned this over enough hours to have an answer ready, but the flatness of the question stripped most of the shape from it anyway. "One of the Praefectus's men came back to Lorch tonight talking about seven women and four dead men. I was in the room when he told it. I've spent three years there, taken from Caledonia, kept as something to be shown off at dinner. I didn't wait to hear it twice."

"Anyone see you leave?"

"No."

"You're certain."

"If they'd seen me, half the province would know it by now."

The woman studied her a long moment, the same flat, weighing look Taran imagined a buyer might give livestock, except there was nothing careless in it. "You're telling me you walked out from under the Praefectus's nose, climbed the fortress wall, and picked your way in the dead of night through a forest you'd never set foot in, to look for a band of killers — on the strength of a report from some deranged young upstart."

"Yes."

"That was either very brave or very stupid."

"I've had three years to learn the difference doesn't always matter. Only the outcome does."

Something shifted in the woman's face — not warmth, not yet, but the particular stillness of someone recalculating. "Tiata," she said, giving Taran the name she hadn't asked for. "You'll answer for yourself properly once we're back where the others are. Until then you walk between us, and you keep both hands where Tacita can see them."

"That's fair," Taran said.

"It isn't meant to be fair," Tiata said. "It's meant to keep you alive long enough to prove you're worth trusting. Fair comes after."

24 — The Eighth
↑ Back to top

Four of them stayed behind. Toria took the near ground, Thana beside her, both watching the fort's dark wall for anything that shouldn't be there. Tove and Thilda held the far edge, facing the trees instead — because whatever had pulled Tiata, Tacita, and Taya out into that dark could just as easily come back out of it wearing a different shape.

It was Taya who reappeared, moving low and fast between the trunks, and she didn't stop to explain herself beyond a single flat sentence, delivered without breaking stride. "Someone from Lorch. Tiata's got her. Coming in behind me."

Tiata walked the stranger in a moment later, Tacita a pace behind, and stopped her in the open ground at the center of the camp. "This is Taran," Tiata said. She looked at Taran. "Tell them your tale. See if they believe you."

Taran didn't hesitate.

"Three years ago they took me out of Caledonia. Since then I've belonged to the Praefectus at Lorch — paraded in front of his guests as proof of what Rome does to things it can't kill outright. Tonight a soldier limped home with a story about seven women and four dead men on ground the fort never bothered watching. I heard him tell it before the household. I decided this was my moment. Slipping out of the praetorium was the easy part, but I waited until just before first light before scaling the wall, so nobody in that fort knows I'm gone yet. If they did, you'd already know it."

For a moment nobody answered her at all.

Then Toria spoke, unhurried, sizing rather than accusing. "A woman doesn't cross ground like that on a soldier's rumor unless she's either got nothing left to lose, or she's very good at making people believe she doesn't."

"Both have kept me alive this long," Taran said. "Which one I am is yours to work out."

"That shouldn't be difficult," Thana said. "We seven aren't still alive through luck alone."

Tove hadn't spoken, and didn't now, but something in how she held herself changed — small, private, the look of someone who remembered exactly how it felt to be measured this way and hadn't forgotten it yet.

Tiata turned to Thilda. "No movement from the fort?"

Thilda shook her head. "Nothing yet."

"Good. But it won't be long."

The grey in the eastern sky was already beginning to give way to shades of deep purple and burnt orange.

Tiata looked at what dawn was doing to the sky, then at the eight of them gathered in the half-light — seven who'd earned their place the hard way, and one who hadn't yet, sitting apart from the rest with a blade still trained loosely somewhere near her.

Toria was already moving, passing what little they had left between hands that took it without comment. Tove checked the horses, running a hand down each foreleg in turn, the same quiet accounting she gave them every morning whether trouble was coming or not. Thana kept her eyes on the fort a while longer before she finally sat, and even then she sat facing it.

Taran ate what was passed to her without being asked twice, and said nothing else. Nobody spoke to her again, not yet, but nobody moved to bind her either, and she seemed to understand that for what it was — not trust, only the shape trust might eventually take, if the day ahead gave her the chance to earn it.

As dawn broke fully over the camp, the fort's stillness did more for Taran than anything she'd said. No ambush had come. No gate had opened, no patrol had ridden out looking for a runaway or anything else. Whatever else was unproven about her, that much of her story had now sat in front of them for an hour and refused to fall apart, and none of them had any reason left to doubt it.

Tove was the first to draw alongside her, settling down close enough to speak without carrying, and said something too quiet for the others to catch. She had her own three years to measure Taran's against — a different captor, a different sea, but the same shape of thing underneath it, and she knew better than anyone in the camp what it cost to walk free of it alone. Taran answered her, just as quiet, and for the first time since the wall, something in her shoulders came down that hadn't come down since Britannia.

25 — Not Possible
↑ Back to top

The Praefectus was clearly rattled.

He would not have named it that himself. But the aide standing in front of him, waiting on an instruction that had come out as noise rather than instruction, could see it well enough.

"Numbers," he said, slower this time. "I want to know exactly who rode out with that patrol this morning, by name, and I want it before I decide anything else is true."

The aide went.

He turned again to the lad.

"Say that again," he said. "The western ground, you say?"

The lad said it again. Nothing in the second telling was any easier to hold than the first.

He doubted it — not the lad's honesty, exactly, but his sense. A recruit six months into his service, sent out onto ground he'd never had cause to fear, could mistake a great many things for a great many other things in bad light and worse panic.

"And you say it was women."

"Seven of them."

Women didn't do that. He turned the shape of the claim over and found nowhere to set it down. Women didn't — and then, unbidden, Taran surfaced in him, the way she always did when the subject came too near her without meaning to. Taran, who had put three trained men on their backs before anyone thought to stop admiring the sport of it. Taran, who—

He looked toward the wall she usually stood against, out of nothing but old habit, and found the space empty. Gone to the kitchens, likely, or sent for water. It didn't matter. She wouldn't have gone far.

But Taran was Caledonian. Whatever the wilds out past the northern sea bred into its women, it wasn't bred into these — not into Roman women, who were subdued and quiet and knew their households, not even into the Germanic women along this frontier, tractable enough once a firm enough hand had been laid on them. Taran herself had proven it. Three years, and she stood by a wall now and said nothing unless spoken to. Even the wildest of them came to heel eventually. That was the whole of the lesson she was kept to teach.

"There were seven, you said."

"Yes."

"It couldn't be right! Not even seven women from these parts could stand against one patrol of my men. If we were in Caledonia, I might give it credence, but not Germania."

"They didn't fight my patrol so much as end it," the lad said, and something in the plainness of it — no boast in it, no shame either, just the flat exhaustion of a man repeating a thing he still didn't fully believe himself — nearly landed.

Nearly.

"And they sent you back," the Praefectus said. "Say that part again. What exactly did they tell you?"

"To walk. Not run. So I'd remember it right."

Not possible. He'd fought Caledonians who wouldn't have thought to do that — men who killed everything in reach and left nothing behind to carry a message, because a message required a kind of patience, a kind of calculation, that raiding parties simply didn't spend on the men they beat. Whoever had done this had thought about him specifically, had decided what he should be told and how he should be made to hear it, and that was a shape of cruelty he had no ready place to put either.

Simply not possible.

The gate captain's voice carried from the passage outside, and behind it the tramp of the watch being gathered in, and the Praefectus let himself follow the sound gratefully, glad of anything that wasn't this.

"Ah, here is the watch! So, this lad is of the belief that he is the sole survivor of this evening's western patrol. It's a fabrication, isn't it!"

Nobody answered him cleanly.

One man had stood a rotation two nights past and sworn the gate hinge had complained without a hand on it, and put it down at the time to wind. Another had heard nothing at all, and said so with the particular firmness of a man defending his own competence rather than reporting a fact. A third began an account of a shape at the treeline that unraveled halfway through into something he could no longer swear he'd actually seen, and stopped, red to the ears, rather than finish it. The gate captain tried twice to bring some order to the telling and twice lost it again, voices climbing over each other, each man's fragment both feeding the fear in the room and refusing to add up to anything the Praefectus could put a name to.

None of it agreed. All of it, somehow, pointed the same direction.

He felt the room slipping out from under him, and heard his own voice come out louder than he meant it to, cutting through the noise rather than commanding it.

"Enough!"

The room went quiet, but it was the wrong kind of quiet — not obedience, just startled men waiting to see what he'd do with the silence now that he had it. He didn't know, for a moment, himself.

"Whatever is out there," he said, forcing the words into something that sounded like a decision rather than a retreat, "will keep till morning. Go to your posts. All of you."

Nobody moved at once. It took a beat too long, one man glancing at another before either obeyed, and that beat told him more than anything the watch had actually said. They dispersed slowly, in a loose, murmuring confusion that hadn't been in the room when they'd arrived, and the Praefectus stood in the emptying space and understood, watching their backs, that he had just given an order nobody in that room had believed either.

26 — Vanished
↑ Back to top

The villicus was up before the household needed him to be, as he always was, and this morning more than most. A patrol gone missing on the western ground, a Praefectus who'd shouted his own watch into silence and sent them to their posts — the day ahead was going to be unusual, and an unusual day needed the ordinary parts of the house running without a crack in them. That was his use to the Praefectus, and he knew it.

He worked through the morning's accounting the way he always did — stores, staff, bread, water, the kitchen fires. Nothing out of place.

Taran should have been at her door by now.

He didn't think much of it at first. She kept to her hours more reliably than most of the men did. The whole household was off its rhythm this morning; she'd be a few minutes behind, that was all.

He went back to the stores.

A while later he checked again. No sign of her near the Praefectus's rooms. No sign of her in the kitchens either.

He went to her chamber himself.

The door gave easily — it always had; nobody had ever thought a lock necessary. The room beyond it was bare. The bed hadn't been slept in, and what few things were hers — the clothes the household had settled on her, the small effects she'd kept about her in three years — were gone. No sign of a struggle. No sign of anything having happened to her at all. Just an empty room, and nothing in it to say why.

He stood in the doorway a moment, running back through the morning for some ordinary explanation. He found none. The realization landed all at once.

He went straight for the Praefectus's chamber.

He didn't reach him. The Praefectus was already behind a half-shut door with his senior officers, arguing over what to do about the western ground — whether to hold, whether to search, whether it was worth committing real numbers to before anyone knew what they'd be committing them against. An aide took one look at the villicus and turned him away.

"Not now," the aide said. "He's occupied."

The villicus didn't argue. He went back to the stores and waited, telling himself it wouldn't be long.

It was closer to an hour before he was let in.

By then the sun was well up, and the household had moved on without him. Men were mustering in the yard — a real muster, shields checked, horses coming up from the stable fast. Whatever the officers had argued behind that door, they'd argued their way to a strike west.

He found the Praefectus in the middle of it, half into his armor, giving orders.

"Speak quickly," the Praefectus said, not looking at him. "I've a garrison to move."

The villicus told him.

The Praefectus's hands stopped on the strap he'd been buckling. He said nothing for a moment, and in that moment the villicus watched him arrive somewhere — the boy's account, still sitting half-believed since the night before, suddenly given a shape he hadn't let himself see until now. Seven women. A story he'd have sworn no woman from these parts would find the nerve for. And Taran gone from her chamber, without a mark to show how, since the very night that story had reached his own hall.

"Gone," he said. "Since when?"

"I don't know. Her chamber was empty when I found it. Nothing disturbed."

The muster stopped.

Orders that had been shouted a moment before hung unfinished. Men looked to the Praefectus for a signal that didn't come. He raised a hand, and the yard went still.

"Halt it," he said. "All of it. Nobody rides west until I know what happened inside my own walls."

He sent men to search — the praetorium, the outbuildings, the granary, the stable, the cellar. He called the night watch in a second time and made them account, one by one, for every hour of the dark.

It went no better than it had the first time.

The search found nothing. No sign of her anywhere large enough to hold a person, no mark on any wall or gate that hadn't been there for years. The night watch gave him the same answers as before, smaller this time, worn down into something closer to honesty. Nobody had seen anything. Nobody had heard anything worth swearing to. The gate hadn't opened. As far as any man on the wall could say, nobody had climbed it.

A woman gone from a fort that had lost four men in the dark the night before, and no mark left behind by either.

A patrol lost in open country was at least a loss he could point to and explain. This wasn't that. A woman he'd kept inside his own walls for three years was gone from under his very roof, and not one man in the fort could tell him how she'd gotten out.

The Praefectus stood in the yard a long while after the last man was dismissed, armor half-fastened, the muster still stalled behind him, and said nothing.

27 — The Ambush
↑ Back to top

Marcellus had known his cousin since they were boys, and he had never seen him the way he'd looked that morning — armor half-fastened, giving orders that changed shape halfway through the giving of them.

That was why Marcellus hadn't argued when the command fell to him. Someone needed to ride out with a clear head, and his cousin's wasn't one this morning.

"Take what you need," the Praefectus told him, gripping his forearm a moment too long. "Bring back the dead. Bring back the truth of it, whatever it turns out to be. I'd rather know than go on not knowing."

Marcellus said he would. He'd have gone regardless of what his cousin asked of him. It happened his cousin had asked.

He rode out with a full century, foot and horse both — more men than the errand strictly needed, and exactly as many as the uncertainty did. Half the column had heard some version of the boy's story by the time they mounted. He didn't correct any of it. Wary men made fewer mistakes than confident ones, and this morning called for fewer mistakes than most.

They found the site inside the hour. Marcellus knew it before anyone told him — the stillness that settles over ground where men have died and nobody has come yet to argue with the fact of it.

Four bodies, roughly where the boy had said. Marcellus dismounted himself rather than send a man ahead.

He knelt by the nearest of them a long while.

The wounds were clean. Not hacked at in panic — placed, each of them, and placed by more than one hand working with the same discipline. Whoever had done this hadn't been afraid while they did it.

"Recover them," he said, straightening. "Gently. They rode out as my men. They come home as my men."

The century went to the work of it. Horses stood. Weapons went, for a while, out of most men's hands.

He walked the ground himself while they worked — drag marks half-swallowed by grass nobody had disturbed further, churned earth where more than four had clearly stood and fought, and then nothing. No trail leading off in any one direction. Whoever had done this had come from everywhere and gone back to it the same way.

A shout went up from the treeline to the north. Not panic yet — just a man's voice pitched to say *something's here.*

Marcellus was moving before he'd decided to move, four infantrymen falling in around him. "Hold the recovery," he called back to his optio. "Cavalry stays with the dead. I'll see what that is."

He didn't get thirty paces into the trees.

The man leading his file went down without a sound — a short, choked exhale, already falling before Marcellus had registered what he was seeing. An arrow buried to the fletching in the side of his throat.

"Down! Cover!" His own sword was clear before the second man's shout finished, four infantrymen scattering for whatever the ground offered, eyes on trees that gave nothing back.

Nothing came. No second shot found any of them.

From the south — too far to be the same hand, too close to be chance — a second shout went up, and behind it the sound of another man falling wrong.

Marcellus understood, before the sound had fully reached him, that this wasn't one threat in one place. His century was already coming apart at seams he hadn't accounted for.

He turned back toward the horses. Toward the dead. Toward the one part of the morning he'd trusted enough to leave lightly guarded.

He didn't reach them in time to matter.

"Cavalry, mount!" he shouted, already running for his own horse, the recovery abandoned where it stood. Two threats, maybe more, in ground his men didn't know — the only answer left was to get the century moving as one body instead of scattered pieces, and that started with getting every rider up.

Men were already swinging into saddles around him, horses shifting, unsettled by the shouting and the smell of blood already thick from the four dead being loaded. Marcellus reached his own mount and had one hand on the pommel, one foot already leaving the ground, when the horse reared under him.

It went up hard and sudden, no warning in it, no cause he could see — and he was thrown back off it before he'd gotten his weight over the saddle, hitting the ground with the wind knocked clean out of him.

He didn't see the dart. He only understood, lying on his back with the sky swimming and his own horse screaming somewhere above him, that whatever had put it there hadn't been aiming for him at all.

Then the rest of it came down at once.

A woman broke from the treeline to his left, a spear already leveled, and put it into the chest of the nearest mount before the rider had finished cursing his own horse's panic. Another came from the right with an axe, short and heavy, swinging low and taking a second horse's legs out from under it in a single stroke — the animal screaming, going down sideways, the rider caught half under it.

Behind him, at the rear of the line, two more shapes moved through the tangle with blades already working, and Marcellus, still fighting to get air back into his lungs, understood with a cold, distant clarity that this hadn't been aimed at his men at all. It was aimed at the horses. Every one of them.

He rolled, got a hand under himself, and looked up in time to see her.

She came through the chaos without hurrying, a curved blade in one hand, and went straight for his own horse where it still reared and screamed above him — one swing, low and hard, and the animal's foreleg buckled under it and it went down in a heap that missed him by less than an arm's length. She didn't stop to watch it fall. She was already moving to the next.

Around him the whole cavalry line was coming apart into exactly the kind of ruin he'd have called impossible an hour before — horses down, horses bolting into each other, riders thrown or trapped or simply gone, the ordered shape of a century dissolving into noise and blood and animals dying in ways no discipline could answer.

He tried to get to his feet. He didn't get far.

A hand closed over his shoulder from behind, and a blade found his throat before he'd finished turning into whoever held it — close, controlled, no wasted motion in it at all. He went still. Whoever this was had done this before, and knew exactly how much pressure it took to make a man understand that stillness was the only thing keeping him alive.

"Don't," a voice said, low, near his ear. Not shouted. It didn't need to be.

He didn't.

A second woman appeared at his other side, matching pace as he was hauled to his feet and moved, half-walking and half-dragged, away from the wreckage of his own command. She gripped his arm with a power he would never have expected from a woman, and a faint smirk lifted one corner of her mouth.

"You shaved well this morning, centurion," she said, close to his ear. "Don't make us do it again now."

The blade against his throat remained as steady as a plumb line.

Around him, the screaming of horses and men swallowed whatever sound he might have made if he'd been fool enough to make one.

He caught fragments of it as they moved him — a third shape, an axe, a fourth with a spear, all of it already withdrawing as quickly as it had come, the whole assault folding itself back into the trees the way it had come out of them, leaving behind a heaving, dying mass of horseflesh and men too disoriented to know yet what had actually happened to them.

Nobody was looking at him. That was the thing he understood last and worst of all, dragged backward into cover with a blade still resting light against his skin — in all that noise and blood, not one man of his own century so much as glanced his way to see that their commander was being taken.

28 — Captive Ignored
↑ Back to top

Tacita and Thana walked the centurion in, blade still at his throat, to the edge of the clearing where the firelight thinned into shadow — close enough to hear every word said around that fire, too far from it to feel any of its warmth.

They sat him against a lone birch just outside the light and bound his wrists behind it, then his ankles, and walked away, Thana casting a single sneer back in his direction before completely ignoring him.

Taran's eyes stayed on him a moment longer than anyone else's did, then dropped to her own hands, and she said nothing either.

Tove had built the fire up higher than usual, feeding it with the particular generosity she brought to good nights, and there was meat again, enough of it, passed hand to hand, nobody keeping more than their share. Thilda sat close to the flame with her spear across her knees, unhurriedly working a nick out of the blade with a whetstone. Thana stretched her legs toward the fire and let her head fall back, eyes half-closed, as if she'd done nothing more strenuous that day than walk somewhere pleasant.

"Four horses down before any of them understood what was happening," Toria said. "I've never seen a line come apart that fast."

"They never had a chance to hold anything," Taya said, not looking up from the arrow in her hands. "Not once they split their own line chasing two shouts that weren't where the real danger was. By the time they went back for the horses, half the century was still in the trees looking the wrong way."

Tiata sat where she always sat, a little apart, the falx laid flat across her knees, and let the noise of the fire run without steering it. She'd already heard the whole of it once, walking back — every angle, every kill, exactly how the ambush had folded itself back into the trees, exactly as planned. She didn't need it told to her twice. She let them tell it to each other instead, which Taran was coming to understand was its own kind of gift, the kind Tiata gave without seeming to think of it as one.

It was Thana who turned the talk toward her.

"And our newest terror," Thana said, tipping her chin at Taran, the old sharpness in it gentled down into something that was almost fond. "South side, drawing off half their foot before any of them knew there was a fight. First real work with us, and she puts an arrow through a man's throat before he's gone ten paces into the trees. I'd call that a fair morning's introduction."

"I had the angle Taya gave me," Taran said. It came out quicker than she meant it to, the words crowding each other, still, when something mattered to her — three years of stillness hadn't worn that out of her, only taught her when to let it run and when to leash it. "She read the ground. I only had to be where she said."

"You only had to hold and not flinch," Taya said. "Most people flinch, the first time it's real and not practice. You didn't."

Tove reached over and knocked a shoulder against hers, warm and solid. "First time with us, and already better than half the recruits I've ever seen a Roman drillmaster claim credit for."

"I've held a bow longer than I've held anything else," Taran said. She turned the yew stave over in her lap, the wood worn smooth in places that weren't worn smooth by three years of sitting idle on a wall. "This very one. Just not for three years."

Even Tiata leaned in, just slightly.

"I went in for the blades," Taran said. "That was the whole of it. I never knew what he'd done with the bow — never let myself wonder, these three years."

She paused.

"It was hanging above his own armory rack. Something for his guests to be shown and told about — the wild woman's bow, same as the wild woman herself." Her jaw set, hard, before she went on. "I never knew he'd kept it. Not until I saw it hanging there."

She ran a thumb along the grain, slow, confirming something rather than merely touching it.

"I took it down with my own hands." One corner of her mouth lifted, not quite a smile. "It still remembered my draw."

Tove's hand found her shoulder again, no words in it this time, just weight, just presence.

"Then it's yours again. The wall only borrowed it a while," Tiata said, at last, flat and certain, and nothing more.

The fire cracked. Somewhere past its light, unremarked, unaddressed, a man sat with his wrists bound and his back straight and every word of it landing on him whether he wanted it to or not.

Nobody looked his way.

Thilda finished with the whetstone and laid the spear aside, testing the edge once against her thumb before she seemed satisfied. "First watch," she said, to no one in particular and everyone at once, already rising. "I'll take it."

"I'll spell you at the turn," Taya said, and that was that — no discussion needed, the shape of the night already understood by all of them.

They banked the fire lower as the talk wore down into the easy, unhurried quiet that followed a good night rather than a hard one. Tove passed the last of the meat around a second time. Toria stretched out on her back with her hands behind her head and watched the smoke go up into a sky just beginning to show stars. Tacita fed the fire once, without being asked, and said nothing at all.

Taran sat with the bow across her knees a long while after the talk had moved past her, running her thumb along the same stretch of grain, over and over, returning to it as if it might have stopped being true.

Past the light, the man they'd left there hadn't moved. No water had been brought to him. No blanket, against the cold that would come once the fire burned all the way down.

29 — Watching
↑ Back to top

The Praefectus stood at the tower rail, the watchman beside him, and had not moved from it in some time.

He had told himself, going up, that a commander belonged where he could see the ground his men were on. Below, in the yard, were the aide, the gate captain, the men he'd sent to search a chamber and found nothing in it. Up here there was only the west, and the watch beside him, and the wood rail under both his hands.

The sun had gone low enough now to sit in his eyes, the sky past it turning toward dusk. He had been up there since midafternoon, watching the same stretch of trees for hours with nothing in them worth the watching.

"Sir."

The watchman went still, eyes fixed west. A moment passed before his hand came up, flat, angled along the tree line where the light was worst.

The Praefectus followed it and saw nothing. Trees. The long grey smear where the forest gave way to the cleared ground before the walls. Nothing that hadn't been there an hour ago.

"There," the watchman said. "Coming out of the tree line. Low ground, past the old ford marker."

He looked again, and this time something in the grey resolved itself into shape — not one shape, several, small with distance and made smaller by the failing light, moving in a loose scatter rather than any order a century should have kept. No line to it. No column. Just men, or the suggestion of men, coming on foot.

Foot.

A century that had ridden out with horse and foot both did not come home on foot. Not all of it.

"How many," he said. His own voice came out thinner than he meant it to.

"Can't say yet, sir. Too far. Could be a dozen. Could be less."

His hands closed harder on the timber rail. The line came on slow and uneven, some of the shapes leaning on others, like something being poured out of the trees.

"Send down," he said. "Gate captain. Torches on the wall. I want every man who can carry a light on that approach."

The watchman went. The Praefectus stayed.

He counted, and lost count, and started again, because the light kept taking shapes away from him just as he thought he'd fixed a number to them. Ten. Perhaps twelve. He could not, from this height, in this light, tell wounded from whole, could not tell whether the men leaning on the men beside them were being helped or being carried, could not see, anywhere in that broken line, a horse.

Not one.

He had sent out a full century. Cavalry and foot both, more than the errand needed. He had sent his own cousin at the head of it, had gripped his forearm at the gate and asked him to bring back the dead and the truth of it.

Torches came alive along the wall below him, one after another, pushing the dusk back by degrees, and the approaching line resolved further in their reach — men in Roman kit, unmistakably that much at least, dust and worse on most of them, no horse among them, no banner held up the way a century's banner should have been held even in retreat. He watched for the particular set of shoulders he would have known anywhere, the way his cousin carried himself even tired, even hurt, and did not find it.

He told himself the light was still too poor to be sure of anything.

The gate groaned open below him, and the column — if it could still be called that — began to come through it, one at a time, into the ring of torchlight, where the men who had stayed behind could finally see what had gone out that morning and what had come back of it.

The Praefectus did not go down to meet them yet. He stood at the rail a moment longer, watching from above, and made himself look at every face the torches found.

His cousin's face was not among them.

30 — Marcellus Is Missing
↑ Back to top

The Praefectus went down from the tower himself, in the end. A commander who watched from above while his own men stumbled home under torchlight was no commander at all, whatever his legs wanted to do instead, and so he made them carry him down the stair and out into the yard, where the gate stood open and the wreckage of a century was still finding its way through it.

It was not a column. He understood that the moment he was close enough for the word to matter. A column had shape, discipline, a front and a rear a man could point to. This had none of it — men coming through the gate in no order, at no interval, some upright and some held upright by whoever happened to be walking beside them, and none of them looking at him as they came, which told him more than their faces could have.

He counted without meaning to. He lost the count twice, started it a third time, and made himself stop, because a number wasn't going to be the thing that told him what he needed to know.

"Form them," he said, to the gate captain, and heard how thin it came out even as he said it. "Get water into them. Then bring me whoever can still speak plainly."

Plainly was the wrong word to have asked for. He understood that within the first quarter hour.

A man near the gate, blood dried black down one side of his face from a wound he didn't seem to know he had, told it start to finish twice in a row, and the two tellings didn't agree with each other on where the first shout had come from. Another, further in, hadn't heard any shout at all — he'd been at the horse lines the whole time, he said, and the horses had simply started dying around him, one after another, no cause he could name, as if something in the ground itself had turned against them. A third began an account of the centurion going down and stopped halfway through it, unable to say with any certainty whether he'd seen Marcellus fall or only remembered, afterward, believing that he had.

None of it agreed. The Praefectus had heard that particular kind of disagreement before, two nights running now, and it no longer offered him even the comfort of surprise.

He went from man to man himself rather than wait for the reports to be gathered and smoothed into something usable, because he had learned, watching his own watch fail to agree with itself over a mere shape at a treeline, that smoothing was exactly what he could not afford tonight. He wanted the pieces raw. He would do the fitting of them himself.

Slowly, unevenly, out of order, a shape began to come together that he did not want.

The recovery had been underway — four dead, his cousin's own hand on the work of bringing them in gently, as he'd asked. Then a shout from the north, and a man down before he'd gone ten paces past the tree line, an arrow in his throat from somewhere none of them had marked. Then, before that shock had finished landing, a second shout from the south — too far to be the same hand, too close to be accident, and it was that detail, more than any other, that settled cold in the Praefectus's stomach as he heard it repeated by three separate men who hadn't spoken to each other since. Two threats, deliberately placed, deliberately timed. Not a raiding party stumbling onto Roman soldiers. Something that had known exactly where his men would be standing before his men had known it themselves.

And then the horses.

It was clear from the accounts that the cavalrymen had not been the target of the attack. It was the horses that had been targeted, which was a tactic he could not fathom. A blade to the leg here, a spear into the chest there, one animal after another put down where it stood, its rider thrown or trapped beneath it rather than cut down where he sat. He counted the wounded men off against the empty picket line and understood, before anyone told him so directly, that whoever had done this had not been trying to end his century. They had been trying to strand it.

Nobody had gone looking for anybody in the middle of it. That much was plain in how every man told it. A heaving mass of screaming horseflesh and struggling cavalrymen, men fighting to get clear of animals dying under and around them, and not one of them sparing a look for who stood where. It was only back inside his own gate, torches up, the wounded being counted off, that anyone had thought to ask where the centurion was — and found no answer waiting for them.

Every man in front of him assumed Marcellus was dead. Not one of them could say so.

He stood in the middle of his own yard, torches guttering low, the last of the wounded being walked off toward whatever the fort could give them.

"At first light," he said, and this time his voice did not come out thin. "A party goes out. Full strength, foot and horse both — what horse we have left. We bring back our dead. All of them. And we do not come back inside these walls until we know that every man is accounted for."

Everyone present knew exactly who the Praefectus was wanting to find.

31 — Cold Captive
↑ Back to top

Taya caught the movement at first light.

Torches still lit at the gate, more of them than a normal muster needed. She watched long enough to count — horse and foot both, more men than yesterday's, nobody riding out ahead of the rest — then went back for Tiata and Thilda without waking anyone else.

"They're moving again," she said. "Same ground as yesterday, by the look of the column. More men this time."

Tiata was on her feet before the sentence finished. "How many more."

"Enough that they're not expecting an easy morning."

They went together, low along the same ridge Taya had used the night before, and settled where the trees still held dark.

The column came on slower than yesterday's had. Men rode close together who had no reason to, foot soldiers keeping pace with the horses rather than falling into their own line, eyes going to the treeline every dozen paces whether anything moved in it or not.

"They think we're still out here," Thilda said, low.

"We are still out here," Tiata said.

The recovery went slowly, and badly, every man working with one hand on his weapon. A shout went up once — nothing, a man startled by his own shadow — and the whole column went still for ten breaths before anyone moved again.

"They're jumping at nothing," Taya murmured, some satisfaction in it.

"That's the point of nothing," Tiata said. "Yesterday cost them a centurion and however many of that column they're still counting, in less time than it takes to load a cart. Today they don't know if it happens again, or when, or from where."

They watched the whole of it — the wounded horses put down where they lay, the dead loaded with more speed than care, a count taken and taken again, men calling names back and forth across the field.

The names went round twice. One went unanswered both times. Marcellus's own optio said it a third time, alone, to nobody.

They watched the column go, slower going back than it had come, and turned for camp once the gate had swallowed the last of it.

By the time they were back, the morning at their own fire had already moved on without them.

Marcellus looked like he hadn't slept at all.

Tiata went to him first, before she'd said a word to anyone else, and crouched down to cut the binding at his wrists and ankles.

"The armor comes off," she said. "The tunic under it, the greaves, the braccae, the boots, the cloak. All of it."

"I'm a Roman officer—"

"You were," Tiata said, and waited.

He gave it up piece by piece, hands shaking, and nobody helped him. When it was done there was a pile at his feet, and a man kneeling on the ground in an under-tunic, the cold coming in off the east.

Thana looked him over once, head to foot.

"The little man looks cold," she said, and went back to what she'd been doing.

Tove dropped a bundle of grey wool on the ground a pace from his knees. "That's the choice," she said. "Wear it, or don't. I don't care either way. You might."

He looked at it. A peasant girl's dress, plain, worn soft at the seams by someone else's years in it. He looked away.

"I'll manage," he said.

Thana gave a snort. "That's what he thinks now."

They rebound his bare ankles, and bound his wrists in front of him this time, loose enough to let him work at the knot if he wanted to. He was left at the birch while the camp went on exactly as it would have without him in it.

Taran strung the bow herself that morning, unhurried, turning it in the light to check the tiller, and slung it across her back when she was satisfied. She sat when she wanted to sit and stood when she wanted to stand. She laughed at something Tove said. She crossed in front of him a dozen times through the morning, each time ignoring him, just as he had ignored her in the Praefectus's house.

By midday the cold was a fact he couldn't ignore.

"The Praefectus's silver boy is beginning to tarnish," Thana said, passing with an armful of wood, not slowing. "He's going purple around the edges."

He was shivering uncontrollably now, but said nothing.

The light went early under the trees. He had told himself, through the morning, that a Roman officer did not dress himself as a peasant woman to spare his own comfort. He believed it until he was shivering so hard his teeth were chattering.

"I'll take it," he stammered, when Tove came near enough.

Tove nodded once and picked the wool up off the ground. "Can't have you catching cold on top of everything else, can we now."

"Taran," she called. "Help me dress the sweet lad, if you don't mind."

His wrists were still bound, so Taran returned with a blade to cut the binding. A blade Marcellus recognised as belonging to his cousin.

"Arms in the air," said Taran, as she slid the dress down over his head.

"There we are," said Tove. "That's much better, isn't it." And she chucked him under the chin.

He had watched Taran's hands the whole time. He knew them. He'd watched them stand empty at a wall for three years, offered a practice blade only when his cousin wanted something proven to a room full of guests. He had watched them now, deftly handling a blade, and working coarse wool down over his shoulders, and said nothing.

"Better tie it to the birch again," called Thana, from the warmth of the fireside. "It'll shiver itself out of its sack, otherwise."

Tove helped Taran rebind his wrists behind the birch, and they both turned away to the fireside.

Nobody looked his way again that night.

32 — Phenomenon
↑ Back to top

The party had ridden out at first light. The Praefectus stayed in the yard, near the gate, through the whole of the morning.

Nothing happened. No rider came back early with word of trouble.

They came back sooner than he'd expected. He was still telling himself it was too early when the watch on the wall called them in.

Four rode. The rest walked beside litters, ten of them, canvas drawn over what they carried.

He counted the litters before he counted the men. Ten.

He went for the nearest rider before the horse had stopped.

"Well?" he said. "Well?"

The rider was young. It took him a moment to understand he was the one being asked.

"Sir, we recovered six more of the fallen, as well as the four fallen the previous evening. The ground was—"

"Is he among them."

"No, sir."

"Then where."

"We don't know, sir. We searched as far as—"

"You searched." Men near the gate stopped what they were doing. "You searched and you come back and tell me you don't know."

"Sir, every man who rode out yesterday is accounted for now. Six dead. Fifty-four returned. Centurion Marcellus is the only one who isn't."

The only one.

He had sent his men to bring back proof. What they'd brought back was that there was none to bring.

"Then go out again," he said. "Now. Every man who can still sit a horse."

"Sir, the light—"

"I did not ask you the hour." He had hold of the rider's arm before he knew he'd reached for it. "You will turn this fort inside out before you tell me a man of mine isn't anywhere. Men don't simply—"

"Praefectus."

Rufinus had come up behind him, close, not touching him yet.

"Let him go, sir. He's told you what there is."

"There is more. There has to be more."

"There isn't. Not today. Not from him."

He looked down and found his hand still closed on the rider's arm, and opened it. The rider stepped back and didn't look at him again.

The yard had gone quiet. The litters kept coming through. A horse shifted somewhere behind him and blew out through its nose.

"Sir," Rufinus said. "Not here."

"I am not finished—"

"You are finished here."

Draco took the other arm, and between the two of them he was moving. None of the returning men would look at him.

They took him into the record room off the praetorium and shut the door and didn't let go until he'd stopped trying to go back out.

"They saw that," he said, once he could say anything.

"They saw a man who's lost more in two days than most commanders lose in a year," Draco said. "It will pass."

"It will not pass. Four men. Then a woman gone from my own house without a trace. Then a full century sent out and come back in pieces. Now this. My cousin. Gone the same way she went. Not dead. Not found. Just not there."

Neither centurion had an answer to this.

"You need to send word up the line," Rufinus said. "Tonight. Before it's a week old and looks like something you sat on."

"And say what." His voice came out flat. "Say that seven women ended a patrol and left a witness on purpose. Say that a woman kept three years under guard walked out of a secure fort leaving no trace. Say a centurion, my own cousin, vanished off a battlefield, right under the noses of his own century. Say that the same century returned in total disarray, having been shattered by the same hands - by seven women. Say that the entire cavalry division of that century returned on foot, having had - every man of them - his steed cut down beneath him. And I have no consistent account of any of it because not one man among them saw it clearly enought to give account." He looked at them both. "Tell me which part of that I write down and which part I leave out, so whoever reads it doesn't decide I've lost the use of my own mind."

"Write what happened," Draco said. "Not what it means. Numbers. Names. What was seen, and by how many. Let them draw their own conclusions."

"And if their conclusion is that I imagined it?"

"Then it's their error, not yours," Rufinus said. "But you don't get to decide that by staying silent instead."

The Praefectus sat a long while at the table without touching the wax. Twice he started a line and stopped, reading back what he'd written.

In the end, he wrote it plain. Numbers. Names. What was seen, and by how many. He left out the word phenomenon, though it was the only word that could rightly describe what was happening. Written down, addressed to a man who'd never stood in his yard, it would stop being his own and become the thing they used to explain him away.

The centurions despatched it before he could read it a third time.

33 — Nobody There
↑ Back to top

Six days, and no patrol had crossed the western ground.

Taya reported it each evening, back from whatever ridge had held her that day. North, twice. East, three times, once close enough to hear the horses. South, once, a foraging party more than a patrol, gone before it had covered any real distance. West, nothing. Not a boot print laid fresh over the old ones. Not a rider sent to test what had swallowed a centurion.

"They've stopped believing that ground is theirs," Tiata said, the third evening, and nobody argued it.

They made sure the fort didn't forget why.

An arrow went into the gatepost on the fourth night, close enough to the watchman's shoulder to take the color out of his face, gone before anyone could name a direction to answer it in. On the fifth, a shape stood at the treeline in full daylight long enough for three separate men on the wall to swear to it, and was gone by the time any of them had found the nerve to point.

By then Marcellus had stopped watching much of anything.

*

The first day, he'd flinched at every shadow that crossed near the birch, tracked every pair of boots that passed within reach, tested the give in the tether at his ankles when he thought no one was looking.

By the fourth, he'd stopped testing it. It held. It had always held.

He ate what was left in front of him — bread, a strip of dried meat, whatever the fire had produced that night — without looking up to see whose hand had set it down. He drank when the waterskin appeared near his knee and didn't wonder anymore who'd carried it. His wrists had been unbound since the second day. He'd stopped raising them to check.

Toria passed within a foot of him twice a day, on her way to the horses, and he'd stopped turning his head to follow her.

Thana had stopped needling him somewhere around the fifth night, not out of any mercy, but because a man who no longer flinched wasn't worth the breath it cost to bait him.

He answered Tiata once, on the sixth morning, when she crouched in front of him and asked, flatly, whether he was still capable of hearing her. He said something. None of them could afterward agree on what it had been, only that it hadn't been a sentence a Roman officer would have recognized as his own.

He had stopped being watched, in the sense that mattered to a man. He had simply become a fixture of the camp — tethered, fed, and otherwise unremarked.

*

Taya caught the muster leaving the fort before it had cleared the gate properly, and was back at camp with the word before the column had covered a third of the ground between.

"They're coming," she said. "Full strength again, and the Praefectus riding at the head of it this time. Not a patrol. A rescue."

No one scrambled. There was nothing to scramble for — the horses stayed tethered exactly where they stood, saddled and waiting, because a camp stripped down to nothing said the same thing a fled one would. This one needed to say the opposite: that whoever lived here hadn't gone anywhere.

They went to ground at the treeline in good order and watched the column close the distance. Tacita stayed last, at the birch, and didn't move until the column was near enough that a man at its front could have named the color of the leaves behind her.

She cut the tether at Marcellus's ankles with one stroke, unhurried even then, and was gone into the foliage before the sound of it had finished reaching him.

*

Draco had argued against the Praefectus riding out himself, the night before, and lost. Rufinus hadn't bothered arguing at all, only asked how many men he wanted at his own back, and set about finding that many.

They went in with more caution than the strike that had cost them a century — smaller numbers, slower pace, scouts thrown well ahead and well wide, every man of them watching the trees without needing to be told why. None of them expected an easy day. Many of them, by the look on their faces, expected not to come back to the fort alive.

They found the camp by midafternoon, close by the ford on the fort's blind side — a fire still smoking where it had been kicked over rather than banked, bedding laid out around it in a rough circle, tack and gear stacked with the particular order of people who'd meant, until a few minutes before, to come back to it. Horses stood tethered at the treeline's edge, saddled, waiting on riders who plainly hadn't gone far.

A shape lay near the birch, half-curled on the ground rather than sitting against it. The grey wool dress Tove had thrown at his feet days before hung off one shoulder now. No rope on the wrists. No rope at the ankles. Nothing was holding him there at all.

The Praefectus was first to the clearing, ahead of his own scouts, and first to the shape on the ground. He went down on both knees beside it and turned the face up out of the dirt, and knew him before he'd finished the motion — the same man whose forearm he'd gripped at his own gate, the same Marcellus he'd grown up alongside.

Marcellus's eyes found the Praefectus's and held there a moment, flat and still — no relief in them, no fear, no recognition of the man who'd raised half a fort to come and find him. He said nothing. He made no sound at all when the Praefectus said his name, once, then again, closer.

No one had stopped him from being found.

Draco bent down and took the Praefectus by the arm. "Come, sir."

He rose quietly, and the men behind him stooped and lifted Marcellus up between them and carried him out of the clearing.

*

Nothing came at them.

That was the part that undid them more than the fire pit or the bedding or the man who no longer answered to his own name.

Every man in that clearing had spent the walk in braced for an arrow out of the trees, a dart from somewhere unseen, a blade rising out of ground that had already proven it could hide exactly that. They had come ready to fight for him, to buy him back at whatever price was asked, to beg if begging was what it took. None of it had been required. The camp had simply opened itself and let them walk in and take what they'd come for.

The gear was real. The horses were real, tethered and saddled as if their riders had only just stepped away from them. The fire was still catching air where it had been kicked apart, not yet gone to embers. Whoever had lived here hadn't gone far, and hadn't gone long ago. And not one soul stood anywhere in sight to account for any of it.

They didn't linger to search the treeline. They left the way they'd come, faster than caution strictly allowed, every man among them certain — without one shred of anything to prove it — that eight pairs of eyes were counting them out of that clearing the same way they'd been counted in.

Nothing made a sound. Nothing moved. Nothing.

34 — Rome on Its Head
↑ Back to top

They had put three ridges between themselves and the fort by dusk, and even Thilda had allowed it was enough.

It was Tove who spoke first.

"I keep thinking about the gate," she said, turning a piece of kindling in her hands without feeding it to the fire yet. "Not tonight's gate. Tomorrow's. The next watchman who has to stand it, knowing what stood outside it last week and walked away without anyone catching a glimpse."

"He won't sleep," Thana said. "None of them will. We didn't have to kill the whole garrison to ruin their sleep for the season — we only had to send one man back to remind the rest what waiting for us feels like." She stretched toward the fire. "I've fought plenty who feared dying. This is the first time I've built one that fears living."

Taya nodded, not looking up from the arrow she was fletching. "A man afraid of dying is done costing you anything the moment he's dead. A man afraid of living carries it into every room he sits in, for as long as he lasts. That's worth more to us than half a garrison dead."

Thana inclined her head. "I won't argue that."

Toria had been quiet longer than usual. "I keep thinking about tonight's report," she said. "The one the Praefectus has to write now, once his hand stops shaking enough to hold the stylus." She looked into the fire rather than at any of them. "What does a man like that even say. *I lost a patrol. I lost a woman I kept three years and never once thought could leave. I lost a century, and my own cousin's mind besides, and none of it to an enemy I can name to a superior without sounding mad.* I'd almost pity the wax."

"Almost," Tiata said, and left it there.

"It won't stay at Lorch," Thilda said. "Whatever he writes goes up the line to someone who's never smelled that fort's air. That man reads numbers he can't square and a shape he doesn't like, and he has two choices. Believe it, and admit the frontier's rotting somewhere nobody budgeted for. Or doubt it, and hope the next report proves him right." She fed the fire. "Either way, he won't sleep well either. Fear moves up a chain slower than it moves down one. But it moves."

"That's the part I like best," Tove said, leaning forward. "Not just Lorch. Whoever sits above the man who sits above the Praefectus. Some official who's never thought about a frontier fort by name, holding a dispatch that says eight women undid a century and walked a Roman officer home in a dress, and nobody can properly say how it was done." She shook her head. "That's not a bad week for one Praefectus anymore. That's the whole of Rome on its head."

"You're imagining a very large collapse from one very small dress," Thana said.

"Am I. Rome's whole trick is convincing everyone that who stands over whom can't ever change. Man over woman. Captor over captive. Citizen over everyone who isn't." Tove nodded toward Taran. "Dress one man exactly the way he'd have dressed one of us, tether him exactly the way he tethered her, and you haven't just humiliated a centurion. You've shown every man who believed in that order that it isn't fixed. It only looked fixed because nobody had the nerve to turn it around on them yet."

Taran hadn't spoken. She sat apart from the fire's full light, the bow across her knees, thumb moving along the grain.

"I only care what it's done to a brute who used to hand me a practice blade and call it generous," she said, finally, low. Her jaw worked once. "Three years, he stood there and let his cousin show me off like a hound that had learned to heel. Now half a garrison lies awake wondering if the thing they can't see is standing at their own door." A thin smile, no warmth in it. "Let the empire crack if it wants to. I only wanted the one house to know it wasn't as safe as it thought."

Nobody pushed her past that. Tove's hand found her shoulder, but no words were exchanged.

Taya let the quiet hold a moment. "I'd be careful how satisfied we let ourselves get, all the same. A frightened system doesn't only despair. Sometimes it hunts harder, because it can't afford to be seen doubting itself. The bigger the crack we imagine we've made, the more men Rome will decide it can spend proving the crack isn't there." She set the finished arrow down. "Tonight, we've earned the fire. Tomorrow, we should assume they're already deciding how much they're willing to lose finding out we exist."

"They already know we exist," Toria said.

"They know a story exists," Taya said. "There's a difference, and it's the difference that's kept us alive this long. Let's not spend it early because it felt good to imagine the size of what we'd done."

Tiata had let the whole of it run without steering it. She added her weight now, once.

"All of that is true, and exactly how we meant it to be," Tiata said. She let the falx settle across her knees, in no hurry to say the rest. "But Rome doesn't come apart because of one dead century, or one broken man, or one report a Praefectus can't bring himself to write plainly. It comes apart from years of exactly that, done over and over, by people it never thought worth watching. Tonight was one weight added to the pile. There'll be others before we're finished." She looked around the fire, unhurried, and nobody argued with any of it. "Toria said as much, the first morning I rode with any of you. Not to beat Rome in open ground. To make the men running it start doubting the ground they're already standing on."

Nobody argued that either.

Tacita hadn't spoken. She fed the fire, reached over once and set her hand against Taran's arm, and went back to the fire.

Nobody needed her to say more than that.
