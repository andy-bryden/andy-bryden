Chapter 36. — The Benefikee-ar-reeus.
 Lig-arius had been benefikee-ar-reeus for nine years, on the governor's staff at Mohgoon-tee-ar-koom. His work fell under no centurion. He answered directly to the governor's office, and took whatever posting they gave him. Some months he had none at all. When a report needed checking in person, he went and checked it, and wrote down what he found. He had done this work in three provinces before Ghermarnea Superior, and had been recalled to testify at two courts-martial on the strength of his reports.
 Most of his postings were unremarkable. He had settled a dispute over a quartermaster's numbers, and once found that a death on the road blamed on bandits was a quarrel between two soldiers. Generally, that was about as far as his role took him.
 Priscus came to his table one morning in early autumn, two sheets of papyrus in hand.
 "Lorkh," he said. "You'll want to read both of these before I explain any of it."
 Lig-arius read the first, then the second, taking his time over both.
 "What else do you know?" Lig-arius asked.
 "The pryfektus there has held the post three years, and served on the Caledonian frontier before it," Priscus said. "He's no novice. The tribune thought it was grief. I let it go on that understanding, the first time. I'm not willing to let it go a second."
 "What changed?"
 "The second dispatch." Priscus leaned over and tapped the sheet in Lig-arius's hand. "A found man should be a relief. Instead he's a fresh problem. He can't explain the six days nobody moved, the camp still warm with nobody in it, or what's wrong with the man he found." He paused. "A man losing his composure over a death, I understand. Losing it over a rescue is different."
 "You want me to go."
 "I want someone at Lorkh who isn't the pryfektus, asking questions the pryfektus doesn't seem to be able to answer."
 Lig-arius took the dispatches to carry with him. A cornicularius had no authority to send a benefikee-ar-reeus out on a case, and both men knew it. Neither raised the point. The report needed to go further up the chain, whether or not Priscus had the authority to send it there himself.
 He left within the week.
 The road southeast ran well for the first day, then narrowed and roughened as it left Rome's settled country behind. Lig-arius rode alone, a single packhorse behind him, and crossed in four days without incident.
 Lorkh didn't look like a fort under siege. The walls stood intact. The gate was manned. Nothing about it explained two ambushes, a missing woman, and a centurion out of his mind.
 It was the men who told him something was wrong before he'd exchanged a word with any of them.
 The sentry who took his name at the gate skipped the routine questions — travelling alone, what business, how long he meant to stay. He waved him through and kept his eyes on the tree line west of the fort.
 Inside, nobody crossed near the western wall without glancing at it first. Two soldiers stood talking near the granary, and their talk stopped the moment they noticed him.
 The pryfektus received him immediately and didn't try to hide his relief. Most men in his position treated an investigator's arrival as an accusation before a word had passed between them.
 "You've read the dispatches," the pryfektus said.
 "Both."
 "Then you already know most of it." He said it without shame. "Ask what you need. I've nothing left to hide."
