Chapter 38. — Seven and Eight.
 Lig-arius spent the first day on testimony, which included spending time with Markellus himself.
 He had asked to see the centurion and was taken to him — a man sitting upright, watched over, silent, eyes open and fixed on nothing. The physician had nothing to add beyond what the second dispatch had already said, but Lig-arius was certain he could see a spark of knowledge in the otherwise vacant stare. He asked if he could be alone with the centurion, and the guard withdrew to the outer chamber, closing the door behind him.
 Lig-arius sat opposite Markellus for a time, saying nothing. He watched his eyes, and he could tell Markellus was not entirely catatonic, yet there was something strange about his stare. He tried to reason with him, asking what he remembered of the ambush. Markellus lifted his gaze momentarily, then his eyes dropped again.
 He asked him what he remembered of the six days in the camp of the attackers. For a moment, he thought he had broken through. He was sure he saw Markellus forming the word "attackers" with his lips, but there was nothing more.
 Lig-arius decided to change tack. He stood over Markellus now and raised his voice.
 "Does Diskipleena mean nothing to you?" he shouted. "How can you claim to be a centurion when you allow your men to walk back to camp in disarray and without you? A Roman soldier holds his order even in captivity. How much more a centurion?"
 Markellus faintly shook his head, but there was no other response.
 "Where is your Virtooss? I've seen the dress. How could you have allowed such a thing?" He leaned down and spoke directly into Markellus's ear. "And your Fahma is destroyed. Do you have no regard for your father's house? Have you not thought of the man who put his confidence in you? You have brought shame on yourself, and your cousin, the pryfektus, is a ruined man. Do you not care about how your men will remember you?"
 He sat down again, opposite Markellus, and watched him. For a few moments there appeared to be no response. Then Markellus lifted his head and looked Lig-arius in the eye.
 "Femineetahs outweighs Diskipleena," he said. "I have seen Virtooss in women beyond anything you or I will ever have. Fahma has no more substance than the morning mist."
 Then his chin dropped to his chest, and he said no more.
 Lig-arius sat watching him for a few moments longer, turning the words over in his mind, but he couldn't make any sense of them. Whatever fever had produced them, it wasn't anything he'd encountered before. He rose silently, and left the chamber.
 The second day he asked for an escort to take him out to the western ground, but the pryfektus was unable to raise one. The men simply refused, one excuse after another, until the pryfektus ordered Rufeenus to select four men he could trust, and give them the option of forming the escort or immediate dishonourable discharge. Rufeenus was also ordered to accompany the escort.
 The clearing where the ambushes had taken place was exactly as described in both dispatches — ground churned wide by more than one fight, and drag marks all running the same direction, toward where the recovery party's carts must have stood. All of this was as he expected, but there was absolutely no clue as to the identity of the assailants.
 After he had combed the ambush site, he asked the escort to take him to the place where they had found Markellus. The escort brought him within sight of the camp and refused to go any further. They simply would not take another step closer. Rufeenus reiterated the threat of immediate dishonourable discharge, but all four said they would rather dishonourable discharge than face what Markellus had faced. Lig-arius had never witnessed terror like this in seasoned soldiers before. Even Rufeenus insisted that he needed to stay with the men to prevent them running. So Lig-arius approached the camp alone.
 He stepped cautiously out of the tree line, into the clearing that was clearly a lived-in camp, laid out exactly as the second dispatch had described it — the fire was burning low, eight bedrolls arranged around it, five horses tethered nearby, calm and well-cared-for.
 He looked again — eight bedrolls.
 The lad had said seven. Lig-arius had gone back over that testimony more carefully than any other he'd taken, because it was the only account not given in the middle of a fight, yet here were eight bedrolls. Either the lad had been in error, or an eighth had joined them. Could this be the Caledonian captive who vanished?
 He knew the woman only by report: three years a captive, taken from Caledonia, kept for display rather than labour. He had built his working picture of this band from the only material available to him — local, frontier-born women, if they were indeed women, who shared a grudge against the local Roman fort, but a Caledonian would have no place among them.
 She had no kin on this frontier. No tribe here that he knew of had connections with Caledonia. By every account, she had stood three years at a wall in that fort and said nothing to anyone beyond what was required of her. He couldn't fathom how a foreigner could join a band of locals, who would then trust her enough to fight beside her the very next day. In his experience, that kind of loyalty took years to build.
 Even granting the eight — granting Taran, granting whatever local grievance had raised the rest — he could not make the numbers answer for what had been done to Markellus's century. Two ambush points had worked in coordination, timed apart just far enough to split the response. Every horse in a mounted division had gone down inside minutes, in a fight fifty men had not been able to hold together long enough to protect their own mounts. A centurion had been taken from under the noses of his own escort, and not one man among fifty had seen it happen. This was command — someone reading the ground, dividing the labour, timing the strikes to land when the confusion was thickest. He had questioned officers, in three provinces, who could not have managed that with a century of trained men under them.
 He walked back to the fort as the light went, putting the report into its final order in his head. He would write what he had, plainly, the way a report should be written. Seven confirmed at the first engagement, on the word of a released witness he judged reliable. A camp, found after, showing eight. A captive missing from the pryfektus's own household that same night — the timing noted, the connection unproven. Coordination in the second engagement beyond what he would expect of an undisciplined local band, suggesting direction from a source not yet identified. He would ask, before he left Lorkh, that a wider search be made west and south of the fort, and that Markellus be questioned again the day he found his voice, if he ever did.
 He would leave one thing out of the report: that eight women alone might have done all of it. He turned the sentence over as he walked and could not make himself set it to wax. The ground had told him plainly enough. What he lacked was a place in his own mind built to hold it. A man writes down what he has a place for. He had none for this.
