Chapter 19. — Released.
 They crossed the ford at first light, and said nothing until the fort's shape rose clear through the trees ahead of them.
 Tigratavaah split them to their work. She and Tahna went to ground on the west side, within sight of the supply road and the blind stretch of wall behind it — the side Tigratavaah had already read as open, and the side that mattered most to confirm before anything else. Tacita and To-riaah worked around to the east, where the wall stood higher and better watched, reading what a real approach from that direction would cost, in case it was ever needed. Tovv-eh and Thilda ranged the ground behind the western tree line, marking cover, water, and a line the horses could hold without giving anyone away.
 What the west side gave up matched everything Tigratavaah had reported. Wagons rolled through the gate on that side on a loose, trusting schedule, waved through without a challenge called down from the wall. No patrol crossed that ground all morning. Every patrol that did go out swung east and north instead, walking the line the fort had always watched.
 "They still don't look this way," Tigratavaah said, low, watching a wagon roll through unchallenged. "Not once, all morning."
 Tovv-eh found ground worth using not far back from the road, on the blind side — a hollow, water close, cover thick on three sides. Thilda walked its edges herself before she'd trust it.
 "There's a patrol runs somewhere near this ground," she said, once she'd finished. "Small. Four men, maybe five. Not part of the northern loop — a stray from it, most likely. If we hold here quiet, we take it clean tomorrow, well clear of where we're sitting."
 Tiata turned that over, the falx across her knees. "Tomorrow, then. Tigratavaah first light, eyes on their—"
 She didn't finish it.
 Tigratavaah was already on her feet, one hand raised flat for quiet, head turned toward the trees south of the hollow. A moment later the others heard what she'd caught first — voices, unhurried and very close.
 "That's them," she said, low. "The same patrol. Come further off their line than they should have, and headed straight toward us." She looked to Thilda. "This was meant to be clear ground."
 "It should have been," Thilda said, already moving. "They shouldn't stray this far from the northern loop."
 Tiata was up, falx in hand.
 "Positions," she said. "The ones we marked for tomorrow. We use them now."
 They moved fast and silent, each of them already knowing where Thilda's ground had placed her, where Tovv-eh's strength would matter, where Tigratavaah's bow would open it. The patrol walked into it exactly as Tigratavaah had read them — unhurried, unaware — and the ambush took four of them down within a dozen heartbeats of the first arrow.
 The fifth was young, and when To-riaah's blade came level with his throat he didn't raise his own to meet it. He dropped it instead, both hands open, shaking hard.
 "Hold," Tiata said, before To-riaah's arm had finished its swing.
 To-riaah held.
 Tiata studied the boy a long moment.
 "How long have you served here?" she asked, in workable Latin.
 "Six months," he managed. "Less."
 "Then you're young enough to still remember what fear feels like," Tiata said. "That's useful to me today." She nodded to To-riaah, who lowered the blade. "You're going to walk back to that fort. Not run — walk, so you have time to remember all of it. Tell them four of your patrol are dead and you're the one left to say so. Tell them seven women did it, in less time than it takes to explain, on the side of the fort nobody thought needed watching."
 The boy said nothing. He seemed to have lost the power of speech altogether.
 "Tell them exactly that," Tiata said. "Don't soften it for your own sake, and don't guess at anything you didn't see. The truth, plain, is worth more to me than anything you could invent to shrink it."
 They stripped him of blade and shield, anything that might let him claim afterward he'd fought rather than cowed, and let him go.
 Tigratavaah watched him go until the trees took him, then turned to the others.
 "That wasn't silence," she said. "That's the opposite of the watch-station."
 "It was meant to be," Tiata said. "A forgotten station gets erased quietly, and nobody above it ever has to explain the gap. A garrison this size, with men left who'll ask questions when four of their own don't come back — that fort needs a story it can't control, told by someone who was there and can't be accused of exaggerating it." She looked toward the fort, still standing exactly as it had that morning, not yet knowing what it had lost. "Let them wonder what's coming next. Wondering costs more than four men ever could."
 They left the ambush site and moved deeper into cover before the fort could send anyone out looking. Behind them, a boy walked alone toward a garrison that would soon be a great deal less certain of what stood outside it.
