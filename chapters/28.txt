Chapter 28. — Captive Ignored.
 Tacita and Tahna walked the centurion into the clearing, blade still at his throat.
 The others had also slipped away from the scene of chaos, and they all converged on the campsite.
 Tiata suggested they take Markellus to a lone birch at the edge of the clearing, where the firelight thinned into shadow, and to bind his wrists behind it.
 "We should get him talking," Tahna said. "See what we can find out about how that fort actually runs. See if he'll tell us anything useful."
 "He won't be able to tell you much," Tigratavaah said. "My father's men lived under it eleven years — Rome calls it Diskipleena, a man drilled to hold his place and take his orders, nothing more. He's just a spoke in the big Roman wheel. He won't know much more than his own patrols. He'd have no idea what's happening further up the line."
 "He deserves a good thrashing anyway," To-riaah said. "He rode out this morning thinking he was in command. Let's give him something else to think about instead."
 "He was in command, as far as he's concerned," Tovv-eh said. "Trouble is, if we knock him around, he'll tell himself he took it like a soldier, faithful to his Emperor. It'll just give him more to crow about."
 "He and his cousin treated me like a cur," Taran said. "He deserves no better himself."
 Tiata was quiet a moment, before turning to Taran.
 "You know, Taran," she said, "you're right. He doesn't deserve another moment of our time."
 She turned back to Tahna and Tacita. "He might as well enjoy the birch for company tonight, and we'll see to him in the morning."
 Tacita had held the blade steady at his throat all this time, and they now escorted him to the birch, where they sat him down and bound him. They both stood and walked away, Tahna casting a single sneer back in his direction before completely ignoring him.
 Tovv-eh had built the fire up higher than usual, feeding it with her usual generosity. Thilda sat close to the flame with her spear across her knees, unhurriedly working a nick out of the blade with a whetstone. Tahna sat down and stretched her legs toward the fire, letting her head fall back, eyes half-closed.
 "Four horses down before any of them understood what was happening," To-riaah said. "I've never seen a line come apart that fast."
 "They never had a chance to hold anything," Tigratavaah said, not looking up from the arrow in her hands. "They split their own line chasing two shouts that weren't where the real danger was. By the time they went back for the horses, half the century was still in the trees looking the wrong way."
 Tiata sat a little apart, the falx laid flat across her knees. She'd already heard the whole of it once, walking back — every angle, every kill, exactly how the ambush had folded itself back into the trees.
 It was Tahna who turned the talk toward Taran.
 "And our newest terror," Tahna said, tipping her chin at Taran, the old sharpness softened. "South side, drawing off half their foot before any of them knew there was a fight. First real work with us, and she puts an arrow through a man's throat before he's gone ten paces into the trees. I'd call that a fair morning's introduction."
 "I had the angle Tigratavaah gave me," Taran said. "She read the ground. I only had to be where she said."
 "You only had to hold and not flinch," Tigratavaah said. "Most people flinch the first time it's real. You didn't."
 Tovv-eh reached over and knocked a shoulder against Taran, warm and solid. "First time with us, and already better than half the recruits I've ever seen a Roman drillmaster claim credit for."
 "I've held a bow longer than I've held anything else," Taran said. She turned the yew stave over in her lap, the wood worn smooth in places. "This very one. Just not for three years."
 Even Tiata leaned in at this.
 "I went into the armoury for the blades," Taran said. "I never knew what he'd done with my bow — never really thought about it."
 She paused.
 "It was hanging above his own armoury rack. Something for his guests to admire and be told about — the wild woman's bow, same as the wild woman herself." Her jaw set, hard, before she went on. "I never knew he'd kept it. Not until I saw it hanging there last night."
 She ran a thumb lovingly along the grain.
 "I took it down with my own two hands," she said, with a hint of satisfaction. "It still remembers my draw."
 Tovv-eh's hand found her shoulder again.
 "Then it's yours again," Tiata said, at last.
 The fire crackled. The centurion sat too far from it to feel its warmth, but close enough that every word had reached him.
 Thilda finished with the whetstone and laid the spear aside, testing the edge once against her thumb before she seemed satisfied. "First watch," she said, already rising. "I'll take it."
 "I'll spell you at the turn," Tigratavaah said, and that was the evening finished.
