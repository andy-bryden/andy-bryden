# 21 — The Tigress of Lorch

Taran had learned, over the last three years, that captivity didn't need bars to be a cage.

She awoke that morning, as always, in the small chamber off the Praefectus's own quarters, dressed in the clothes required by the household, her red hair loose and flowing, as required.

Breakfast she took standing, near the door, as required. She didn't mind it. Standing let her see the room.

The morning brought two officers up from Raetia, minor men on some minor errand, and the Praefectus did what he always did when new eyes came through his gate — found a reason to have her brought into the hall and, in the particular tone he used only for this, mentioned that she'd been taken in Britannia three years past, that she'd fought three of his own men to a standstill, that even now, three years tamed, she could probably still put a blade through a man's throat if she were given the opportunity.

"Show them," he said, as he held out the practice blade.

She showed them. It cost her nothing to move well for an audience that thought they understood what it meant — proof of Rome's reach, proof that even the wildest thing dragged out of the furthest edge of the map could be brought to heel and kept, years from home, still red-haired, still dangerous-looking. She'd learned to give them exactly that and nothing more. The part of her that actually knew how to kill a man, she kept for herself, and these shows helped to keep her hand in.

The two officers left before midday, suitably impressed, and the household settled back into its ordinary rhythm — one she'd had three years to learn in every particular. She knew which guards drank in the evening. She knew the armory was checked at dawn and rarely again before dusk. She knew the Praefectus's moods better than the rest of the household. She knew when he wanted her silent, when he wanted her shown off, when a bad week made him crueler and a good one made him softer.

She had spent three years watching all of it. She had no plan yet, but had made herself patient instead. She knew that she would recognise the moment when it came.

Dusk came grey and early that day, cook-fires already lit, and she was standing where she always stood at that hour — near the Praefectus, in the long room where he took his reports.

A lad was brought in still shaking — stripped of blade and armour, dust and worse on him, eyes full of fear.

"Speak," the Praefectus said.

The boy spoke.

Four of his patrol were dead. They were killed inside a dozen heartbeats, on the western ground nobody watched, by seven women who'd laid an ambush. They'd let him go. They'd told him to walk, not run, so he'd remember it right. They'd told him to tell it plain.

The room went very still.

Taran watched the Praefectus's face — the flush, the anger, the fear. He shouted something at an aide. He demanded numbers, demanded a search, demanded answers. Nobody moved with any real purpose. Everyone in that room had spent so long believing the western ground didn't matter that not one of them had the first idea what to actually do now that it had proven otherwise.

She stood very still herself, near the wall and felt a cold certainty settle in her chest that she hadn't felt in three years.

Seven women were on the western ground, waiting, watching, moving exactly when the moment offered itself and not a heartbeat before.

She had no idea who they could be, but she knew that whoever they were, they were the same type of woman as her — women who had been taken and measured and put on display for what other people needed them to be, who had decided, at some point, to stop being anyone's trophy and start being something with teeth of its own.

The Praefectus was still shouting when she slipped out of the hall. She took the passage that led to the armory that nobody would check again before morning.
