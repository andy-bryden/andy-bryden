 Greenup
  OSS agents in the Tyrol, 1945

 Chapter One
 The File from Cinecittà
 The villa on the outskirts of Bari smelled of damp limestone, stale Adriatic brine, and cigarette smoke.
 On the second floor, Dyno Löwenstein sat behind a folding trestle table littered with aerial reconnaissance prints, carbon paper, and mimeographed personnel rosters. Rain clicked against the glass of the tall terrace doors. Across the bay, gray convoy ships sat low in the water under a flat winter sky.
 Löwenstein rubbed his eyes. He had Kurt Löwenstein's posture—the stoop of a Berlin intellectual who had spent years leaning over draft resolutions and anti-fascist pamphlets before the Gestapo burned the family flat. Now, in the uniform of an American officer, he was reading casualty summaries from the Secret Intelligence branch of the 2677th Regiment.
 A knock rattled the wood.
 "Come," Löwenstein said.
 Fred Mayer came in first, walking with the quick, slightly forward-leaning gait of an auto mechanic clearing a crowded floor. He was twenty-three, short, broad across the shoulders, with thick dark hair combed straight back. Behind him came Hans Wijnberg, taller, thinner, pale from weeks indoors at the radio school, holding a woolen overseas cap in both hands.
 "You wanted us, Lieutenant?" Mayer said. His voice was Brooklyn dockside over an unyielding southwest-German foundation—Freiburg under Flatbush.
 "Sit," Löwenstein said. He pointed to two wicker chairs with frayed seats. "Shut the door behind you, Wijnberg."
 Wijnberg closed it, clicked the latch, and sat. He kept his cap in his lap, thumbs smoothing the piping.
 Löwenstein pushed two manila files toward them. "Personnel down in Caserta wants both of you reassigned. Signal corps depot for you, Hans. Translation pool in Naples for Fred. They think twenty-three-year-old refugees from Baden and Amsterdam are better used sorting mail and interrogating captured supply clerks."
 Mayer didn't look at the files. "We didn't train six months in Maryland and Algeria to file requisition slips in Naples."
 "They know German," Löwenstein said flatly. "They don't know you."
 "You know us," Mayer said. "Tell Caserta no."
 "I did." Löwenstein leaned forward, his elbows on the glass-topped table. "Which means you're my problem now."
 He turned one of the aerial prints around so they could see it. It was a mosaic of high-altitude photographs taken by an RAF Mosquito. A narrow valley, almost black against white peaks, with a faint gray spine running down the center.
 "The Brenner Pass," Löwenstein said. "Every train feeding Kesselring's army in northern Italy comes through this slit. Ammunition, coal, panzer engines, fresh divisions. The Fifteenth Air Force drops five hundred tons of high explosive on the tracks; the Germans bring in three thousand Italian forced laborers and have the rails relaid thirty-six hours later. We don't know what gets through. We don't know the repair schedules. We don't know when the trains are lined up waiting for green signals."
 "Then send a team to sit on the tracks," Mayer said.
 "We did," Löwenstein said. He tapped a pencil against a red grease-pencil circle on the map. "Twice. Company E dropped three men near Kufstein in November. Dead in four days. Another pair jumped into the Zillertal three weeks ago. Picked up before they unbuckled their harnesses. The locals hear an aircraft engine, look at the snow, see tracks, and call the Gendarmerie. If you're not from the valley, you don't last long enough to turn a radio on."
 Wijnberg looked at the map, then at Löwenstein. "What's the drop zone?"
 "There isn't one. The valley floor is walled in by flak batteries and radar pickets. If you drop low, you hit rock or you get shot out of the harness. If you drop high, you land on ice at nine thousand feet in the middle of winter."
 "We can jump into the high ground," Mayer said.
 "Can you ski?" Löwenstein asked.
 Mayer shrugged. "I've seen it done."
 "You've seen it done," Löwenstein repeated, his voice dry. "Hans, can you ski?"
 "No," Wijnberg said.
 Löwenstein dropped his pencil. "Then you'd freeze to death five miles from the nearest road. You're city boys. Fred, you know Freiburg and the Ford plant on Bedford Avenue. Hans knows the canals in Amsterdam. You drop into the North Tyrol, you won't even know what color the bus tickets are. You won't know which cop in the village has a son on the Eastern Front and which one is an SS fanatic who will shoot you in a tavern latrine."
 The room went quiet except for the rain. On the harbor, a destroyer sounded a single, muffled horn through the mist.
 "There's nobody else," Mayer said. He leaned his forearms against his knees, eyes fixed on Löwenstein. "You're sitting here with two men who speak the language, who hate them as much as you do, and who are ready to get on an airplane tonight. You want to wait around until the Fifteenth Air Force runs out of bombers?"
 "Determination doesn't keep you alive when you walk into an inn with the wrong accent, Fred."
 "Then find us a guide," Mayer said.
 Löwenstein looked at him.
 "You've got thousands of them in the cages," Mayer continued, his tone matter-of-fact. "Every week the British drag another five hundred Austrians out of the mountains in the north. Half of them were drafted. A quarter of them were socialists before thirty-eight. Find one who knows the Brenner, who knows the farms, who wants his town back. You put him in front, I handle the operational legwork, Hans runs the set."
 Löwenstein didn't answer immediately. He stood up, walked to the tall windows, and looked out at the harbor.
 "If he turns on you," Löwenstein said to the glass, "he hands you to the Gestapo before you reach the first rail junction. You know what happens to German Jews carrying American papers and codebooks."
 "We know," Wijnberg said quietly.
 Löwenstein turned back. He reached into an iron lockbox sitting on the credenza behind his desk, pulled out a thick buff folder tied with faded red string, and dropped it onto the table between them.
 "Cinecittà," Löwenstein said. "The prisoner camp outside Rome. The British Eighth Army took a batch of deserters off the Gothic Line two weeks ago. Field security sorted out twenty-odd Austrians with clean records before the Anschluss. One of them walked away from an infantry regiment near Bologna in December."
 Mayer pulled the folder across the blotter. He untied the knot and opened the first page.
 Pasted to the top right corner was a small, grainy black-and-white photograph - a lean man in a collarless Wehrmacht tunic, high cheekbones, dark hair cropped close to the scalp, staring straight at the camera with an expression of stubborn exhaustion. Underneath, typed in purple carbon:
  WEBER, Franz. Born - Oberperfuss, Tirol, 1920. Rank - Oberleutnant, Gebirgsjäger. Catholic. Deserted unit 18 December 1944.
 "Oberperfuss," Löwenstein said, tapping the map. "It's a farming village on a plateau twelve miles west of Innsbruck. Look at the elevation. It sits right above the Inn Valley. On a clear morning, you can look down and see the switches at the main freight depot."
 Mayer stared at the photograph. He ran a thumb over the paper.
 "Get the jeep," Mayer told Wijnberg. "We're going to Rome."

 Chapter Two
 Deserter of Oberperfuss
 The sound stage smelled of unwashed wool, damp straw, and carbolic soap.
 Before the war, Mussolini's film crews had built Roman palaces inside the Cinecittà complex. Now, concertina wire divided the hangars into holding pens, and hundreds of men in gray coats sat on wooden benches under the dead carbon-arc lights, waiting for their names to be called by Allied field police.
 An armed British sergeant opened the side door to an empty sound-editing booth. "Number forty-one," the sergeant said, jerking his thumb back toward the wire. "That's your man. Twenty minutes."
 Dyno Löwenstein pulled out two wooden chairs and set them on the bare floor. Fred Mayer stayed on his feet, leaning his hip against a heavy soundproof partition, his hands jammed into the pockets of his field jacket.
 The door clicked open. A British corporal pushed Franz Weber into the booth, then shut the door from the outside.
 Weber stood straight. His Wehrmacht field-gray tunic was missing its collar tabs, national eagle, and shoulder boards, leaving dark rectangular scars where the stitches had been cut. His trousers were stiff with dried Bolognese mud. He had hollowed cheeks, dark stubble, and eyes that moved quickly between the two men, assessing their insignia. He settled on Mayer.
 "Sit down, Herr Oberleutnant," Löwenstein said in standard High German, pointing to the empty chair.
 Weber did not move toward the chair. "I gave up the rank when I crossed the lines, Captain." His voice had the flat, clipped vowels of the North Tyrol.
 "Sit anyway," Löwenstein said.
 Weber sat. He rested his chapped hands on his knees and kept his back off the wood.
 Mayer stepped forward, speaking in the broad, hard-edged German of the southwest. "Why did you leave your battalion?"
 Weber looked up, his gaze steady. "Because we were losing."
 "A lot of men are losing," Mayer said. "Most of them keep shooting until an artillery shell takes their legs off. You took off your boots, crawled through three hundred yards of canal mud, and waved a white undershirt at the British Eighth Army."
 "The war was lost two years ago," Weber said quietly. "At Stalingrad. Everything after that has been murder."
 "That didn't bother you in Poland?" Mayer stepped closer, leaning down slightly so Weber had to tilt his head. "Didn't bother you in Russia? You wore the tunic four years. You collected your pay."
 Weber's jaw tightened. "I was drafted in thirty-nine. An ordinary infantry regiment. In the Ukraine, my company was ordered to guard a railhead while the Sicherheitsdienst cleared the ghetto. They loaded women and children into lime-washed cattle cars. In Yugoslavia, we burned three villages because a partisan cut a telephone line. If you think I took pride in that uniform, you don't know who you're talking to."
 "I know what the dossier says," Mayer snapped. "I also know the Abwehr puts smart men in deserter cages all the time. Good story, clean papers, anti-Nazi Catholic background. You get repatriated, you find out who's working for the Allies, and by Tuesday morning everyone is hanging from meat hooks in Munich."
 Weber didn't blink. "If I were working for the Abwehr, I would have told your British interrogator that I loved Churchill. I told him the truth - I am an Austrian, I am a Catholic, and Adolf Hitler is a criminal who is dragging my country down into an open grave. If that makes me a spy in your eyes, send me back to the pen."
 Löwenstein cleared his throat, breaking the tension. "Your home is Oberperfuss."
 "Yes."
 "Your family?"
 "My mother, Aloisia. Three sisters - Luise, Eva, Margarethe. My father died when I was young." Weber turned his eyes back to Löwenstein. "My family is known in the district. We are not Party members. None of us."
 "And the village?" Mayer asked, his tone still sharp. "How many Nazis?"
 "A few," Weber said. "The innkeeper. A couple of postmen. The new mayor the Party put in after thirty-eight. The rest are farmers. They go to Mass, they curse the food requisitions, and they hide their grain under the floorboards."
 Löwenstein unfolded a pocket map and spread it across the desk between them. It showed the valley of the Inn River, with Innsbruck in the center and the mountain roads curling south toward Italy.
 "What is the view from the ridge above your mother's house?" Löwenstein asked.
 Weber glanced at the contour lines without hesitation. "From the high meadows above Oberperfuss, you look down directly onto the valley floor. To the east, the main rail line from Germany enters Innsbruck. To the south, you see the marshaling yards and the mouth of the Sill Gorge—the entrance to the Brenner."
 Mayer watched the Austrian's eyes as he spoke. There was no hesitation, no calculation. The man was visualizing his home ground.
 "If someone had a transmitter on that ridge," Mayer said, "could they watch the rail traffic?"
 Weber looked from the map back to Mayer. His expression changed, the weariness giving way to sudden caution. "A transmitter in Oberperfuss wouldn't last three days if the Germans brought a detector van up the mountain road. The valley echoes. You'd need to hide it in the rafters. You'd need people to carry the messages. People who could walk through the checkpoints without their hands shaking."
 "Could your sisters do that?" Mayer asked.
 Weber's face went cold. "I won't bring the Gestapo down on my mother's house."
 "The Gestapo is already there," Mayer said. He pulled up the other chair and sat down directly across from Weber, so close their knees nearly brushed. "You think this ends quietly? In six months, the Russians are going to cross the Danube from the east. The Americans are coming through the Bavarian passes. The SS will turn Tyrol into a bunker. They'll blow every bridge, draft every fifteen-year-old boy in Oberperfuss, and fight street by street until the valley is rubble."
 Weber was silent. His hands gripped his knees until the knuckles turned white.
 "You want to save your family?" Mayer said, his voice dropping low, hard, and urgent. "You take us in. We set up an intelligence post above Innsbruck. We tell the bombers exactly what to hit—the rail yards, the repair shops, the switch engines—and what to leave standing. When the ground forces get to the pass, you help us bring the district down from the inside before the SS can burn it."
 Weber looked at Mayer's face, studying the sharp, dark features. "You're not American-born."
 "No," Mayer said. "Freiburg. Jewish. My father won the Iron Cross in the last war. In thirty-eight, they made him scrub the street with a toothbrush. We got out. Now I'm coming back."
 The sound of boots and distant shouts drifted through the high rafters of the Cinecittà studio.
 Weber stared down at the map, his thumb tracing the blue line of the Inn River where it curved past his home.
 "You can't jump into the valley," Weber said, his voice barely above a whisper. "There are searchlights at Zirl, radar at Kematen, flak batteries ringing the airfield. They would shoot you to pieces before you cleared five hundred meters."
 "We know," Mayer said.
 "And you can't walk across the Swiss border. The alpine patrol has ski posts every kilometer."
 "Then tell us how we get in."
 Weber looked up, meeting Mayer's eyes.
 "The Sulztalferner," Weber said. "It's a glacier. South of the Stubai Alps, nearly three thousand meters high. It's completely barren. In the winter, there are no patrols because nobody believes a human being can survive the descent."
 Mayer looked at Löwenstein, then back at the Austrian.
 "Can you get us down it?" Mayer asked.
 Weber stood up, his shoulders straight, no longer looking like a prisoner. "If you can jump into nine feet of powder without breaking your necks, I can get you to Oberperfuss."

 Chapter Three
 The Brooklyn Grid
 The staging house in Brindisi was a requisitioned stone villa surrounded by olive groves that rattled in the damp sea wind.
 In the ground-floor salon, Hans Wijnberg had stripped an OSS SSTR-1 transceiver down to its chassis on a mahogany dining table. The room smelled of solder resin, lead batteries, and the sour red wine Löwenstein bought by the demijohn from an orchardist down the lane.
 Mayer stood over a six-foot contour map of the Tyrol weighted down at the corners with brass shell casings. Franz Weber, now wearing a sanitized American olive-drab field uniform without insignia, stood on the opposite side of the table, his finger resting on an expanse of white contour lines bunched tightly between sharp elevation markers.
 "Here," Weber said. "Sulztalferner. Southwest of the Schrankogel. Elevation roughly three thousand meters."
 Löwenstein walked in, carrying a fresh carbon-copied weather intercept from Caserta. "The USAAF navigators at Manduria say jumping blind onto a high-altitude glacier at night is suicide. If the wind shears off the ridges, you'll slam into granite faces before your canopy even fully deploys."
 "The navigators aren't jumping," Mayer said without looking up. "The valley floors are out. What else is there, Franz?"
 "Nothing," Weber said. "Every pass between Switzerland and Italy is monitored by mountain border guards on skis. The Inn Valley itself is a trap. If we hit the glacier, we drop into deep snow, but there are no Germans for twenty kilometers. It is the only door that isn't locked."
 "Assuming you survive the drop," Löwenstein said. "What happens to the radio?"
 Hans looked up from his balance meter, holding an alligator clip between his fingers. "The SSTR-1 is packed in rubber shock-mounts inside water-sealed canisters. Transmitter, receiver, hand-generator, spare tubes, batteries. If the canister hits soft snow without cracking a boulder, it works."
 "And if the canister lands in a crevasse?"
 "Then we have a very long walk back for nothing," Hans said evenly.
 Mayer tapped the map at Innsbruck. "We need to secure our communications before we get on that plane. If the Funkabwehr picks up our traffic, standard OSS enciphering will keep them busy for a few hours, maybe a day. But if they crack the grid coordinates, they'll know which train yards we're reporting before the bombs drop."
 Hans reached into his canvas map case and pulled out a pocket street guide of New York City, dog-eared and creased from his months living with his brother in Brooklyn. He opened it flat on top of the Austrian Alps.
 "We transpose," Hans said.
 Löwenstein arched an eyebrow. "Transpose what?"
 "Brooklyn," Mayer said, a faint grin breaking through his face. "Hans and I know the borough street by street. The Germans don't."
 Hans took a black grease pencil. "Innsbruck is Flatbush. The main passenger station at the center is Grand Army Plaza."
 "The freight marshaling yards," Mayer pointed with the tip of his thumb, "where the rail lines split toward Italy—that's Ebbets Field."
 "The air base at Hotting?" Hans asked.
 "Floyd Bennett Field," Mayer answered instantly.
 Weber looked at the two young men, his brow furrowed as he watched them turn his homeland into an American street grid. "And the Sill Gorge? The entrance to the Brenner pass?"
 "Gowanus Canal," Mayer said. "If Hans transmits that two heavy coal trains are delayed at Ebbets Field heading toward the Gowanus, the German radio interceptors in Munich will waste three days trying to find an industrial sector matching the phonetics. By the time they figure it out, the Fifteenth Air Force has already pulverized the switches."
 Hans pulled a small notebook from his shirt pocket, inscribed the cipher substitutions in a tight, microscopic hand, tore the page out, and handed it to Löwenstein. "Bari Station Victor gets that copy. I keep the other in my heel."
 Löwenstein looked at the slip of paper, then folded it neatly into his tunic pocket. He pulled another document from his clipboard—an operational dispatch stamped with red ink - TOP SECRET - EYES ONLY - OPERATION GREENUP.
 "Caserta approved the flight window," Löwenstein said quietly.
 The room went still. Outside, the Mediterranean rain pattered against the shuttered terrace doors.
 "When?" Mayer asked.
 "The 885th Bomb Squadron at Brindisi has an aircraft prepped. A B-24, black finish, equipped for low-level night insertion. Captain Billings is the pilot. He says there's a break in the weather over the Alps starting tomorrow night, February twenty-fifth. It gives you an eight-hour window before the next front moves in from the Atlantic."
 Löwenstein laid the manifest flat on the table, unscrewed his fountain pen, and signed his name across the authorization block.
 "Three jump manifests," Löwenstein said. "Sergeant Frederick Mayer. Sergeant Hans Wijnberg. Franz Weber, civilian guide. You take three days' rations, cold-weather jump smocks, your personal sidearms, and three pairs of alpine touring skis packed into the primary equipment container."
 Weber nodded, his hand resting on the elevation markers. "The skis are not optional. If that container doesn't drop cleanly on our heading, we do not come off that ice field. In February, unbroken alpine powder is bottomless."
 "The drop container is rigged to an independent static line," Löwenstein said. "Billings will drop it right down your chute path."
 He capped his pen.
 Mayer looked at Hans, who was already seating the quartz crystals back into the olive-drab steel casing of the SSTR-1.
 "Get your kit stowed, Hans," Mayer said. "We're going to Flatbush."

 Chapter Four
 The Joe Hole at Midnight
 The stripped-down B-24 Liberator had no gun turrets, no armor plating, and no interior heating. Its outer skin was painted dead matte black.
 In the aft fuselage, the smell of 100-octane aviation fuel and hydraulic fluid hung thick in the uninsulated cold. Mayer, Wijnberg, and Weber sat on the metal ribbed floor, their backs wedged against the bulkhead. Each man was bundled into heavy canvas winter jump smocks worn over layers of wool, with thick gloves, jump boots, and chest-mounted reserve chutes strapped tight over their main packs.
 At eighteen thousand feet, the air was thin enough to make their lungs ache around the rubber rims of their oxygen masks.
 Up in the cockpit, Captain John Billings flew with the cockpit lights blacked out, watching the pale luminescence of the instrument needles. He had flown supply drops into Yugoslavia and northern Italy, but threading a four-engine bomber through the high granite corridors of the Tyrol in dead winter was a different matter. To stay below German radar stations at Verona and Innsbruck, he had crossed the Adriatic at low altitude, then climbed steeply over the Venetian plain into the teeth of an alpine crosswind.
 The intercom crackled in the crew chief's headset. The sergeant leaned down to Mayer, pulling his mask back for a second to shout over the deafening roar of the four Pratt & Whitney radial engines.
 "Ten minutes to initial point! Get hooked up!"
 Mayer tapped Weber and Hans on their shoulders. They dragged themselves to their feet in the cramped, pitching fuselage. The vibration came up through the soles of their boots, rattling their teeth.
 Hans checked his quick-release box with gloved fingers. His face was chalky under the flight goggles, but his eyes were steady. Weber moved with practiced alpine economy, checking the straps on the supply canisters lashed near the drop hatch—the "Joe hole," a round exit cut into the floor where the belly turret had once been.
 Down through the dark opening, the world was a jagged monochrome nightmare.
 Moonlight caught the ridgelines—immense razorbacks of black rock slicing through fields of brilliant, blinding snow. Billings was flying below the peaks now, weaving through the valleys, banking the heavy bomber so close to the cliff faces that the shadow of the wings seemed to scrape the crags. Downdrafts slammed the aircraft downward by fifty feet in a stomach-dropping lurch, then kicked it back up into the slipstream.
 A bell rang once. A sharp, mechanical hammer against brass.
 Above the hole, the red indicator lamp flicked on.
 The crew chief dropped to one knee by the rim, sliding back the protective canvas cover. A gale of minus-twenty-degree air tore into the fuselage, instantly freezing the moisture on the men's goggles and cutting through three layers of canvas.
 "Static lines!" the sergeant roared.
 Mayer snapped his bronze carabiner onto the overhead cable running the length of the ceiling, yanking it back twice to test the lock. Weber hooked in second. Hans hooked in third.
 The sergeant gripped the release lanyard on the first supply canister—the one holding the food, the dry clothes, and the two pairs of cross-country skis.
 Billings leveled the wings. Down below, the dark serrations of rock opened up into an immense, unbroken white sheet tilted at a terrifying angle - the frozen expanse of the Sulztalferner glacier. There were no lights, no roads, no trees. Only the dead pale glow of ice under the stars.
 The clock on the bulkhead read 01:05.
 The red lamp cut out.
 The green lamp hit them like a flashbulb.
 "Go! Go! Go!" the crew chief screamed, kicking the equipment container down into the dark.
 Weber lunged first. He dropped through the hole feet-first, his arms clamped across his reserve pack, sucked instantly into the black wash of the slipstream.
 Mayer didn't pause. He grabbed both sides of the circular rim, threw his legs down into the open sky, and let the wind tear him from the plane.
 The blast of four propellers hammered his ribs, spinning him blind into the freezing air before the static line snatched the pack off his back. There was a violent, upward crack that rattled his spine, and the white sheet of the glacier rushed up to meet him.

 Chapter Five
 Crevasse on the Glacier
 The canopy cracked open, snapping Mayer's head back against the nylon risers with the force of a hammer blow.
 The deafening howl of the B-24's four radial engines shrank into a faint, dying whine, swallowed by the immense granite basin. For several seconds, there was absolute, breathless stillness. The air was thin, pure, and so bitterly cold it burned his throat. Beneath his dangling boots, the vast white slope of the glacier tilted sideways in the moonlight, expanding until the texture of the wind-scoured crust resolved into jagged drifts and shadowy terraces of blue ice.
 Mayer hit feet-first, drove straight through the crust, sank to his chest in powdery snow, and pitched forward. The silk canopy drifted down on top of him, smothering him in yards of suffocating white fabric.
 He fought his way out, spitting ice crystals, fumbled with numb, stiffening fingers at his chest harness, and punched the center release disc. The straps whipped free. He struggled to heave himself up, but the powder gave way under his elbows; he sank to his waist, his jump boots finding no bottom in the flour-like drifts. The temperature hung near ten below zero, with a cutting thermal downdraft shrieking off the knife-edge crest of the Schrankogel.
 "Hans!" Mayer yelled, his voice flat, deadened by the immense expanse of snow. "Franz!"
 Fifty yards uphill, a dark shape dragged itself violently out of a snowbank. It was Hans Wijnberg. His canopy had dragged him thirty yards across a wind-swept patch of blue ice before snagging on a jagged granite horn.
 "I'm here!" Hans called out, his breathing ragged and shallow. He was clutching the steel box of the SSTR-1 transceiver against his ribs; its padded container had landed four yards away, anchored by its small auxiliary chute. "The set is intact! I have the crystals!"
 Through the darkness below, Franz Weber clawed his way toward them on his hands and knees. He had no skis under his boots. He was floundering through the drifts like a drowning swimmer, his face white, his jump smock torn at the shoulder where he had scraped across a boulder.
 "The equipment crate!" Weber shouted, his teeth chattering so hard the words slurred. "Where is the cargo chute?"
 They scanned the pale blue expanse of the Sulztalferner. Sixty yards downhill, a deep, furrowed drag mark cut through the untouched powder, curving around a wind-sculpted dome of ice before vanishing over a sheer break in the topography.
 Mayer and Weber crawled to the edge on their bellies, dragging themselves through the drifts.
 The snow shelf ended in an ink-black slit four feet wide. Below lay a vertical crevasse, its icy blue walls plunging hundreds of feet down into the bowels of the glacier. Far down in the blue shadow, wedged irrevocably into a narrow bottleneck of sheer ice, lay the crumpled, mangled wreck of the primary supply container.
 "All three pairs of skis," Weber breathed, staring down into the abyss. His voice was drained of blood. "The heavy mountain boots. The dry woolens. The spare rations."
 None of them had skis.
 Mayer stared down into the crack, the bitter wind whipping his hair across his forehead. He spat onto the ice. "We aren't climbing down three hundred feet of dead ice to get them. We leave it."
 He pushed himself up and attempted to take a stride toward the lower slope. The crust gave way instantly with a dull, hollow whoosh. Mayer dropped to his ribs, his legs pinned by the suffocating weight of five feet of powder. When he tried to lever his weight on his forearms, the snow acted like dry sand, sliding and caving in around his chest.
 Hans tried to push forward to join them and sank to his hips, gasping as freezing powder forced its way down into the tops of his jump boots.
 "We can't walk through this," Hans whispered, clutching the seventy-pound radio unit against his stomach with trembling hands. "We'll burn every calorie we have before we cover half a mile."
 Weber looked down the vast, rolling expanse of the Sulztalferner. Below them lay three miles of treacherous, crevassed glacier dipping toward the treeline. Beyond that lay the locked mountain huts of the Gries valley.
 "If we stay in this drift until sunrise," Weber said, "our feet will freeze. By morning, gangrene sets in and you cannot stand."
 "Then don't let us stay in it," Mayer said, his jaw locked, dragging his torso forward out of the hole with his elbows. "You're the mountain trooper, Franz. Pick the ridge lines. We break the snow together."
 Weber did not argue. He moved to the front. Without skis, he had to throw his entire body into the drifts, using his thighs and hips to batter down a crude trench in the snow.
 Mayer fell into the furrow behind him, leaning his broad shoulders into the drifts to widen the channel, while Hans trudged in their wake, cradling the heavy steel transceiver against his chest.
 One agonizing, lung-searing stride at a time, their boots soaked and numb, their breath freezing into icicles on their collars, the three men began the descent on foot through the arctic dark.

 Chapter Six
 Horn-Sled to Längenfeld
 The timber walls of the Amberger Hütte emerged from the gray predawn gloom like a stranded barge in an ocean of snow.
 Drifts reached halfway up the ground-floor shutters. The mountain refuge had been locked since October, its chimney capped against winter storms.
 Mayer stumbled against the heavy pine door, his legs shaking with exhaustion. His jump trousers were frozen solid from the knees down, stiff as sheet metal, scraping against his raw, bleeding shins with every step. Hans collapsed onto the snow-covered porch, his head slumped against the radio container, his breath rattling in his throat like broken glass. Weber's face was bloodless, his lips purple.
 "Is the door barred?" Mayer asked, his voice a hoarse rasp.
 Weber didn't answer. He drew his heavy trench knife, rammed the steel blade into the gap between the heavy oak jamb and the iron lock-hasp, and threw his entire body weight against the hilt. The frozen wood groaned, splintered, and gave with a sharp, gunshot crack.
 Weber kicked the door inward.
 Inside, the hut smelled of dry pine, cold soot, and wool mothballs. The air was freezing, but it was dead out of the wind.
 Weber went straight to the cast-iron stove, stripping dry shavings from an iron basket with trembling hands. He struck a match; the sulfur flared, catching shavings of dry larch. Within minutes, a dull orange glow flickered through the stove's draft door, casting long shadows across the empty tables.
 Hans pulled his gloves off with his teeth. His fingertips were waxy white and hard as wood. Mayer grabbed Hans's hands and shoved them under his own wool sweater, pressing them against the warmth of his bare armpits. Hans let out a ragged hiss as the blood began to needle into the deadened tissue.
 Weber set a dented copper pot full of clean snow on the stove lid. He looked at Mayer.
 "From here down to Gries is five miles," Weber said. "Steep cart tracks, packed with waist-deep drifts along the creek bed. We have to walk it on foot. If we stay here, our joints will seize and we won't get up."
 They rested for two hours, drinking warm melted snow that tasted of old copper. Mayer massaged his numb feet, pulled dry wool socks from his jump pack, and laced his leather boots tight.
 By mid-morning, they stepped back out into the blinding glare of the alpine snow.
 Without skis, the march down the Sulzbachtal was an agonizing trudge. For four hours they stumbled down the creek gorge, sinking past their knees at every third stride, hauling the heavy steel radio container by turns. By noon, exhausted and soaked to the bone, they reached the cleared logging trails on the outskirts of Gries im Sulztal.
 Weber signaled them to halt behind a woodcutter's shed.
 "Stay out of sight," Weber whispered. "I'll go into the village."
 Mayer drew his sidearm beneath his coat, crouching beside Hans in the shadow of the logs. Twenty minutes later, Weber returned. He was pulling a heavy, wooden Tyrolean mountain sled—a steerable horn-sled (Hornschlitten) with curved ash runners, used by valley farmers to haul firewood down the ice roads.
 "I found an old friend," Weber said quietly, breathing hard. "Told him I was on transit leave and hauling personal trunks down to the main valley."
 They loaded the heavy rucksacks and the padded SSTR-1 radio container onto the wooden slats of the sled, lashing them tight with lengths of hemp rope.
 "Get on," Weber said.
 Mayer and Hans squeezed onto the rear of the sled frame, while Weber gripped the curved ash steering horns in front. With a hard shove of his boots, Weber launched the sled onto the hard-packed, icy cart track leading down toward Längenfeld.
 The wooden runners hissed furiously against the packed ice. The sled picked up terrifying speed down the steep switchbacks, rattling over frozen ruts and dodging rock walls where the gorge plunged sheer down toward the roaring river. Weber leaned his body weight hard into the corners, dragging his iron-shod heel to brake, snow spraying into Mayer's and Hans's faces as they whipped beneath the frozen pines.
 At the bottom of the grade, the gorge opened up into the broad basin of the Ötztal. Weber steered the sled into a snowbank beside a farm barn on the road to Ötztal-Bahnhof.
 They unloaded the gear, hid the sled behind the haystacks, and brushed the snow from their clothing. Under their open coats, Mayer and Hans wore civilian wool trousers and sweaters, their military insignia long removed. Franz Weber adjusted the collar of his mountain-trooper field jacket—the Austrian deserter once again disguised as an ordinary soldier on leave.
 Down by the bridge, a dull mechanical rumble shook the morning stillness.
 An old Postbus, its engine coughing blue diesel exhaust, turned the corner from the depot, chains rattling on its rear tires as it braked at the village stop.
 "Pick up the radio case," Mayer muttered, his eyes on the driver through the frosted windshield. "Act like you've been riding this route your whole life."

 Chapter Seven
 Inspection at Telfs
 The post-bus dropped them at Ötztal-Bahnhof under a slate-gray afternoon sky.
 The station smelled of sulfur, brown coal smoke, and damp gravel. Freight cars with chalked destination markings—Brenner, Bozen, Verona—sat on the sidings while anti-aircraft gunners in steel helmets stood behind quadruple 20mm cannons mounted on flatbeds, watching the cloudy ceiling.
 Weber bought three third-class tickets to Inzing with German reichsmarks from his pre-mission stash. Mayer and Hans kept their heads down, their collars turned up against the sleet, Mayer carrying the heavy canvas rucksack with the radio tucked beneath dirty work overalls.
 The local passenger train from Landeck rolled in with a screech of rusted brake shoes.
 The carriages were packed with off-duty rail workers, Tyrolean women carrying string bags of rutabagas, and wounded infantrymen returning from valley convalescent homes, their arms in sling-splints and bandages gray with coal dust. Mayer and Hans squeezed into the corridor of a second-class compartment, wedged between the frosted window and an iron luggage rack. Weber stood in the gangway, his Wehrmacht cap pulled low over his brow, arms folded over his chest.
 The train jolted forward, wheels clicking rhythmically over the frozen rail joints along the south bank of the Inn.
 At Telfs, the train stopped. Boots thudded onto the metal boarding steps.
 Two Feldgendarmerie sergeants pushed through the sliding door into the corridor. They wore heavy wool greatcoats and the crescent-shaped metal gorgets of the military police hanging from steel chains around their necks. Both carried MP 40 submachine guns slung muzzle-down across their ribs. Behind them was an older plainclothes official in a green loden coat and a felt fedora—railway Gestapo.
 "Papers," the lead sergeant barked, his voice carrying the flat, harsh cadence of Saxony. "Soldiers show leave passes and transit orders. Civilians show identity cards and work permits."
 The corridor went quiet. Passengers began fumbling into their coats, producing frayed paper passes stamped with purple eagles.
 Hans stood stiffly against the glass, his fingers buried deep in his coat pockets. Inside his heel was the cipher key; at his feet sat the rucksack containing a high-frequency transmitter that meant an immediate firing squad. Mayer shifted his weight half an inch, positioning his shoulders between the approaching sergeant and Hans.
 The sergeant checked a corporal's medical furlough, glanced at the soldier's face, stamped the paper, and stepped forward.
 His eyes locked onto Mayer's civilian wool coat, then dropped to the oversized rucksack on the floor.
 "You," the sergeant said, pointing a gloved finger. "Papers."
 Before Mayer could reach into his pocket for the forged French transit slip, Franz Weber stepped directly into the sergeant's path, blocking the aisle with his shoulders.
 "Are you blind, Feldwebel?" Weber snapped in the arrogant, biting bark of an Austrian officer dealing with rear-echelon enlisted men.
 The sergeant blinked, caught off balance. He looked at Weber's shoulder boards, then down at his mountain boots.
 "Herr Oberleutnant—"
 "Wir sind schon kontrolliert worden!" Weber cut him off, his voice loud enough to turn heads down the entire carriage. "We were checked at Ötztal before boarding. These men are under my orders, transporting electrical repair parts for the signal directorate in Innsbruck. If you want to delay critical Reichsbahn priority repairs to inspect paper that has already been stamped twice today, by all means, step out at Inzing and answer to the district transport commandant."
 Weber stepped in closer, his chin up, his eyes boring into the sergeant's face with cold, aristocratic disgust.
 The military policeman hesitated. His thumb hovered over his ticket-punch. He looked at Mayer, whose face was completely expressionless, then back at Weber's combat infantry ribbon and hollowed cheeks. In the German army of 1945, crossing a frontline combat officer over a bureaucratic paperwork check was an easy way to end up reassigned to a punishment battalion on the Oder.
 "Carry on, Herr Oberleutnant," the sergeant mumbled. He gave a half-hearted salute, turned on his heel, and pushed past them toward the rear of the carriage.
 Weber didn't move. He kept his arms crossed, staring down the gangway until the sliding door at the far end clicked shut.
 Ten minutes later, the train slowed, steam whistling through the relief valves. A painted wooden sign emerged through the sleet outside - INZING.
 "Move," Weber murmured, not looking at them.
 They stepped down into the slush of the empty platform as the locomotive hissed and pulled away toward Innsbruck. They didn't cross through the station house. Weber led them down the embankment, through a rusted wire gate, and onto an unpaved farm lane running south toward the dark wall of the mountains.
 The sleet turned to wet snow as they reached the edge of the woods.
 Ahead of them, rising abruptly from the floor of the Inn Valley, the mountain road wound up through the black pine forests toward the high plateau of Oberperfuss.
 "Three miles uphill," Weber said, adjusting his pack. "Keep off the main track. Watch for timber sleds."
 Mayer hoisted the radio rucksack higher onto his shoulders. "Lead the way, Franz."

 Chapter Eight
 The Hayloft at Schatz Farm
 The farmhouse sat on a rise above Oberperfuss, its heavy wooden eaves banked with snow.
 Below, in the valley trough, the blacked-out silhouette of Innsbruck lay dead silent under the curfew. Franz Weber led them through the apple orchard behind the barn, stepping softly through the frozen crust. He reached the heavy timber back door, paused, and tapped four times in a deliberate rhythm against the wood.
 A latch clicked. The door opened an inch, showing the yellow rim of a kerosene lantern held low.
 "Who is it?" a woman's voice whispered in broad Tyrolean dialect.
 "Mutter," Weber said. "Open up."
 The door swung wide. Aloisia Weber stood on the stone threshold, a wool shawl pulled tight over her nightdress. Her face went gray, her hand shaking so hard the lantern chimney rattled against its brass base.
 "Franzl," she breathed.
 "Get inside, Mother," Weber said quietly, pushing Mayer and Hans through the opening ahead of him before stepping in and sliding the iron bolt into place.
 The kitchen was warm, smelling of dried elderberries, cold lard, and wood smoke from the tiled Kachelofen. Three young women stood near the doorway leading to the bedrooms - Luise, Eva, and Margarethe. Luise had a kitchen knife in her right hand; when she saw her brother's face, her hand dropped to her side.
 "You're dead," Luise whispered. "The district command sent a notice to the mayor in January. Missing in Italy. Presumed killed or taken."
 "I wasn't killed," Franz said. He unslung his pack, setting it heavily on the scrubbed pine table. He gestured to Mayer and Hans, who stood with their caps in their hands, their breath still steaming in the cool perimeter of the room. "These are friends. American soldiers."
 Aloisia Weber set the lantern down on the table, her eyes darting between Mayer's battered civilian coat and the thick, rubber-sealed rucksack Hans carried.
 "Americans," she said, her voice shaking. "Franzl, what have you done?"
 Before Franz could answer, the floorboards above them creaked. Footsteps descended the narrow wooden staircase from the loft. A heavyset man in his fifties stepped into the kitchen, wearing gray loden trousers and a collarless shirt - Alois Abenthung, the former mayor of Oberperfuss, removed from office by the Nazis after the 1938 Anschluss.
 Abenthung looked at Weber, then slowly turned to inspect Mayer and Hans. He didn't look afraid; his eyes were hard and calculating.
 "Franz," Abenthung said. "The gendarme from Zirl was asking about your mother's ration coupons yesterday. If they find you here, they don't send you back to the front. They hang you in the square."
 "I didn't come back to hide, Alois," Weber said. "We came to work."
 Mayer stepped forward. "I'm Sergeant Mayer. US Army. This is Sergeant Wijnberg."
 Abenthung stared at Mayer's face, listening to the accent. "You speak like a German."
 "I am a German," Mayer said evenly. "From Freiburg. I left in thirty-eight because your new Reich was setting synagogues on fire. Now I'm back with the Americans. We have a radio set, and we need a place to transmit."
 The kitchen fell dead quiet. Outside, a branch heavy with wet snow snapped in the orchard with a sharp crack.
 "You cannot transmit from this house," Abenthung said bluntly, cutting through the silence. "The Gestapo knows Franz is missing. His mother's home is the first place the Gendarmerie will check if a direction-finding van picks up a signal from this plateau."
 Weber looked at Abenthung. "Where do we put Hans?"
 "At Alois Schatz's place," Abenthung said without hesitation. "The Taps farm, two hundred meters down the slope. Schatz is reliable. His attic has a blind crawlspace behind the hay storage, and his roofline is masked by fir trees. If a patrol comes up the road from Zirl, Schatz's dogs will bark five minutes before they reach the gate."
 Abenthung looked at Aloisia Weber, then turned back to Hans.
 "Pick up the machine, Sergeant," Abenthung said. "We'll run the antenna wire from Schatz's gable across the apple trees disguised as a wash line. You stay in the rafters. No one in the village except Schatz, Franz, and me will know you are alive."
 Hans nodded, hoisting the olive-drab steel box of the SSTR-1 transceiver.
 Ten minutes later, guided by Abenthung through the shadows of the orchard, Hans slipped into the hayloft of the Schatz farm. There, buried under dry rye straw and pine dust, Operation Greenup established its secret listening post above the Inn Valley.

 Chapter Nine
 Frozen Clothesline
 Dawn brought an iron frost that turned the mud in the farmyards hard as concrete.
 At first light, Alois Schatz's wife carried a wooden basket of wet linens and rough work clothes out into the orchard behind their barn. The steam from the wash curled around her headscarf in the zero-degree air.
 Up in the barn gable of the Schatz farm, a small square ventilation flap opened half an inch. Hans Wijnberg fed the end of an enameled copper antenna wire through the gap. Alois Abenthung caught it from below, spliced it deftly into the galvanized clothesline wire strung between the barn eaves and a rusted iron hook on the cider shed, and helped Frau Schatz pin heavy work trousers and sheets along its length with wooden pegs.
 From fifty yards away on the mountain track, it looked like every other Tyrolean farmstead on washday - frozen laundry rattling stiffly in the alpine wind.
 Inside the barn attic, Hans worked in absolute darkness, kneeling on a sheepskin pelt behind a massive bulkhead of packed rye straw.
 The cold was sharp enough to numb his ears within minutes. He wore fingerless wool gloves, blowing hot breath into his cupped knuckles before touching the knurled vernier dials on the faceplate of the SSTR-1. The olive-drab steel casing sat on an inverted wooden butter churn. He inserted a frequency quartz crystal into its ceramic socket, connected the lead from the clothesline antenna to the binding post, and slotted the ground wire into an iron spike Abenthung had driven deep into the earth beside the foundation stones.
 Fred Mayer sat on a hay bale near the small loft door, watching the approach road through a knothole in the timber siding. He had a British Welrod silenced pistol laid flat across his knee.
 "Time?" Mayer whispered.
 Hans checked his silver pocket watch propped against the transmitter chassis. "07:14. Bari opens the schedule at 07:15."
 Hans put on the heavy bakelite headphones and switched on the receiver.
 A high, hissing wash of atmospheric static filled his ears—the sound of empty shortwave channels rolling over the Mediterranean and the jagged crests of the Alps. He turned the vernier dial by fractions of a millimeter, hunting for the steady carrier tone from OSS Station Victor.
 The second hand on his watch clicked past the twelve.
 Hans took the brass telegraph key between his thumb and forefinger. His hand was steady, but his breath was shallow. German radio detection vans—Funkpeilwagen—patrolled the valley roads between Hall and Telfs, carrying rotating loop antennas designed to triangulate illegal transmissions within ninety seconds of key-down.
 He closed the circuit.
  Dit-dit-dit... dah-dit-dah...
 He tapped out the call sign. Three letters, repeated twice, followed by the four-figure authentication group from the silk cipher sheet tucked into his heel.
 The transmitter tubes glowed a faint, dull orange inside their perforated metal cage. With every press of the key, a tiny blue spark leapt across the contact points.
  GREENUP TO VICTOR. GREENUP TO VICTOR.
 Hans released the key and switched instantly to receive.
 The static roared in his ears. Nothing. The hiss of the ionosphere, punctuated by the distant clatter of a commercial teleprinter signal bouncing out of Munich.
 Mayer shifted on his bale. "Anything?"
 "Quiet," Hans said, his eyes closed, straining against the background noise.
 He waited thirty seconds. The protocol demanded strict radio discipline - no continuous transmission longer than two minutes, then a mandatory break to keep the German direction-finders from obtaining a clean bearing.
 At 07:17, he keyed the transmitter again.
  Dah-dah-dit... dit-dit-dah...
  PACKAGE DELIVERED. THREE PIECES ON SHELF. EBBETS FIELD IN SIGHT.
 He cut the power and switched back to the headphones.
 For ten seconds, there was only the cold roar of the ether. Then, cutting cleanly through the crackle of static, came the rhythmic, mechanical chime of high-speed Morse, sent with the crisp, unmistakable rhythm of an American military operator at Bari:
  VICTOR TO GREENUP. WE HEAR YOU. GODSPEED.
 Hans let out a slow, ragged breath. A grin broke across his pale face as he pulled the headset down around his neck.
 "They have us," Hans said.
 Mayer stood up, brushed the dry chaff from his wool coat, and slipped the Welrod back into his shoulder holster.
 "Good," Mayer said, stepping toward the ladder. "Now I need a uniform."

 Chapter Ten
 The Seamstress of Oberperfuss
 Luise came back up the mountain road on the evening bus from Innsbruck, carrying her nurse's tin lunch canister inside a straw basket.
 Underneath the boiled cabbage and ration rye lay three blank convalescent furlough sheets—Genesungsurlaubsscheine—stamped with the red cross of the Innsbruck Reserve Military Hospital. Tucked beside them inside a roll of gauze was a brass circular stamp of the Wehrkreiskommando and an ink pad soaked in purple aniline dye.
 In the back room of the Weber farmhouse, Maria Hörtnagl spread an old bedsheet over the table. She was a village seamstress with thick silver hair pinned tight behind her ears and hands scarred by needle pricks. On the sheet lay a field-gray officer's tunic and breeches, brought out from the attic of an anti-Nazi neighbor whose son had fallen near Kharkov two winters earlier.
 "He was six feet tall," Maria said, looking Mayer up and down with a tailor's cold appraisal. "You are five-foot-six. If you walk into Innsbruck looking like a boy wearing his father's coat, the Feldgendarmerie will pick you up at the bus stop."
 "Cut it down," Mayer said. "Take four inches off the skirts, tighten the waist, take in the shoulders."
 Maria picked up her heavy tailor's shears. "Strip off your shirt."
 For two hours, the room was silent except for the snip of steel through heavy wool and the scrape of chalk. Mayer stood on a wooden footstool while Maria pinned the rough wool around his shoulders. Franz Weber sat in the corner by the stove, filling in the convalescent pass with a steel-nibbed dip pen, copying the handwriting of the chief medical officer at the reserve hospital.
 "Unit?" Franz asked, pen hovering above the blank line.
 "Mountain infantry," Mayer said. "Eighty-eighth Regiment. Say I caught shrapnel outside Monte Cassino. Broken femur, scarred lung tissue. That covers my limp and gives me an excuse if my breathing sounds short."
 "Diagnosis - Splitterverletzung linker Oberschenkel und Brustkorb," Franz muttered, scratching the words into the fibrous paper. "Convalescent leave - four weeks. Destination - Innsbruck and surrounding district for alpine air rehabilitation."
 Franz pressed the brass seal into the purple ink pad, lined it up carefully with the edge of the signature block, and brought his palm down hard on the wood. The imperial eagle and swastika appeared, slightly smudged at the bottom rim—an authentic, bureaucratic mark.
 Maria pulled the pinned jacket off Mayer's shoulders, laid it flat across her knees, and began feeding the seam through the foot-treadle sewing machine. The needle rattled in a rhythmic, mechanical clatter.
 By midnight, the tunic was finished. She had restitched the silver aluminum cord along the collar, attached the shoulder boards of a Wehrmacht Leutnant, and sewn the black-and-silver ribbon of the Iron Cross Second Class through the second buttonhole.
 Mayer pulled on the breeches, the polished jackboots Franz had brought from his own wardrobe, and the altered tunic. He buttoned it to the throat.
 He turned toward the small mirror hung beside the cupboard.
 The reflection was sharp. The broad shoulders of a Brooklyn mechanic filled out the field-gray wool. The dark, comb-backed hair, hard jaw, and cold dark eyes fit the mold of a combat officer who had spent four years in the line.
 "How do I look?" Mayer asked.
 Franz leaned back in his chair, studying him from the boots to the cap.
 "Like a bastard who just buried half his platoon in Italy," Franz said. "Keep your left shoulder dropped when you walk. Don't look at the MPs. If an enlisted man salutes, don't smile—just touch the brim of your cap like you hate his guts for being alive."
 Mayer adjusted the silver braided wire on his visor cap, pulled the forged medical pass from the table, and slipped it into his breast pocket directly behind the Iron Cross ribbon.
 "I'm catching the first bus down," Mayer said.

 Chapter Eleven
 The Footbridge at Ebbets Field
 In Oberperfuss, Anna Niederkircher—the sharp-eyed, fiercely anti-Nazi landlady of the Gasthof Zur Krone and mother of Franz Weber's fiancée, Anni—quietly arranged the urban logistics for Mayer's entry into Innsbruck.
 Through Niederkircher's trusted family channels, contacts were secured with Franz's sisters, Margarethe Kelderer and Eva Weber, who maintained an apartment on the third floor of Anichstraße 19 in the heart of Innsbruck.
 At dawn, Mayer took the local commuter bus down from the plateau into the city.
 He walked south with a deliberate, stiff hitch in his left stride, leaning lightly on a dark wooden walking stick. Enlisted men passing him on the Maria-Theresien-Straße snapped to attention, their boots smacking the wet pavement as their hands whipped to their temples. Mayer returned each salute with a curt, bored flick of his gloved right fingers, his face set in a permanent scowl.
 He took up a base of operations inside the city, shuttling between the Anichstraße flat and the local Offizierskasino, where his forged convalescent papers, iron-gray tunic, and Iron Cross ribbon gave him an unassailable cover among German officers on transit leave.
 He crossed the gravel approach to the freight yards.
 A high pedestrian iron footbridge spanned the tracks south of the Hauptbahnhof. Mayer climbed the rusted stairs slowly, stopping on the central platform as if catching his breath from the exertion of his wounded hip. He rested his forearms on the cold iron railing and looked down.
 Below him lay twenty sets of parallel rails - the main sorting depot for all military traffic moving toward the Brenner.
 It was choked.
 Because Allied bombers had severed the southern lines near Trento two days earlier, freight trains were backed up from the yard throat all the way to the southern tunnels. Mayer counted twenty-two distinct freight assemblies idling on the sidings.
 On siding four - twelve flatcars carrying Sturmgeschütz III assault guns, their long barrels lashed down with steel cables, draped in tarpaulins.
 On sidings seven and eight - thirty-four tank cars of aviation fuel from the synthetic refineries in Silesia, waiting for fresh locomotives.
 On siding eleven - a forty-car train of closed boxcars bearing red explosive-hazard markers, guarded by Waffen-SS sentries with submachine guns stamping their feet against the cold.
 Mayer did not take notes. He looked at his watch, traced the track switches with his eyes, and committed the layout to memory using the Brooklyn grid - Ebbets Field, right field corner, three locomotives idling on the third spur.
 That evening, he walked into the Stiftskeller beer hall near the old town center.
 The vaulted dining room was crowded with officers and technical staff from the transport command. Smoke from synthetic tobacco hung in blue sheets under the stone arches. Mayer found an empty stool at a corner table near the stove, ordered a quarter-liter of rough red South Tyrolean wine, and sat quietly, nursing his leg.
 Within twenty minutes, two captains from the Wehrmacht-Transportleitung sat down across from him, unbuttoning their greatcoats.
 "The line is dead again past Matrei," the older one said, slamming his leather gloves onto the oak table. "A single B-25 took out the viaduct at dawn. Now we have four thousand tons of heavy artillery shells sitting on track nine in the freight yard, and the stationmaster doesn't have enough steam engines to shuck them into the tunnels."
 "Why not move them back to Hall?" the other asked.
 "With what coal? The yardmaster says they sit where they are until tomorrow night at the earliest. If the Americans come over tomorrow, the whole district goes up like a powder keg."
 The officer looked across at Mayer's Iron Cross ribbon. "You just back from Italy, Leutnant?"
 Mayer took a slow sip of wine. "Cassino. Then the hospital at Merano."
 "You're lucky to be out of it," the captain grunted, signaling the waitress for two beers. "Down there, you can at least shoot back. Here, we sit on a powder magazine waiting for the Fifteenth Air Force to drop the roof on our heads."
 "Let's hope their navigators are as blind tomorrow as they were last week," Mayer said, his voice flat and perfectly cynical.
 The German laughed bitterly. "They don't need navigators. You can see twenty freight trains from twenty thousand feet without glasses."
 Mayer finished his wine, dropped three reichsmarks on the table, and pushed back his chair with an audible grunt of pain from his hip.
 "Good evening, gentlemen," he said, touching his cap.
 He walked out into the blackout cold, heading directly to the apartment on Anichstraße where Eva Weber was waiting to receive his intelligence notes.

 Chapter Twelve
 Thunder Over the Brenner
 The slip of onionskin paper reached Oberperfuss inside Luise's woolen sock.
 At 23:00, Hans sat under the crawlspace rafters with the SSTR-1 humming against his shins. He translated Mayer's notes into four-digit groups, substituting Brooklyn landmarks for the switch tracks, the flak nests, and the ammunition sidings.
 His thumb worked the key in steady, rhythmic bursts:
  TARGET EBBETS FIELD. TWENTY-ONE ASSEMBLIES STATIONARY. TRACKS SEVEN THROUGH ELEVEN. HIGH EXPLOSIVE CARS CENTRAL SPUR. FLAK SUPPRESSION SILL GORGE. EXECUTE IMMEDIATE.
 Bari acknowledged with three short clicks.
 Fourteen hours later, at 13:20, the air raid sirens across Innsbruck began their mechanical wail.
 The sound bounced off the sheer limestone cliffs of the Nordkette, multiplying until the entire valley hummed with the vibration. On the streets below, pedestrians scrambled for cellar grates and concrete blast shelters. Mayer stood in the doorway of a bakery two hundred yards from the freight yards, his service cap pulled down against the soot falling from the railway chimneys.
 High above the southern peaks, where the Brenner road cut into the clouds, the first wave appeared.
 They came in combat boxes of twelve - B-17 Flying Fortresses of the 15th Air Force, trailing white contrails that scored the cold blue sky. The roar of seventy-two Wright Cyclone engines rolled down into the valley like sustained thunder, shaking the cobblestones under Mayer's boots.
 The flak batteries along the Sill Gorge opened up. Black puffs of 88mm shrapnel blossomed in clusters around the lead formation, but the bombers didn't deviate. Their bomb bay doors swung open in unison.
 A second of silence hung over the city. Then the high, tearing whistle began.
 The first stick of five-hundred-pound general-purpose bombs struck the southern throat of the marshaling yards. The earth jumped under Mayer's heels. A line of geysers—black dirt, pulverized ballast, and twisted rails—tore upward two hundred feet into the air.
 Then the second wave dropped their payloads squarely across tracks seven through eleven.
 A red flash split the dust cloud. The forty-car ammunition train Mayer had tagged the night before went up in a chain reaction. The shockwave blew the plate-glass windows out of three blocks of storefronts, showering the street in crystalline shrapnel. A locomotive boiler ruptured with a scream of high-pressure steam, flung three hundred feet sideways into a line of idling coal tenders.
 Secondary explosions rolled across the basin for twenty minutes. Black smoke, thick with the stench of burning cordite and scorched creosote, billowed against the mountainside, blotting out the afternoon sun.
 The Brenner rail link was severed.
 By dusk, the fires were still burning, casting an orange glare over the ruins of the Hauptbahnhof. But the silence that followed was broken by the sirens of military police trucks.
 Mayer walked back toward Anna Niederkircher's pension. At the corner of the Museumstraße, three Kübelwagens blocked the intersection. SS troopers in camouflage smocks and Feldgendarmerie with submachine guns held at low ready had strung concertina wire across the avenue.
 Every officer on the street was being stopped. Three Wehrmacht captains stood against a brick wall, their tunic pockets turned inside out while a Gestapo agent cross-checked their paybooks against a teleprinted master manifest.
 Mayer pulled back into the shadow of an arched entryway.
 The strike had been too accurate. The German transport command knew immediately that the bombers hadn't dropped by radar through cloud cover; they had hit specific tracks with pinpoint intelligence. The city was being sealed. Every German uniform without a verified post in the local garrison would be dragged into Gestapo headquarters at Herrengasse 1 by midnight.
 Mayer reached into his tunic, fingered the forged convalescent pass, and turned down an unlit alley.
 The officer's coat had done its job. If he wore it another twelve hours, it would be his shroud.

 Chapter Thirteen
 Jean Marcel
 In the kitchen stove at Anichstraße 19, the Wehrmacht officer's uniform went into the fire. The coarse gray wool smoldered with a thick, greasy stench before the draft caught it, roaring into white ash.
 Mayer left the apartment before sunrise dressed in a faded civilian cardigan and patched trousers.
 Three streets away, behind a darkened storefront on the Leopoldstraße, sat the radio repair workshop of Robert Moser.
 Moser was forty-two, a slight man with spectacles, pale fingers stained with flux, and an ear ruined by years of tuning heterodynes. In Innsbruck, Moser fixed civilian radios that were legally prohibited from receiving foreign broadcasts; in secret, he supplied shortwave components to the local resistance and forged worker papers for escaped Italian and French internees.
 The shop smelled of burning rosin, zinc, and damp sawdust. Moser barred the front door behind Mayer, led him into the back alcove behind racks of disassembled Telefunken consoles, and pulled an oilcloth packet from an iron strongbox.
 "You're done being a lieutenant," Moser said, speaking in rapid, nervous Tyrolean. "The Gestapo has motorized inspection squads combing the hotels and officers' messes. If you can't show a paybook registered with the local garrison commandant, they put you against the cellar wall."
 He unfolded a stiff, blue cardboard identity card. On the cover was stamped - Service du Travail Obligatoire.
 Inside was Mayer's photograph, lifted from an extra print Franz had brought from the hospital files, stamped with the round seal of the Arbeitsamt Innsbruck.
 "Jean Marcel," Moser said, tapping the card with a grease-stained thumb. "Born in Lyon. Electrician. Assigned to the regional electrical maintenance depot under contract to the Reichsbahn. It gives you clearance to enter transformer substations, industrial switchyards, and factory maintenance tunnels."
 Mayer inspected the forgery. The ink was authentic German bureaucratic blue, the paper properly aged with tea water.
 "My French is rough," Mayer said.
 "Then don't speak it," Moser replied bluntly. "French forced laborers don't make conversation with Germans. If an SS patrol barks at you, you shrug, look stupid, and say - 'Je ne comprends pas, monsieur.' If you have to speak German, speak it with an atrocious French accent. Mix up your genders. Call every officer Herr Feldwebel. They think all Frenchmen are lazy idiots anyway. They won't look twice."
 Moser reached under a bench and pulled out a heavy wooden toolbox with an iron handle. Inside were three heavy screwdrivers, insulated pliers, a volt-ohmmeter, rolls of friction tape, and copper wire.
 Beside the box lay a suit of dark-blue canvas overalls, worn through at the knees and blackened with oil, along with a greasy cloth flat-cap and scarred, steel-toed boots.
 Mayer stripped off his civilian coat and pulled on the overalls. The canvas was stiff with dried industrial grease. He rubbed his hands against a dirty lathe bed, smudging graphite across his palms, then dragged two fingers across his jawline and neck to kill the clean look of an officer's skin.
 He pulled the flat-cap down low, shadowing his eyes.
 "Walk across the room," Moser ordered.
 Mayer took three strides.
 "Stop," Moser snapped. "You're still walking like an officer with a saber at his hip. Head up, shoulders square. That gets you shot by nine o'clock."
 Moser stepped in and shoved Mayer's left shoulder down. "Hunch your back. Drop your chin toward your chest. You're cold, you haven't eaten a decent meal since forty-two, and you hate the Germans who dragged you here from Lyon. When you walk, look at the cobblestones three paces in front of your boots. Never make eye contact with a man in boots."
 Mayer reset his stance. He let his broad shoulders slump forward, dropped his gaze to the floorboards, and picked up the heavy toolbox. The dead weight of the iron tools dragged his right shoulder lower. He shuffled forward with a tired, mechanical trudge.
 Moser watched him closely, then nodded once.
 "That will do," Moser said. "Now take the box and walk out the front. There's a transformer station near the rail yards that needs inspection. See if you can get past the gate."
 Mayer slipped the Arbeitsausweis into the breast pocket of his greasy overalls, picked up the toolbox, and stepped out onto the Leopoldstraße.
 Fifty yards down the street, an SS checkpoint had barricaded the tram tracks with sandbags. Two sentries in field-gray coats were tearing through the canvas bags of an elderly farmer's cart.
 Mayer didn't slow his pace. He dragged his boots through the slush, eyes fixed on the muddy water in the gutter, the heavy toolbox bumping against his thigh with a dull metallic thud.
 "Halt," a sentry barked, stepping into his path with a Kar98k rifle held across his chest.
 Mayer stopped. He didn't look at the soldier's face. He let his jaw hang slightly slack, slumped his shoulders, and fumbled into his overalls with blackened, greasy fingers.
 "Pardon, m'sieur," Mayer mumbled, his voice flat, thick with an exaggerated French nasal drawl. "Ausweis. Travailleur."
 The German barely glanced at the blue card. He smelled the dirty grease on Mayer's jacket, saw the scuffed boots and the heavy screwdrivers in the wooden tray, and curled his lip in disgust.
 "Französisches Schwein," the sentry muttered, waving his gloved hand dismissively toward the open road. "Weitergehen. Los."
 Mayer took his pass back with a clumsy, ducked nod. "Merci, patron."
 He shuffled through the sandbag gap, keeping his eyes on the cobblestones, heading west toward the industrial rail sidings of Kematen.

 Chapter Fourteen
 Stollen Four
 The tunnel mouth at Kematen had been blasted directly into the sheer limestone base of the mountain, shielded from aerial photography by heavy camouflage netting and evergreen boughs.
 A narrow-gauge rail spur led into the rock. Two SS guards in black helmets and rubberized coats stood by the reinforced steel blast gates, checking work badges under the yellow glare of an electric work lamp.
 Mayer shuffled forward in the line of forty foreign workers—Poles, Czechs, and Frenchmen with hollow faces and greasy canvas smocks. His wooden toolbox hung from his right hand, dragging his shoulder down.
 The SS guard took Mayer's blue Arbeitsausweis, glanced at the photo, and checked the red maintenance authorization slip Robert Moser had forged two days prior - Elektro-Instandsetzung - Stollen 4 (Electrical Maintenance - Gallery 4).
 "Tool check," the guard barked.
 Mayer dropped the box onto the plank table, popped the latch, and stood back with his eyes lowered. The guard stirred the screwdrivers and wire coils with a wooden truncheon, found nothing, and thrust the blue card back into Mayer's chest.
 "Reingehen," the guard said.
 The tunnel interior was cool, damp, and smelled of drilling sludge, machine cutting oil, and ozone.
 Incandescent bulbs strung along heavy rubber cables hung from the rock ceiling every twenty yards, casting long, swaying shadows across the concrete floor. Deep inside the mountain, the cavern widened into an assembly gallery nearly three hundred yards long. Air compressors thudded in the distance, their rhythmic exhaust echoing off the raw rock walls.
 Here, safe from Allied carpet bombing, the Reich had dispersed the manufacturing lines for the Messerschmitt Me 262—the world's first operational jet fighter.
 Allied command in Caserta was convinced that hundreds of these twin-engine jets were being secretly assembled in alpine caverns, ready to surge into the sky and wipe the Fifteenth Air Force from the air.
 Mayer pulled a test-meter from his box, climbed an aluminum ladder near a central power distribution panel, and began unscrewing a junction plate. From eight feet off the floor, he had an unobstructed view down the entire length of Gallery 4.
 The shark-nosed fuselages sat in rows on steel assembly jigs. He began counting.
 One. Two. Three. Twenty-four airframes in various stages of completion.
 He climbed down, moved to an overhead lighting run twenty yards further down the track, and reset his ladder near the wing-fitting station. He looked inside the engine nacelles slung beneath the swept wings.
 They were empty.
 There were no Junkers Jumo 004 axial-flow turbojets anywhere on the floor. Where the high-precision turbines were supposed to sit, bare aluminum cowlings rested on wooden cradles. Critical copper wiring harnesses were missing; lead weights hung from the tail assemblies to balance the unfinished frames.
 A German civilian foreman in a brown smock, carrying a steel clipboard, walked past the base of Mayer's ladder. A younger technical sergeant from the Luftwaffe walked beside him, arguing in hushed, furious tones.
 "We have nineteen airframes ready for pre-flight installation," the sergeant said, pointing his pencil at a row of unpainted wings. "Where are the turbines from Regensburg?"
 "Blown to scrap at the Salzburg junction," the foreman spat, not slowing down. "The train never crossed the river. We have no turbine rotors, no hydraulic fluid, and the night shift from the labor barracks stole forty meters of rubber fuel line to make shoe soles. If Berlin wants twenty jets this week, they'll have to push them out the tunnel by hand."
 Mayer adjusted his screwdriver against a dead bolt, committing the words to memory.
 There was no hidden armada. The underground factory was a hollow shell, strangled by the destruction of the Reich's rail network. The airframes were metal statues, marooned in the rock without engines, without fuel, and without a rail link to transport them to an airfield.
 At 18:00, the shift siren wailed through the cavern.
 Mayer packed his tools, latched the wooden box, and shuffled out into the cold mountain drizzle with the evening labor crew. He caught the local commuter train back toward Inzing, keeping his eyes on the floorboards.
 By midnight, the message was coded and transmitted from the Oberperfuss attic:
  KEMATEN PRODUCTION NEGLIGIBLE. TWENTY-FOUR AIRFRAMES INCOMPLETE. ZERO JET ENGINES AVAILABLE. FACTORY GROUNDED BY LACK OF RAIL LOGISTICS. DISMISS THREAT.
 Bari received it without a repeat request. Across Italy, the strategic calculators at Allied Air Command crossed the Kematen jet threat off their target ledgers.

 Chapter Fifteen
 The Redoubt That Never Was
 In mid-April, the snow began to pull back from the lower slopes, leaving the meadows black and greasy with meltwater.
 Reports from Supreme Headquarters Allied Expeditionary Force in Paris and the 15th Army Group in Caserta were fixated on a single nightmare - the Alpenfestung—the National Redoubt. German propaganda claimed that elite SS formations, armed with advanced weaponry and subterranean supply dumps carved into the granite, were preparing to retreat into the Bavarian and Austrian Alps to wage a guerrilla war that could drag on for years.
 Fred Mayer and Franz Weber spent five days moving through the valleys that were supposed to form the redoubt's impenetrable core.
 Weber wore civilian loden trousers and an old woolen hunting jacket, carrying an axe over his shoulder like a farmer clearing winter deadfall. Mayer walked ten paces behind him in his oil-stained blue overalls, the wooden toolbox banging against his thigh, his French worker's pass pinned inside his shirt.
 They scouted the mouth of the Zillertal, the Stubai approach, and the narrow defiles flanking the Brenner highway.
 Where Allied intelligence maps showed fortified perimeter belts and heavy concrete pillboxes, Mayer found only log barricades thrown together by the Volkssturm—conscripted teenagers in oversized helmets and elderly men with First World War Mannlicher rifles.
 At a supply depot near Jenbach, where rumors claimed underground fuel reserves were buried beneath the rock, Mayer watched through binoculars from a pine ridge. Three rusted tanker trucks sat on wooden blocks without tires. A half-track with a blown cylinder head was being towed into a barn by a team of two draft horses.
 On the third afternoon, they stopped at a forestry shed above the Sill Gorge.
 Below them, a motorized convoy of the 6th SS Mountain Division sat stalled along the road. The vehicles were not fighting machines; they were wood-gas civilian trucks, delivery vans, and requisitioned city buses loaded with filing cabinets, mimeograph machines, and administrative clerks from Munich. German soldiers sat on the running boards boiling potatoes over open fires fueled by wooden ammo crates.
 "Look at them," Franz muttered, lowering his field glasses. "Caserta thinks there are twenty divisions dug into the granite with five years of rations."
 Mayer spat onto the wet pine needles. "They don't have enough gas to drive fifty kilometers. If they tried to fight in these gorges for a month, they'd starve to death."
 "There are no bunkers," Weber said. "No artillery emplacements in the rock. No underground hospitals. It's just Goebbels on the radio, shouting into the dark while the clerks run away with the paper."
 That night, Mayer slipped back up the trail to Oberperfuss.
 In the barn crawlspace, Hans Wijnberg sat under the eaves with his knees pulled up to his chin to ward off the damp chill. The SSTR-1 rested on the butter churn, its battery terminals connected to a truck battery Weber's cousin had hauled up from Inzing.
 Mayer laid a hand on Hans's shoulder. "Take a message."
 Hans pulled his scratch pad close, his pencil poised.
 "To Victor," Mayer dictated quietly. "Alpenfestung is a complete myth. Repeat - no fortifications, no underground supply dumps, no heavy armor concentrations in North Tyrol. Defenses consist of scattered Volkssturm road obstacles and demoralized transit echelons out of fuel. Recommend Allied armored columns advance without delay. Do not slow for siege operations."
 Hans translated the plain text into Brooklyn cipher, shifting the names of the valleys into New York cross streets, then began working the brass key.
  Dit-dit-dah... dah-dit-dit...
 The dots and dashes cut through the Tyrolean night, bouncing off the ionosphere down to the receiver antennas at Bari.
 Two days later, the report was on the desk of General William J. Donovan in Washington and forwarded directly to Allied command. The myth of the Alpine Redoubt, which had shaped Allied strategy for months, was dead.

 Chapter Sixteen
 Safehouse at Rum
 The safehouse was a shuttered hunting lodge above Rum, nestled against the foot of the Nordkette where the pine forest met the upper pastures.
 Inside, blackout blankets were nailed across the window frames. On the timber table sat a kerosene lantern, a porcelain teapot of bitter chicory brew, and a topographical map of the Innsbruck municipal district.
 Dr. Karl Gruber stood by the dark hearth. He was thirty-five, tall, broad-shouldered, an electrical engineer with sharp gray eyes and the quiet, measured bearing of a man accustomed to running industrial plants under the eyes of the Gestapo. Gruber was the chief of the O5—the Austrian resistance movement—across the entire Tyrol.
 Franz Weber stepped in first, followed by Mayer, who still smelled of coal grease from the rail yards.
 Gruber looked at Mayer's blue mechanic's overalls, the scuffed hands, and the wiry frame. He did not extend his hand immediately.
 "Franz tells me you are an American lieutenant," Gruber said in clean, educated German.
 "Fred Mayer. OSS," Mayer replied, unbuttoning his jacket to show the small brass compass sewn into the lining of his collar. "And I don't have time to fence, Herr Doktor. The American Seventh Army is grinding through Bavaria. In two weeks, maybe three, their lead combat commands will hit the pass at Scharnitz and roll down into the Inn Valley."
 Gruber pulled a wooden chair out and sat down, gesturing toward the table. "Sit down, Lieutenant."
 Mayer sat. Franz remained standing by the heavy door, watching the dark hallway.
 "We have two thousand men pledged in Innsbruck and the surrounding valleys," Gruber said, his voice low and deliberate. "Railway dispatchers, telegraph operators, city police, and Austrian soldiers in the local garrison who will turn their rifles on the SS the moment an order is given. We have arms caches in the basements of the university clinic and hidden in the limestone caves above the Hungerburg."
 "What don't you have?" Mayer asked.
 "Fuel," Gruber said bluntly. "And control over your bombers."
 Gruber leaned forward, placing both palms flat on the map over Innsbruck.
 "If your Seventh Army arrives expecting the National Redoubt," Gruber said, "they will bring three divisions of heavy artillery and the Fifteenth Air Force. They will shell the bridges, drop five-hundred-pound bombs into the residential quarters, and turn Innsbruck into another Monte Cassino. We do not want our city destroyed to liberate ruins."
 "My bombers are only hitting targets I give them," Mayer said. "And the Seventh Army doesn't want to fight for every cellar if they don't have to."
 "Under the Moscow Declaration," Gruber countered, "Austria is promised restoration as an independent state only if we prove we contributed to our own liberation. We intend to take this city ourselves. We will disarm the Wehrmacht garrison, seize the provincial government at the Landhaus, and broadcast the liberation from the radio transmitter on the Adolf-Hitler-Platz before your tanks cross the Inn."
 Mayer studied the engineer's face. Gruber wasn't a reckless romantic; he was calculating the postwar future with the precision of a surveyor.
 "If you move too early," Mayer warned, "the SS will bring motorized units down from the Brenner and shoot you against the nearest wall."
 "We will not move too early," Gruber replied. "We move when the Americans are close enough that the German commanders know the war is over. But to mobilize our squads, we need gasoline. Every civilian vehicle is dry. Gauleiter Hofer has requisitioned all the fuel reserves in the district for the Party leadership."
 Mayer thought of Robert Moser's network of rail contacts and the fuel depots he had scouted at the freight sidings.
 "I can get you fuel drums," Mayer said. "And I have a direct radio link to OSS headquarters in Bari. When you are ready to move, I radio the Seventh Army to halt artillery strikes against the city center. But you deliver the garrison. If one German battery opens fire on the American lead scouts from the valley slopes, they will blanket the basin with white phosphorus."
 Gruber extended his hand across the table. His grip was dry and hard.
 "We deliver the city, Lieutenant," Gruber said. "You keep the artillery silent."
 Franz Weber checked his pocket watch. "It's past curfew. We need to get back across the river."
 Mayer stood up and pulled his flat-cap down over his forehead. "Stay in touch through Moser's shop. When the time comes, don't miss."

 Chapter Seventeen
 Raid on Anichstraße
 On the morning of April 18, Robert Moser did not open his radio shop on the Leopoldstraße.
 By noon, word passed through the railway couriers - two men in leather trench coats had dragged Moser out of his workshop through the alley. The officer in charge was Gestapo Kriminalsekretär Walter Güttner, a veteran interrogator transferred from Vienna to crush the Tyrolean underground.
 The Gestapo had not stumbled onto the network by chance. Karl Niederwanger, an informant tied to local black-market and resistance channels, had been arrested and turned, providing the Gestapo with specific leads on foreign workers and safe addresses across Innsbruck.
 On the night of April 20—Adolf Hitler's fifty-sixth birthday—Innsbruck lay under a total blackout curfew.
 In the third-floor apartment at Anichstraße 19, Fred Mayer sat at the kitchen table in his blue mechanic's overalls. With him were Franz Weber's two sisters - twenty-two-year-old Eva, who carried dispatches up the mountain, and Margarethe Kelderer, whose home served as the urban relay point. A single candle burned inside a ceramic mug to shield its light from the window.
 "Moser is in Herrengasse," Mayer said quietly. "Niederwanger was seen with the SD yesterday. This apartment is compromised. We clear out tonight."
 Eva leaned forward, her face pale. "Franz meets us at the river footbridge at one."
 Before they could gather their papers, heavy boots thudded up the linoleum stairs outside.
 There was no warning knock. A master key scraped in the lock, and the heavy door was kicked open against the wall. Four Gestapo agents in dark civilian trench coats flooded the apartment, Walther PPK pistols drawn, their flashlight beams blinding the dark room.
 "Keine Bewegung! Hände hoch!"
 Mayer stood up slowly, raising his hands, his blue French worker card already palmed in his left fingers. Two agents slammed him face-first onto the pine table, grinding his cheek into the wood. His right wrist was wrenched behind his back until the joint popped, and cold iron ratcheted tight around his wrists. Another agent seized Eva by the arm, forcing her into a corner chair.
 Walter Güttner stepped slowly through the shattered entrance, his gray fedora casting a shadow across his eyes, his leather coat unbuttoned. He glanced at Margarethe Kelderer, who stood frozen near the hallway door, clutching her hands against her apron.
 Margarethe did not attempt to run. She was a mother with young children in Oberperfuss; fleeing down a fire escape meant immediate execution for her family. When Güttner barked an order to secure the premises, Margarethe spoke with desperate, calculated composure. She kept her eyes lowered, pleading that she was merely a simple housewife renting rooms, begging the Gestapo officer for permission to return to Oberperfuss to settle her minor children before being taken to headquarters for questioning.
 Güttner, confident that the primary conspirators—the foreign electrician and Eva Weber—were securely in irons, made a fatal bureaucratic miscalculation. He granted her a temporary arrest deferral (Haftaufschub) until the following morning.
 The moment the Gestapo dragged Mayer and Eva down the stairwell, Margarethe wasted no time. She packed no clothes; she slipped out through the courtyard gate, avoided the Innsbruck rail checkpoints entirely on foot, and crossed the snow-choked alpine ridges into Bavaria. She found refuge in a Catholic convent in Munich-Pasing, where the nuns kept her hidden in the cellars until the arrival of the American army.
 Down on the street, Mayer and Eva were thrown into the rear of a black Gestapo van. The iron doors slammed shut, and the engine roared as they sped toward the interrogation cellars of Herrengasse 1.

 Chapter Eighteen
 The Water Tub in Herrengasse
 The basement of Herrengasse 1 was cold, damp, and smelled of wet lime, carbolic acid, and sewage.
 They separated them in the lower corridor. Two guards dragged Eva Weber toward the women's holding cells at the end of the hall; within forty-eight hours, her transfer order was stamped - Reichenau transit camp on the eastern edge of Innsbruck.
 In the cell next to Mayer's, a man groaned behind an iron-studded door—a wet, rattling sound that went on through the night without stopping. It was Robert Moser. Güttner and his squad had beaten him with steel rods to break the resistance contact chain. By the morning of April 23, the groaning stopped. Moser died on the damp straw of internal hemorrhaging, his mouth clamped shut, taking the location of the Oberperfuss radio to his grave.
 In the center of Mayer's cell, an iron hook hung from a ring bolted into the ceiling arch.
 Walter Güttner took off his suit jacket, folded it neatly over the back of an iron chair, and rolled up his shirt sleeves. Beside him stood Dr. Primbs, the police physician, wearing a rubber apron and holding a brass pocket watch on a chain.
 For the first twelve hours, Mayer clung tenaciously to his cover. When Güttner demanded names, Mayer spoke only broken, nasal French - "Jean Marcel... électricien... je ne sais rien..."
 The Gestapo stripped him. During the physical examination, the interrogators discovered he was circumcised—an immediate, damning anomaly for a supposedly Catholic French laborer from Lyon.
 Two Gestapo men jerked Mayer's arms behind his back, ratcheted his wrists into heavy steel handcuffs, and looped a hemp rope through the chain. They tossed the loose end of the rope over the ceiling hook.
 "Ein letztes Mal," Güttner said quietly, his voice level. "Who dropped you? Where is the radio?"
 Mayer shook his head, spitting blood onto the flagstones. "Je ne sais rien... Jean Marcel..."
 Güttner nodded to the guards.
 They hauled on the rope.
 Mayer's arms were hoisted straight up behind his back, wrenching his shoulder joints out of their sockets as his feet left the floor. The pain was immediate and blinding, tearing through the pectoral muscles and ligaments. Mayer gasped, his head snapping forward.
 They lowered him into a galvanized zinc tub filled to the rim with freezing water.
 Hands shoved his head under. The water rushed into his nostrils and throat, cutting off oxygen while the weight of his suspended body pulled his dislocated shoulders taut. When his chest began to convulse, they yanked the rope, pulling his head out of the water.
 Mayer choked, vomiting water and bile onto the rim of the tub.
 Dr. Primbs stepped forward, two fingers pressing against the carotid artery in Mayer's neck, checking the seconds on his pocket watch.
 "Pulse is one-forty," Primbs said impassively. "He can take another round."
 Güttner leaned in close to Mayer's ear, the smell of his tobacco sharp against the reek of the cellar. "You're not French, Marcel. Your hands have calluses from tools, but you move like a soldier. Where is the transmitter?"
 Mayer's body convulsed; his nose was shattered, his left cheek crushed, his lungs burning with bloody water.
 He knew the French cover was dead. If the Gestapo concluded he was a German-Jewish traitor or a local Austrian partisan, they would execute him against the cellar wall before sunrise.
 The only way to survive—and the only way to protect Hans Wijnberg and the radio at the Schatz farm—was to alter the game entirely.
 When they dragged his head from the water tub again, Mayer looked up through his swollen, blackened eyes. He spit a mouthful of dark blood onto Güttner's polished shoe and spoke in clear, unaccented German:
 "Stop wasting your time, Güttner."
 The interrogators froze.
 "My name is Frederick Mayer," he rasped, his voice raw but completely steady. "I am an American soldier. Serial number 0-132-4752."
 Güttner stepped back, his eyes narrowing in shock.
 "Where is the transmitter?" Güttner barked. "Where is the rest of your unit?"
 Mayer met the Gestapo man's gaze without flinching, delivering the deliberate lie he had prepared in the dark:
 "There is no radio," Mayer lied. "I dropped blind onto the Sulztalferner from an Eighth Air Force bomber two months ago. My radio operator's chute failed over the glacier—he broke his neck on the ice. The equipment container fell into a crevasse and was lost. I have operated in this district alone. If you hang a captured American officer as a spy when the Seventh Army is forty miles away, you will hang from the same beam before May is over."
 Güttner stared at him for a long, heavy minute.
 Realizing they were holding a high-value American operative rather than an ordinary partisan, Güttner ordered the torture halted immediately and had Mayer dragged upstairs to Gestapo Chief Werner Hilliges.

 Chapter Nineteen
 Gallows Warning
 They dragged him up the narrow basement stairs by his armpits, his boots leaving wet streaks on the stone risers.
 The upstairs office had a high plaster ceiling, tall windows with blackout paper, and a framed portrait of Adolf Hitler hanging crookedly behind a mahogany desk. An iron radiator hissed in the corner.
 Güttner shoved Mayer into a straight-backed wooden chair facing the desk. Behind the blotter sat Gestapo Kriminalrat Werner Hilliges, a career police official with slicked-back gray hair and gold-rimmed spectacles, smoking a cigarette through a horn holder.
 Güttner wiped his hands on a damp towel. "He claims he is an American officer."
 Hilliges took the cigarette holder from his mouth, looking at Mayer's split lip, the swelling shutting his left eye, and the torn overalls soaked in bloody water.
 "Your serial number?" Hilliges asked in dry, bureaucratic German.
 Mayer spat a clot of dark red onto the hardwood floor. He answered in English - "Mayer, Frederick. First Lieutenant, United States Army. Serial number 0-132-4752."
 Hilliges leaned back in his leather chair. "You have no uniform. You were arrested in civilian clothes, in the company of known Austrian traitors, carrying forged French identity papers. Under the Führer's Commando Order, you are not a prisoner of war. You are a spy. The penalty is death by hanging within twenty-four hours."
 Mayer let out a dry, rattling cough that ended in a raw chuckle.
 "Look out that window," Mayer said, switching to the hard, unvarnished German of southwest Germany. "What do you see?"
 Hilliges didn't move.
 "You see clouds," Mayer continued, his voice steady despite the jagged pain in his ribs. "Behind those clouds, the American Seventh Army is crossing the Danube. The French First Army is pushing through the Black Forest. In two weeks—maybe ten days—American tanks will be parked outside the Landhaus. The war is over, Herr Kriminalrat. It's finished. It died at Stalingrad, and it's being buried in Berlin."
 Güttner stepped up beside the chair, raising his truncheon. "Shut your mouth."
 "Let him talk, Walter," Hilliges said, his eyes fixed on Mayer.
 "You want to talk about commando orders?" Mayer said, leaning toward the desk, ignoring the throb in his dislocated shoulder. "Every American combat unit carries lists of Gestapo and SD personnel. Your names are on them. If I die in this cellar, General Patch will not treat this district as a conquered province. He will treat it as a crime scene. Every officer in this building—you, Güttner, your doctor with the pocket watch—will be on a gallows at Landsberg before the summer is out."
 The room was dead quiet except for the hiss of the radiator. Güttner's hand hovered above the desk, the lead truncheon resting loose in his grip.
 Hilliges tapped his cigarette ash into a bronze tray. "Where is the rest of your team?"
 Mayer met the German's eyes without flinching.
 "There is no team," Mayer lied. "I dropped blind from an Eighth Air Force bomber three weeks ago. My radio operator's chute failed over the glacier. He broke his neck on the ice. The container with the transceiver fell into a crevasse. I walked down the mountain alone, bought those French papers off a pimp in Landeck, and was trying to make contact with local anti-fascists when your idiots kicked in the door."
 Hilliges adjusted his spectacles. "You expect us to believe an American lieutenant operated across North Tyrol for two months without a radio?"
 "Check the reports," Mayer said. "Have your radio direction-finders picked up a single illegal transmission from inside Innsbruck in the last week? No. Because there is no radio. It's just me."
 Hilliges stared at Mayer for a long, heavy minute.
 The Gestapo chief knew the western front had collapsed. He knew the Bavarian railway corridors were severed, and that Berlin was surrounded by Soviet artillery. The men in this room were no longer fighting for the Thousand-Year Reich; they were calculating how many years they would spend at the end of a rope.
 Hilliges crushed out his cigarette. He looked up at Güttner.
 "Put him in cell nine," Hilliges said. "Solitary. Give him clean water and have Primbs reset his shoulder."
 Güttner blinked. "Herr Kriminalrat, the interrogation—"
 "The interrogation is concluded," Hilliges cut him off, his voice flat and hard. "Get him out of my office."
 Two guards took Mayer under the arms and pulled him toward the door. As they reached the hallway, Mayer looked back over his shoulder at Hilliges.
 "Good decision, Herr Kriminalrat," Mayer said. "You might live to see forty-six."

 Chapter Twenty
 We Are Not Leaving
 The mountain rain came down in gray sheets, turning the dirt tracks of Oberperfuss into ruts of black mud.
 In the kitchen of the Weber farmhouse, Aloisia Weber sat with her hands clasped in her lap, her knuckles white. Franz stood by the small window, peeling back the blackout curtain an inch to scan the switchbacks leading up from the valley floor.
 Margarethe's message had reached them at dawn through an alpine forestry courier - Fred and Eva were taken at Anichstraße. Moser is dead in Herrengasse.
 The back door creaked open. Alois Schatz slipped into the kitchen, his loden cape soaked with rain. Behind him came Hans Wijnberg, wearing a rough civilian wool sweater, his eyes bloodshot from days spent in the darkness of the Schatz barn.
 "The Gestapo has had them for five days," Franz said, his voice ragged with exhaustion. "Güttner uses steel rods and the water tub. No man lasts forty-eight hours in that cellar. Eva is twenty-two years old, Hans. She doesn't know how to resist professional interrogators."
 Aloisia looked up, her voice trembling. "They will bring the trucks up the mountain, Hansl. Pack your machine. Franz will take you up into the high barns above Ranggen. You can cross the ridge toward the Ötztal."
 Hans didn't move toward the door. He walked to the kitchen table and pulled out a chair.
 "If Fred had broken," Hans said quietly, his Dutch accent clipping each syllable short, "the Gestapo trucks would have been here on the night of the twentieth. They would have been here within six hours of kicking down the door on Anichstraße."
 Franz turned from the window. "You don't know that. They might be waiting to see who visits the house."
 "No," Hans said. "Güttner doesn't wait when there's an illegal transmitter on the loose. A high-frequency radio is the highest priority target in the district. If Fred gave them Oberperfuss, there would be fifty SS men in this yard tearing the shingles off the roof."
 Hans leaned his forearms on the table, looking Franz in the eyes.
 "Fred hasn't broken. He is taking the beating so we can stay on the air. And Eva won't give you up, Franz. She knows what they do to deserters."
 Aloisia crossed herself, a tear tracing the deep wrinkles beside her nose. "My poor girl..."
 "If I smash the radio and run into the snow," Hans said, "everything Fred has done for the last two months is finished. Bari goes blind. The Seventh Army advances without knowing what's on the Brenner. And when the Americans take this valley, they will find Fred's body in a lime pit at Herrengasse."
 Hans stood up. He looked at Alois Schatz.
 "I have a transmission schedule at 08:30," Hans said. "We go back to your attic."
 Ten minutes later, Hans was back in the crawlspace behind the rye straw at the Schatz farm.
 The air was freezing, smelling of cow manure from the stalls below and damp larch wood. He sat on his sheepskin pelt, plugged the quartz crystal into the oscillator socket, and connected the lead from the clothesline outside.
 He looked at his pocket watch. 08:29.
 He slipped the heavy bakelite headphones over his ears. He switched on the receiver; the high, steady hiss of the ionosphere washed through his head.
 At 08:30:00, he took the brass telegraph key between his thumb and index finger.
  Dit-dit-dit... dah-dit-dah...
 He used the emergency double-transposition silk cipher, tapping the keys with rapid, mechanical precision:
  GREENUP TO VICTOR. MAYER CAPTURED INNSBRUCK APRIL TWENTY. EVA WEBER CAPTURED. MOSER DEAD OR COMPROMISED. REPEAT - MAYER CAPTURED.
 Hans paused. He drew in a long, cold breath of barn air, smelling the dust and the timber.
 His fingers went back to the key:
  WEBER AND WIJNBERG SECURE. ENEMY UNAWARE OF TRANSMITTER LOCATION. ATTIC REMAINS FULLY OPERATIONAL. SEND FURTHER TARGET INSTRUCTIONS. WE ARE NOT LEAVING.
 He released the contact and switched to receive.
 For forty seconds, there was only static. Hans closed his eyes, his thumb resting lightly on the cold brass lever.
 Then, faint and sharp through the mountain noise, Station Victor answered from southern Italy:
  VICTOR TO GREENUP. MESSAGE ACKNOWLEDGED. DIRECTIVE - MAINTAIN WATCH. AIR RECONNAISSANCE DIVERTED TO YOUR SECTOR. STAND BY.
 Hans pulled off the headset and rested his forehead against the olive-drab steel lid of the transceiver. Below him, the cows shifted in their stalls, the floorboards groaning in the morning quiet.
 They were still alive.

 Chapter Twenty-One
 The Matull Bluff
 In the second-floor administrative wing of Herrengasse 1, Gestapo Kriminalrat Werner Hilliges held a fresh five-by-seven photograph under the green glass shade of his desk lamp.
 The print showed Fred Mayer against a whitewashed basement wall - left eye swollen purple and shut, nose broken, lips split, dark blood crusted across his collarbones.
 The door opened. Two guards escorted Hermann Matull into the office.
 Matull was an OSS agent who had been dropped into Austria weeks earlier on a separate mission and captured near Landeck. The Gestapo kept him in a clean second-floor cell, hoping to use him as a radio pawn to feed disinformation back to Bari. Matull wore his own rumpled civilian trousers and had lost twenty pounds, but his mind was sharp.
 Hilliges slid the photograph across the green blotter.
 "Look at this man," Hilliges said. "Do you know him?"
 Matull picked up the print. He looked at the bruised, battered face. He recognized Mayer instantly from the staging base at Brindisi. He saw the dislocated shoulder drooping awkwardly in the frame, the raw abrasions on the wrists from the Strappado rope.
 Matull did not look up. He let out a low, theatrical whistle, shaking his head with an expression of stunned horror.
 "Blimey!" Matull said in English, then switched to German so Hilliges wouldn't miss a syllable. "What have you done to him?"
 Hilliges leaned forward. "He claims he is a first lieutenant."
 Matull threw the photograph back onto the blotter with disgust. "A lieutenant? Are you insane? If that's what he told you, he's laughing at you from the grave. That is Frederick Mayer. He's one of the chief directors of American strategic operations in the Mediterranean theatre. He reports directly to General Donovan in Washington."
 Hilliges's jaw went slack. The cigarette between his fingers burned down to the yellow paper.
 "A director?" Hilliges's voice had lost its dry, bureaucratic chill.
 "He runs the entire infiltration network for the alpine sector," Matull lied, his tone dripping with cold condescension. "Why do you think he was in Innsbruck himself? To take notes on boxcars? He came to negotiate the regional capitulation. And you hung him from a meat hook and drowned him in a tub."
 Matull leaned over the desk, pointing a finger at the Gestapo chief's chest.
 "When General Patch's tanks cross the Inn—and they will be here by Tuesday—the first thing the Seventh Army counter-intelligence corps will ask for is Frederick Mayer. If he's dead, or if he's an invalid, you and every man in this building will be swinging from the lampposts on the Maria-Theresien-Straße before sunset. The Americans don't negotiate when you murder their command staff."
 Hilliges stood up so fast his chair screeched against the parquet. He didn't look at Matull. He looked at the door.
 "Guards," Hilliges barked. "Take the prisoner back to his room."
 Ten minutes later, the heavy iron bolt on cell nine screeched open.
 Mayer was sitting on the damp straw, his back against the limestone wall, his dislocated shoulder throbbing in time with his heartbeat. He braced himself, expecting Güttner with the lead truncheon.
 Instead, two orderlies entered carrying a zinc tub of steaming hot water, two bars of real tallow soap, and a stack of clean white towels. Behind them came Dr. Primbs, carrying a medical bag and a bottle of surgical iodine, his hands visibly shaking.
 "Herr Mayer," Primbs stammered, avoiding Mayer's swollen eye. "Please. Allow me to examine your shoulder."
 Mayer didn't move. He watched the doctor set the medical kit on the stool. "What happened to 'Marcel'?"
 "A regrettable misunderstanding," Primbs murmured, taking Mayer's right arm gently at the elbow. "Deep breath, please."
 Primbs braced his foot against the bench, gripped the forearm, and yanked with a sudden, practiced snap.
 The joint popped back into its socket with a dull thud. Mayer hissed through his clenched teeth, white sparks exploding behind his eyes, but the tearing pressure in his chest eased immediately.
 "Hot water," Primbs said, gesturing toward the tub. "A razor. Please clean yourself."
 An hour later, Werner Hilliges returned. In his arms he carried a fresh change of clothes - a charcoal-gray three-piece wool suit tailored in Innsbruck, a crisp white cotton shirt with a stiff collar, a silk necktie, and a pair of polished black leather Oxfords.
 "Courtesy of the provincial administration," Hilliges said, his voice quiet, almost deferential. "Your presence has been requested."
 Mayer washed the dried blood from his ears and neck, the hot water stinging the raw cuts across his face. He pulled the white shirt over his bruised ribs and buttoned the dark wool vest. His reflection in the small shaving glass showed a face beaten half to pulp, but beneath the charcoal lapels, he looked formidable.
 "Requested by who?" Mayer asked, knotting the tie with steady fingers.
 Hilliges stepped aside to let him through the iron doorway.
 "Gauleiter Franz Hofer," Hilliges said. "His car is waiting in the courtyard."

 Chapter Twenty-Two
 Dinner at Gut Lachhof
 The black Mercedes-Benz 770 rolled east along the south bank of the Inn, its blue blackout headlamps cutting narrow slits in the fog.
 Mayer sat in the rear leather seat, flanked by two armed Gestapo men who stared straight ahead, neither touching their holsters nor speaking. Beyond Hall in Tirol, the staff car turned south, climbing a narrow paved road through pine woods to Kleinvolderberg until it passed through the iron gates of Gut Lachhof.
 The country estate of Gauleiter Franz Hofer looked like an oversized alpine manor - dark cedar gables, whitewashed masonry, and carved wooden balconies banked with rhododendrons. SS guards with assault rifles stood at the portico, but they made no move to inspect the car; the driver merely flashed an administrative pass and killed the engine.
 Mayer stepped out onto the gravel. His boots were polished leather, but beneath the charcoal wool trousers, his knees were stiff and his broken ribs sent sharp knives of pain through his diaphragm with every breath.
 Werner Hilliges guided him through a heavy oak vestibule into the main dining salon.
 The contrast with the Herrengasse cellar was almost obscene. A bronze chandelier cast warm light across a long mahogany table set with porcelain china, crystal wine glasses, and silver cutlery. A tiled stove radiated dry, cedar-scented heat.
 At the head of the table sat Gauleiter Franz Hofer. He was forty-two, sharp-nosed, with slick brown hair and a narrow mustache, wearing a high-collared Party tunic stripped of gold braid. To his right sat his wife, her hands folded rigidly in her lap. To his left sat a tall, aristocratic man in an expensive gray diplomatic suit - Dr. Rudolf Rahn, the German Ambassador to Mussolini's Salò Republic and Hitler's plenipotentiary to northern Italy.
 Hofer stood up slowly as Mayer entered. His eyes traveled from Mayer's tailored lapels to the ruined landscape of his face—the blackened left eye, the crooked, swollen nose, the split lip.
 "Herr Mayer," Hofer said. His voice was polite, formal, the accent of an Innsbruck shopkeeper who had climbed into imperial power. "Please. Join us."
 Mayer did not smile. He pulled back a heavy oak chair opposite Rahn and sat down, his movements stiff.
 A servant in a white apron moved in immediately, pouring mineral water into a crystal goblet. Mayer ignored the water. He leaned his forearms against the linen cloth, his bruised knuckles resting beside the silver soup spoon.
 "You eat well for a city that has three days of flour left, Gauleiter," Mayer said in German.
 Hofer's jaw tightened, but Ambassador Rahn raised a manicured hand, smiling thinly.
 "We are realistic men, Herr Mayer," Rahn said, speaking in the cultured, melodic cadence of the diplomatic corps. "The war has reached its conclusion. The Führer remains in Berlin, but the communications from the Reich Chancellery ceased twenty-four hours ago. We know the American Seventh Army has entered Bavaria. The French First Army is driving from the west."
 "Then why are we having dinner?" Mayer asked.
 Hofer leaned forward, his hands resting on the edge of the table. "I have fifteen thousand men under arms in this Gau. Mountain troops, Waffen-SS rear echelons, police regiments, and three battalions of Volkssturm. If I order them into the side valleys, they can hold the Brenner approaches for months. Every bridge along the Inn will be detonated. The railway tunnels will be collapsed with dynamite. Your Seventh Army will have to dig us out of the rock with artillery."
 Mayer looked at Hofer through his one open eye.
 "You don't have fifteen thousand men, Hofer," Mayer said, dropping the title entirely. "You have three thousand demoralized deserters waiting for an excuse to throw their rifles into the river, and twelve thousand schoolboys whose mothers will shoot you if you send them against Sherman tanks. You have no gasoline. You have no artillery ammunition. And if you drop one bridge over the Inn, the Fifteenth Air Force will turn this valley into a ditch of burning brick."
 Silence fell over the salon. Frau Hofer looked down at her plate, her fingers trembling against her linen napkin.
 "What is it you want, Mayer?" Hofer asked, his voice low.
 "I want Innsbruck declared an open city," Mayer said. "No artillery defense. No barricades. You issue a standing order to all Wehrmacht, SS, and police units to surrender their weapons and remain in their barracks. You leave every bridge, every railway switch, and every power plant intact."
 "And the political prisoners?" Rahn asked quietly.
 "Every prisoner held in Herrengasse and the Reichenau camp is released by tomorrow morning," Mayer snapped. "Without exception. If one prisoner is executed before the Americans arrive, you hang, Hofer. I personally will see that the rope goes around your neck."
 Hofer's face went white, but he did not shout. He looked toward Rahn.
 The ambassador reached into his breast pocket and produced a gold cigarette case. He did not light one; he held the case flat between his palms.
 "I have channels to Allen Dulles in Bern," Rahn said, looking intently at Mayer. "The OSS mission in Switzerland. We have been conducting preliminary discussions regarding the surrender of German forces in northern Italy. If you can guarantee that the American Seventh Army will accept the peaceful surrender of North Tyrol without conducting immediate summary reprisals against the administrative leadership, I will leave for Switzerland tonight. I will present your terms directly to Dulles."
 Mayer looked from Rahn to Hofer.
 The two Nazi officials were terrified. The curtain was coming down, the Führer was dead or dying in the bunker, and they were looking for an American ticket out of the noose.
 "Tell Dulles that Greenup is holding the valley," Mayer said. "Tell him Innsbruck is surrendering without a shot."
 Rahn nodded, snapped the gold case shut, and stood up from the table. "I depart for the border within the hour."
 Mayer turned back to Franz Hofer. The Gauleiter looked smaller now, his shoulders slumped beneath his gray tunic.
 "Draft the proclamation, Hofer," Mayer said, tapping the mahogany wood. "You sign it tonight, or I walk out that gate and let the artillery level this house."

 Chapter Twenty-Three
 The Open City Decree
 At 06:00 on the morning of May 2, the Telefunken cabinet radio in the study of Gut Lachhof hissed through three bars of funeral music from Wagner's Götterdämmerung.
 Then the voice of the announcer from Hamburg came through the fabric grille, solemn and hollow - It is reported from the Führer's Headquarters that our Führer, Adolf Hitler, fighting to his last breath against Bolshevism, has fallen for Germany this afternoon in his command post at the Reich Chancellery.
 The broadcast ended. The carrier wave droned into silence.
 Franz Hofer sat at his heavy oak writing desk, his hands resting motionless on a leather-bound blotter. Outside in the courtyard of the estate, dark oily smoke billowed from two fifty-five-gallon steel drums. Staff orderlies and SS drivers were dumping armfuls of personnel records, party rosters, and Gestapo index cards into the flames. Several guards had already unbuttoned their tunics and discarded their silver SS collar tabs in the grass.
 Fred Mayer stood against the bookshelf, wearing the tailored charcoal suit. His left eye had swollen into a slit of dark red and purple, but his posture was upright, his thumbs hooked casually in his vest pockets.
 "The radio says he died a hero," Mayer said in dry German. "He shot himself in a cellar under twenty feet of concrete while his capital burned."
 Hofer didn't look up from the blotter. "The Reich is dead."
 "The Reich died a long time ago," Mayer said. "Now sign the papers."
 On the desk lay three typewritten sheets prepared by the provincial chancellery under Mayer's direction:
  1. The city of Innsbruck is declared an Open City (Offene Stadt).
  2. All demolition orders against bridges, rail junctions, power stations, and municipal infrastructure—in accordance with the Nero Decree—are hereby immediately revoked.
  3. All military units, including the Volkssturm, SS rear detachments, and local police, are ordered to cease combat operations, lay down their weapons, and remain in their quarters to await American forces.
 Hofer picked up his gold fountain pen, his fingers trembling slightly. "If I sign this, the SS Division Götz von Berlichingen coming up from Bavaria will execute me for treason."
 "They won't reach Innsbruck," Mayer said. "The American Seventh Army is already past Mittenwald. If you don't sign, American artillery will open up on this valley in six hours, and you will be the first man they put against the wall."
 Hofer stared at the paper. For seven years, he had held absolute authority over Tyrol and Vorarlberg, ruling with the power of life and death over hundreds of thousands of people. Now he was a frightened shopkeeper with an ink pen, bargaining with a Jewish refugee from Brooklyn.
 He pressed the gold nib to the paper. His signature was hasty, the ink slightly uneven - Franz Hofer, Gauleiter und Reichsstatthalter.
 He signed all three copies.
 Mayer pulled the pages toward him, checking each signature. He handed two copies to Werner Hilliges, who stood rigidly near the door, his fedora held in both hands like a petitioner.
 "Hilliges," Mayer said. "Take these to the police presidency and the municipal telegraph office. Broadcast the declaration over the municipal teletype network to every gendarme station in the district. If a single bridge is blown, you answer for it."
 "Immediately, Herr Mayer," Hilliges said. He gave a sharp, nervous bow and hurried out into the hallway.
 Mayer took the third copy and folded it into his inside breast pocket.
 He walked to the window and looked out. Down the mountain, across the silver ribbon of the Inn River, the spires of Innsbruck stood clear against the limestone crags of the Nordkette. The city was intact. No smoke, no artillery flashes.
 A runner from the local O5 network was waiting in the garage below, disguised as a farm laborer on a bicycle.
 Mayer walked down the back stairs, met the man behind the woodpile, and handed him a handwritten note addressed to Dr. Karl Gruber:
  The decree is signed. Hofer has stood down. Take the Landhaus.

 Chapter Twenty-Four
 Seizing the Landhaus
 Rain slicked the stone flags of the Landhausplatz in central Innsbruck.
 Shortly before midnight on May 2, shadows moved along the perimeter of the New Landhaus, the imposing neoclassic seat of regional Nazi power. Men slipped from archways and cellar stairs, their coats buttoned against the drizzle. Each man pinned a fresh red-white-red fabric ribbon to his left sleeve—the colors of an independent Austria.
 Franz Weber stood behind the stone balustrade of the fountain across the square, an MP 40 submachine gun slung muzzle-down beneath his loden coat. Beside him was Dr. Karl Gruber, wearing a dark fedora and a trench coat with his pockets weighed down by heavy Walther pistols. Behind them were thirty armed partisans from the O5 network - off-duty city police officers, deserters, and railway machinists.
 "The sentries at the main doors are conscripts from the 418th Division," Weber whispered. "They know the war is over. Don't shoot unless an officer draws."
 Gruber checked his watch. 23:45. "Move in."
 They crossed the open square in two files, boots scraping against the wet asphalt.
 At the entrance, two teenage Wehrmacht sentries in oversized steel helmets raised their rifles, but when Weber stepped into the doorway light, his officer's cap visible under his collar, he didn't give them time to think.
 "Lower your weapons," Weber snapped, his voice ringing with cold command. "Gauleiter Hofer has declared Innsbruck an open city. General Böhaimb is surrendering the command. Fall in behind us if you don't want to answer to a court-martial."
 The boys looked at the red-white-red armbands, then at the submachine guns emerging from the rain. They dropped their Mausers to the stone floor with a clatter.
 Gruber led the squad through the marble foyer and up the wide central staircase.
 They did not knock on the heavy double doors of the military commandant's office. Gruber threw them open, his boots hitting the parquet floor with loud, deliberate strides.
 General Hans Böhaimb sat behind a massive walnut desk, an operational map of North Tyrol spread out before him. Two staff majors jumped to their feet, their hands darting toward their leather holsters.
 "Hände weg!" Gruber barked, raising his pistol. Behind him, four partisans leveled captured rifles at the majors.
 General Böhaimb did not reach for his holster. He was sixty-one, with close-cropped white hair, bags of dark exhaustion beneath his eyes, and the red collar tabs of a Wehrmacht general officer. He looked at Gruber's red-white-red armband, then at Franz Weber.
 "Who is in charge here?" Böhaimb asked, his voice steady.
 "Dr. Karl Gruber. Commander of the Austrian Resistance," Gruber said, stepping up to the edge of the desk. He pulled Mayer's copy of the Open City decree from his coat and laid it flat over the general's map. "Hitler is dead in Berlin. Gauleiter Hofer has countermanded all demolition orders and stood down the district defenses. If you order your troops to resist, you are not defending Germany; you are murdering your own men. Surrender your sidearm, General."
 Böhaimb looked down at Hofer's signature. He had heard the radio broadcasts from Hamburg. He knew the western front was a graveyard.
 Slowly, without a word, the general unbuckled his leather service belt, complete with holster and bayonet frog, and laid it across the map.
 "The garrison will not fire," Böhaimb said quietly. "My orders to the barracks are already prepared."
 By 02:00, the swastika banner flying above the Landhaus was hauled down, cut into strips, and replaced with the Austrian red-white-red flag.
 Twenty minutes later, Gruber and Weber entered the municipal radio transmitter on the Adolf-Hitler-Platz. The technician on duty, an O5 sympathizer, pushed the master fader to the stop.
 Gruber leaned into the carbon microphone:
 "Citizens of Tyrol. This is the Austrian Resistance Movement. Adolf Hitler is dead. The Nazi administration has collapsed. The city of Innsbruck is liberated, and the Austrian Republic is restored. Remain in your homes, maintain order, and avoid all acts of violence. The war in North Tyrol is finished."
 The announcement went out across the valley on the medium-wave band. In the streets below, church bells began to toll through the rain.
 At 04:30, a runner from the western outskirts burst into the Landhaus chancellery, dripping water onto the floorboards, his face gray with panic.
 "Dr. Gruber!" the runner gasped. "The Americans!"
 Gruber looked up from the dispatch desk. "Have they entered the valley?"
 "They're at Zirl!" the boy stammered. "A full combat command of the 103rd Infantry Division. Tanks, half-tracks, and heavy artillery. They have forward spotters set up on the cliffs above Martinswand. They think Innsbruck is an SS fortress. They're preparing an artillery barrage to flatten the western approaches at sunrise!"

 Chapter Twenty-Five
 The White Bed-Sheet at Zirl
 At 05:30 on May 3, a gray Mercedes staff car tore west down the Inn Valley highway toward Zirl.
 From the front fender antenna fluttered a torn white bedsheet, snapping violently in the slipstream. Behind the wheel sat a nervous German police driver. In the passenger seat sat Fred Mayer, the collar of his charcoal suit turned up against the morning cold, his dislocated right shoulder strapped tight under his vest.
 Ahead, the sheer limestone precipice of the Martinswand loomed over the road, its granite face rising thousands of feet from the riverbank.
 Rounding the curve into Zirl, the road was blocked. Two M4 Sherman tanks of the 103rd Infantry Division sat idling across the asphalt, their 75mm guns traversed straight down the highway. Behind them, infantrymen in olive-drab uniforms and netted steel helmets crouched behind gravel berms with M1 Garands raised, while a forward observation team with binoculars adjusted a radio antenna to coordinate 105mm artillery batteries zeroed on the city.
 The German driver slammed on the brakes. The Mercedes skidded to a halt thirty yards from the lead tank.
 "Raus! Raus!" an American GI screamed from the ditch, waving his rifle barrel.
 Mayer kicked open the passenger door and stepped out onto the gravel. He raised both hands, palm-forward. His face was a map of raw violence - his left eye a swollen slit of black and yellow, his nose crooked and scabbed, dried blood still ringing his nostrils, set above the silk tie and pressed lapels of a German gentleman's suit.
 Two American infantry scouts sprinted forward, safeties off, slamming Mayer against the hood of the Mercedes.
 "Search him!" a sergeant shouted.
 Mayer did not resist. He turned his head sideways against the cold metal, coughing once, and spoke in flat, unaccented Brooklyn English:
 "Take your hands off me, Sergeant. Get your battalion commander on the horn and bring me your G-2."
 The sergeant froze, blinking at the civilian in front of him. "What on earth are you?"
 "First Lieutenant Frederick Mayer, Office of Strategic Services," Mayer said, pushing off the hood with his good arm and straightening his lapels. "Get your forward observers to stand down the artillery. Innsbruck is already liberated. We're holding the city."
 Within five minutes, a command jeep bounced up the shoulder from the rear echelon.
 Major Bland West, assistant G-2 of the 103rd Infantry Division, stepped down into the mud, accompanied by his intelligence lieutenant, Peter Randon. West was armed with a .45 in a shoulder holster, his helmet strap dangling, looking at Mayer with undisguised shock.
 "You're Mayer?" Major West asked. "Caserta lost contact with your call sign three days ago. We had you down as executed by the Gestapo."
 "Not yet, Major," Mayer said, offering a stiff, bruised hand. "The Gestapo broke their teeth on me. Gauleiter Franz Hofer has signed an open-city decree. Dr. Karl Gruber and the Austrian resistance took the Landhaus before dawn, disarmed General Böhaimb, and control the municipal buildings. If you fire one high-explosive round into that valley, you're killing friendly partisans and civilians."
 West turned to Lieutenant Randon. "Call the battery commanders at Seefeld. Cease fire immediately. Tell them to keep the tubes cold."
 Randon sprinted back to the radio jeep, shouting into the handset.
 West turned back to Mayer, his eyes running over the split lip, the tailored suit, and the German staff car with its white bedsheet. "Where is Hofer?"
 "Waiting for you at his estate in Kleinvolderberg," Mayer said. "He wants to surrender to an American officer before the resistance hangs him from a balcony. Follow my car."
 The American column started its engines. With Mayer's white-flagged staff car leading the way, followed by Major West's command jeep and a column of armored scout cars, the 103rd Infantry Division rolled down the highway into the Inn Valley without firing a single shot.
 They crossed the bridge into Innsbruck. The streets were lined with thousands of civilians waving Austrian red-white-red flags and Allied banners, cheering through tears as the American vehicles clattered past intact bridges, undamaged power stations, and untouched historic avenues.
 The convoy bypassed the celebrations, racing east past Hall in Tirol to the iron gates of Gut Lachhof.
 American infantrymen deployed quickly across the gravel driveway, rifles leveled at the timber portico. Mayer walked up the stone steps beside Major Bland West, pushing open the heavy front doors without knocking.
 Inside the study, Gauleiter Franz Hofer stood by his oak desk, dressed in a plain suit, his hands empty at his sides.
 Mayer gestured with an open hand toward the American major.
 "Major Bland West, United States Army," Mayer said in German. "Gauleiter Hofer. As promised."
 Major West stepped forward, unbuttoning his holster flap.
 "Franz Hofer," West announced firmly, "by order of the Allied Command, you are under arrest."
 Hofer offered no resistance. He held out his wrists, his eyes lingering on Mayer—the Brooklyn mechanic who had walked down from a glacier and delivered his Gau into American hands.

 Chapter Twenty-Six
 On the Porch at Oberperfuss
 The mountain thaw came fast in the second week of May, turning the slopes above Oberperfuss into carpets of green rye and dandelions.
 An olive-drab Willys jeep climbed the switchbacks from the Inn Valley, its engine whining in low gear. Dyno Löwenstein sat in the passenger seat, his uniform dusty from the Brenner road, carrying a leather map case and a 35mm camera.
 He found them gathered on the wooden porch outside the Gasthof Zur Krone and the neighboring farmhouse.
 Franz Weber sat on the bench beside his fiancée, Anni Niederkircher, his arm resting quietly behind her shoulders. Standing beside them was Maria Hörtnagl, the village seamstress who had tailored Mayer's Wehrmacht uniform in secret, along with Hans Wijnberg, who wore a clean woolen sweater and held a tin cup of real coffee traded from an American supply sergeant. Fred Mayer leaned against the timber rail, wearing an olive-drab American field jacket. His left eye was still ringed with a fading greenish shadow and the bridge of his nose remained crooked from Güttner's truncheon, but his stance was easy.
 Löwenstein stepped down from the jeep and walked into the yard. He took Mayer's hand in a hard grip, then Hans's, then Franz's.
 "Caserta thought you were dead, Fred," Löwenstein said quietly. "Hans's dispatch on the twenty-fifth was the last clean transmission we logged before the division hit the valley."
 "Hans kept the station open," Mayer said. "He stayed on the air until the 103rd rolled past the switches."
 Löwenstein pulled a wooden stool from the porch and sat down opposite them. He opened his leather case, pulled out the high-altitude aerial mosaic of the Inn Valley they had studied in Bari four months earlier, and laid it across the table.
 "The freight yards at the Hauptbahnhof are gone," Löwenstein said. "The Fifteenth Air Force dropped six hundred tons directly on the switches you marked. The Germans never moved another supply train south to Kesselring. The Messerschmitt tooling at Kematen is being packed into crates for Wright Field. And General Patch's staff has torn up the redoubt maps. There was nothing in these hills to fight."
 Franz Weber looked toward the road down to Innsbruck, his expression subdued.
 "Not everyone came back," Franz said.
 The porch went quiet.
 Robert Moser's body had been recovered from the cellar floor at Herrengasse 1 on the afternoon of May 3; they had buried him in the municipal cemetery near the river.
 Down in Innsbruck, Eva Weber lay in a sterile ward at the Provincial Hospital (Krankenhaus Innsbruck). Though liberated from the brutal conditions of the Reichenau transit camp when the Americans arrived, the severe physical trauma, pneumonia, and internal illness contracted during her imprisonment had shattered her health. She would succumb to complications following emergency surgery before the end of the month, on May 24.
 Löwenstein looked down at the timber planks, silent for a moment, before unbuttoning his camera case and pulling out his Leica.
 "Gather together," Löwenstein said gently. "The people who ran Greenup."
 They assembled on the porch under the warm spring sun - Franz Weber and Anni Niederkircher seated in front, with Maria Hörtnagl, Hans Wijnberg, and Fred Mayer standing behind them against the sun-warmed timber. Mayer looked directly into the lens, unblinking, the hard angles of his battered face cast in sharp relief against the alpine wood.
 The shutter clicked. A clean, mechanical snap.
 Within two weeks, the official after-action summary from the Office of Strategic Services would land on the desk of General William J. Donovan in Washington - Operation Greenup achieved complete tactical and strategic success. It remains one of the most successful operational penetrations of the Third Reich by Allied intelligence.
 On the porch at Oberperfuss, the men and women did not speak of Washington.
 Hans finished his coffee and set the empty mug on the wooden rail. Mayer leaned back against the logs, closed his eyes, and listened to the bells of the parish church ringing the noon hour down in the valley.
