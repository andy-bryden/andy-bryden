Chapter One

↑ Back to top

The villa on the outskirts of Bari smelled of damp limestone, stale Adriatic brine, and cigarette smoke.

On the second floor, Dyno Löwenstein sat behind a folding trestle table littered with aerial reconnaissance prints, carbon paper, and mimeographed personnel rosters. Rain clicked against the glass of the tall terrace doors. Across the bay, gray convoy ships sat low in the water under a flat winter sky.

Löwenstein rubbed his eyes. He had Kurt Löwenstein’s posture—the stoop of a Berlin intellectual who had spent years leaning over draft resolutions and anti-fascist pamphlets before the Gestapo burned the family flat. Now, in the uniform of an American officer, he was reading casualty summaries from the Secret Intelligence branch of the 2677th Regiment.

A knock rattled the wood.

"Come," Löwenstein said.

Fred Mayer came in first, walking with the quick, slightly forward-leaning gait of an auto mechanic clearing a crowded floor. He was twenty-three, short, broad across the shoulders, with thick dark hair combed straight back. Behind him came Hans Wijnberg, taller, thinner, pale from weeks indoors at the radio school, holding a woolen overseas cap in both hands.

"You wanted us, Lieutenant?" Mayer said. His voice was Brooklyn dockside over an unyielding southwest-German foundation—Freiburg under Flatbush.

"Sit," Löwenstein said. He pointed to two wicker chairs with frayed seats. "Shut the door behind you, Wijnberg."

Wijnberg closed it, clicked the latch, and sat. He kept his cap in his lap, thumbs smoothing the piping.

Löwenstein pushed two manila files toward them. "Personnel down in Caserta wants both of you reassigned. Signal corps depot for you, Hans. Translation pool in Naples for Fred. They think twenty-three-year-old refugees from Baden and Amsterdam are better used sorting mail and interrogating captured supply clerks."

Mayer didn't look at the files. "We didn't train six months in Maryland and Algeria to file requisition slips in Naples."

"They know German," Löwenstein said flatly. "They don't know you."

"You know us," Mayer said. "Tell Caserta no."

"I did." Löwenstein leaned forward, his elbows on the glass-topped table. "Which means you're my problem now."

He turned one of the aerial prints around so they could see it. It was a mosaic of high-altitude photographs taken by an RAF Mosquito. A narrow valley, almost black against white peaks, with a faint gray spine running down the center.

"The Brenner Pass," Löwenstein said. "Every train feeding Kesselring’s army in northern Italy comes through this slit. Ammunition, coal, panzer engines, fresh divisions. The Fifteenth Air Force drops five hundred tons of high explosive on the tracks; the Germans bring in three thousand Italian forced laborers and have the rails relaid thirty-six hours later. We don't know what gets through. We don't know the repair schedules. We don't know when the trains are lined up waiting for green signals."

"Then send a team to sit on the tracks," Mayer said.

"We did," Löwenstein said. He tapped a pencil against a red grease-pencil circle on the map. "Twice. Company E dropped three men near Kufstein in November. Dead in four days. Another pair jumped into the Zillertal three weeks ago. Picked up before they unbuckled their harnesses. The locals hear an aircraft engine, look at the snow, see tracks, and call the Gendarmerie. If you're not from the valley, you don't last long enough to turn a radio on."

Wijnberg looked at the map, then at Löwenstein. "What’s the drop zone?"

"There isn't one. The valley floor is walled in by flak batteries and radar pickets. If you drop low, you hit rock or you get shot out of the harness. If you drop high, you land on ice at nine thousand feet in the middle of winter."

"We can jump into the high ground," Mayer said.

"Can you ski?" Löwenstein asked.

Mayer shrugged. "I've seen it done."

"You've seen it done," Löwenstein repeated, his voice dry. "Hans, can you ski?"

"No," Wijnberg said.

Löwenstein dropped his pencil. "Then you'd freeze to death five miles from the nearest road. You're city boys. Fred, you know Freiburg and the Ford plant on Bedford Avenue. Hans knows the canals in Amsterdam. You drop into the North Tyrol, you won't even know what color the bus tickets are. You won't know which cop in the village has a son on the Eastern Front and which one is an SS fanatic who will shoot you in a tavern latrine."

The room went quiet except for the rain. On the harbor, a destroyer sounded a single, muffled horn through the mist.

"There's nobody else," Mayer said. He leaned his forearms against his knees, eyes fixed on Löwenstein. "You're sitting here with two men who speak the language, who hate them as much as you do, and who are ready to get on an airplane tonight. You want to wait around until the Fifteenth Air Force runs out of bombers?"

"Determination doesn't keep you alive when you walk into an inn with the wrong accent, Fred."

"Then find us a guide," Mayer said.

Löwenstein looked at him.

"You’ve got thousands of them in the cages," Mayer continued, his tone matter-of-fact. "Every week the British drag another five hundred Austrians out of the mountains in the north. Half of them were drafted. A quarter of them were socialists before thirty-eight. Find one who knows the Brenner, who knows the farms, who wants his town back. You put him in front, I handle the operational legwork, Hans runs the set."

Löwenstein didn't answer immediately. He stood up, walked to the tall windows, and looked out at the harbor.

"If he turns on you," Löwenstein said to the glass, "he hands you to the Gestapo before you reach the first rail junction. You know what happens to German Jews carrying American papers and codebooks."

"We know," Wijnberg said quietly.

Löwenstein turned back. He reached into an iron lockbox sitting on the credenza behind his desk, pulled out a thick buff folder tied with faded red string, and dropped it onto the table between them.

"Cinecittà," Löwenstein said. "The prisoner camp outside Rome. The British Eighth Army took a batch of deserters off the Gothic Line two weeks ago. Field security sorted out twenty-odd Austrians with clean records before the Anschluss. One of them walked away from an infantry regiment near Bologna in December."

Mayer pulled the folder across the blotter. He untied the knot and opened the first page.

Pasted to the top right corner was a small, grainy black-and-white photograph: a lean man in a collarless Wehrmacht tunic, high cheekbones, dark hair cropped close to the scalp, staring straight at the camera with an expression of stubborn exhaustion. Underneath, typed in purple carbon:

WEBER, Franz. Born: Oberperfuss, Tirol, 1920. Rank: Oberleutnant, Gebirgsjäger. Catholic. Deserted unit 18 December 1944.

"Oberperfuss," Löwenstein said, tapping the map. "It's a farming village on a plateau twelve miles west of Innsbruck. Look at the elevation. It sits right above the Inn Valley. On a clear morning, you can look down and see the switches at the main freight depot."

Mayer stared at the photograph. He ran a thumb over the paper.

"Get the jeep," Mayer told Wijnberg. "We're going to Rome."

Chapter Two

↑ Back to top

The sound stage smelled of unwashed wool, damp straw, and carbolic soap.

Before the war, Mussolini’s film crews had built Roman palaces inside the Cinecittà complex. Now, concertina wire divided the hangars into holding pens, and hundreds of men in gray coats sat on wooden benches under the dead carbon-arc lights, waiting for their names to be called by Allied field police.

An armed British sergeant opened the side door to an empty sound-editing booth. "Number forty-one," the sergeant said, jerking his thumb back toward the wire. "That's your man. Twenty minutes."

Dyno Löwenstein pulled out two wooden chairs and set them on the bare floor. Fred Mayer stayed on his feet, leaning his hip against a heavy soundproof partition, his hands jammed into the pockets of his field jacket.

The door clicked open. A British corporal pushed Franz Weber into the booth, then shut the door from the outside.

Weber stood straight. His Wehrmacht field-gray tunic was missing its collar tabs, national eagle, and shoulder boards, leaving dark rectangular scars where the stitches had been cut. His trousers were stiff with dried Bolognese mud. He had hollowed cheeks, dark stubble, and eyes that moved quickly between the two men, assessing their insignia. He settled on Mayer.

"Sit down, Herr Oberleutnant," Löwenstein said in standard High German, pointing to the empty chair.

Weber did not move toward the chair. "I gave up the rank when I crossed the lines, Captain." His voice had the flat, clipped vowels of the North Tyrol.

"Sit anyway," Löwenstein said.

Weber sat. He rested his chapped hands on his knees and kept his back off the wood.

Mayer stepped forward, speaking in the broad, hard-edged German of the southwest. "Why did you leave your battalion?"

Weber looked up, his gaze steady. "Because we were losing."

"A lot of men are losing," Mayer said. "Most of them keep shooting until an artillery shell takes their legs off. You took off your boots, crawled through three hundred yards of canal mud, and waved a white undershirt at the British Eighth Army."

"The war was lost two years ago," Weber said quietly. "At Stalingrad. Everything after that has been murder."

"That didn't bother you in Poland?" Mayer stepped closer, leaning down slightly so Weber had to tilt his head. "Didn't bother you in Russia? You wore the tunic four years. You collected your pay."

Weber’s jaw tightened. "I was drafted in thirty-nine. An ordinary infantry regiment. In the Ukraine, my company was ordered to guard a railhead while the Sicherheitsdienst cleared the ghetto. They loaded women and children into lime-washed cattle cars. In Yugoslavia, we burned three villages because a partisan cut a telephone line. If you think I took pride in that uniform, you don't know who you're talking to."

"I know what the dossier says," Mayer snapped. "I also know the Abwehr puts smart men in deserter cages all the time. Good story, clean papers, anti-Nazi Catholic background. You get repatriated, you find out who's working for the Allies, and by Tuesday morning everyone is hanging from meat hooks in Munich."

Weber didn't blink. "If I were working for the Abwehr, I would have told your British interrogator that I loved Churchill. I told him the truth: I am an Austrian, I am a Catholic, and Adolf Hitler is a criminal who is dragging my country down into an open grave. If that makes me a spy in your eyes, send me back to the pen."

Löwenstein cleared his throat, breaking the tension. "Your home is Oberperfuss."

"Yes."

"Your family?"

"My mother, Aloisia. Three sisters: Luise, Eva, Margarethe. My father died when I was young." Weber turned his eyes back to Löwenstein. "My family is known in the district. We are not Party members. None of us."

"And the village?" Mayer asked, his tone still sharp. "How many Nazis?"

"A few," Weber said. "The innkeeper. A couple of postmen. The new mayor the Party put in after thirty-eight. The rest are farmers. They go to Mass, they curse the food requisitions, and they hide their grain under the floorboards."

Löwenstein unfolded a pocket map and spread it across the desk between them. It showed the valley of the Inn River, with Innsbruck in the center and the mountain roads curling south toward Italy.

"What is the view from the ridge above your mother's house?" Löwenstein asked.

Weber glanced at the contour lines without hesitation. "From the high meadows above Oberperfuss, you look down directly onto the valley floor. To the east, the main rail line from Germany enters Innsbruck. To the south, you see the marshaling yards and the mouth of the Sill Gorge—the entrance to the Brenner."

Mayer watched the Austrian’s eyes as he spoke. There was no hesitation, no calculation. The man was visualizing his home ground.

"If someone had a transmitter on that ridge," Mayer said, "could they watch the rail traffic?"

Weber looked from the map back to Mayer. His expression changed, the weariness giving way to sudden caution. "A transmitter in Oberperfuss wouldn't last three days if the Germans brought a detector van up the mountain road. The valley echoes. You'd need to hide it in the rafters. You'd need people to carry the messages. People who could walk through the checkpoints without their hands shaking."

"Could your sisters do that?" Mayer asked.

Weber’s face went cold. "I won't bring the Gestapo down on my mother's house."

"The Gestapo is already there," Mayer said. He pulled up the other chair and sat down directly across from Weber, so close their knees nearly brushed. "You think this ends quietly? In six months, the Russians are going to cross the Danube from the east. The Americans are coming through the Bavarian passes. The SS will turn Tyrol into a bunker. They'll blow every bridge, draft every fifteen-year-old boy in Oberperfuss, and fight street by street until the valley is rubble."

Weber was silent. His hands gripped his knees until the knuckles turned white.

"You want to save your family?" Mayer said, his voice dropping low, hard, and urgent. "You take us in. We set up an intelligence post above Innsbruck. We tell the bombers exactly what to hit—the rail yards, the repair shops, the switch engines—and what to leave standing. When the ground forces get to the pass, you help us bring the district down from the inside before the SS can burn it."

Weber looked at Mayer's face, studying the sharp, dark features. "You're not American-born."

"No," Mayer said. "Freiburg. Jewish. My father won the Iron Cross in the last war. In thirty-eight, they made him scrub the street with a toothbrush. We got out. Now I'm coming back."

The sound of boots and distant shouts drifted through the high rafters of the Cinecittà studio.

Weber stared down at the map, his thumb tracing the blue line of the Inn River where it curved past his home.

"You can't jump into the valley," Weber said, his voice barely above a whisper. "There are searchlights at Zirl, radar at Kematen, flak batteries ringing the airfield. They would shoot you to pieces before you cleared five hundred meters."

"We know," Mayer said.

"And you can't walk across the Swiss border. The alpine patrol has ski posts every kilometer."

"Then tell us how we get in."

Weber looked up, meeting Mayer's eyes.

"The Sulztalferner," Weber said. "It's a glacier. South of the Stubai Alps, nearly three thousand meters high. It's completely barren. In the winter, there are no patrols because nobody believes a human being can survive the descent."

Mayer looked at Löwenstein, then back at the Austrian.

"Can you get us down it?" Mayer asked.

Weber stood up, his shoulders straight, no longer looking like a prisoner. "If you can jump into nine feet of powder without breaking your necks, I can get you to Oberperfuss."

Chapter Three

The staging house in Brindisi was a requisitioned stone villa surrounded by olive groves that rattled in the damp sea wind.

In the ground-floor salon, Hans Wijnberg had stripped an OSS SSTR-1 transceiver down to its chassis on a mahogany dining table. The room smelled of solder resin, lead batteries, and the sour red wine Löwenstein bought by the demijohn from an orchardist down the lane.

Mayer stood over a six-foot contour map of the Tyrol weighted down at the corners with brass shell casings. Franz Weber, now wearing a sanitized American olive-drab field uniform without insignia, stood on the opposite side of the table, his finger resting on an expanse of white contour lines bunched tightly between sharp elevation markers.

"Here," Weber said. "Sulztalferner. Southwest of the Schrankogel. Elevation roughly three thousand meters."

Löwenstein walked in, carrying a fresh carbon-copied weather intercept from Caserta. "The USAAF navigators at Manduria say jumping blind onto a high-altitude glacier at night is suicide. If the wind shears off the ridges, you'll slam into granite faces before your canopy even fully deploys."

"The navigators aren't jumping," Mayer said without looking up. "The valley floors are out. What else is there, Franz?"

"Nothing," Weber said. "Every pass between Switzerland and Italy is monitored by mountain border guards on skis. The Inn Valley itself is a trap. If we hit the glacier, we drop into deep snow, but there are no Germans for twenty kilometers. It is the only door that isn't locked."

"Assuming you survive the drop," Löwenstein said. "What happens to the radio?"

Hans looked up from his balance meter, holding an alligator clip between his fingers. "The SSTR-1 is packed in rubber shock-mounts inside water-sealed canisters. Transmitter, receiver, hand-generator, spare tubes, batteries. If the canister hits soft snow without cracking a boulder, it works."

"And if the canister lands in a crevasse?"

"Then we have a very long walk back for nothing," Hans said evenly.

Mayer tapped the map at Innsbruck. "We need to secure our communications before we get on that plane. If the Funkabwehr picks up our traffic, standard OSS enciphering will keep them busy for a few hours, maybe a day. But if they crack the grid coordinates, they'll know which train yards we're reporting before the bombs drop."

Hans reached into his canvas map case and pulled out a pocket street guide of New York City, dog-eared and creased from his months living with his brother in Brooklyn. He opened it flat on top of the Austrian Alps.

"We transpose," Hans said.

Löwenstein arched an eyebrow. "Transpose what?"

"Brooklyn," Mayer said, a faint grin breaking through his face. "Hans and I know the borough street by street. The Germans don't."

Hans took a black grease pencil. "Innsbruck is Flatbush. The main passenger station at the center is Grand Army Plaza."

"The freight marshaling yards," Mayer pointed with the tip of his thumb, "where the rail lines split toward Italy—that’s Ebbets Field."

"The air base at Hotting?" Hans asked.

"Floyd Bennett Field," Mayer answered instantly.

Weber looked at the two young men, his brow furrowed as he watched them turn his homeland into an American street grid. "And the Sill Gorge? The entrance to the Brenner pass?"

"Gowanus Canal," Mayer said. "If Hans transmits that two heavy coal trains are delayed at Ebbets Field heading toward the Gowanus, the German radio interceptors in Munich will waste three days trying to find an industrial sector matching the phonetics. By the time they figure it out, the Fifteenth Air Force has already pulverized the switches."

Hans pulled a small notebook from his shirt pocket, inscribed the cipher substitutions in a tight, microscopic hand, tore the page out, and handed it to Löwenstein. "Bari Station Victor gets that copy. I keep the other in my heel."

Löwenstein looked at the slip of paper, then folded it neatly into his tunic pocket. He pulled another document from his clipboard—an operational dispatch stamped with red ink: TOP SECRET – EYES ONLY – OPERATION GREENUP.

"Caserta approved the flight window," Löwenstein said quietly.

The room went still. Outside, the Mediterranean rain pattered against the shuttered terrace doors.

"When?" Mayer asked.

"The 885th Bomb Squadron at Brindisi has an aircraft prepped. A B-24, black finish, equipped for low-level night insertion. Captain Billings is the pilot. He says there's a break in the weather over the Alps starting tomorrow night, February twenty-fifth. It gives you an eight-hour window before the next front moves in from the Atlantic."

Löwenstein laid the manifest flat on the table, unscrewed his fountain pen, and signed his name across the authorization block.

"Three jump manifests," Löwenstein said. "Sergeant Frederick Mayer. Sergeant Hans Wijnberg. Franz Weber, civilian guide. You take three days' rations, cold-weather jump smocks, your personal sidearms, and three pairs of alpine touring skis packed into the primary equipment container."

Weber nodded, his hand resting on the elevation markers. "The skis are not optional. If that container doesn't drop cleanly on our heading, we do not come off that ice field. In February, unbroken alpine powder is bottomless."

"The drop container is rigged to an independent static line," Löwenstein said. "Billings will drop it right down your chute path."

He capped his pen.

Mayer looked at Hans, who was already seating the quartz crystals back into the olive-drab steel casing of the SSTR-1.

"Get your kit stowed, Hans," Mayer said. "We're going to Flatbush."

Chapter Four

↑ Back to top

The stripped-down B-24 Liberator had no gun turrets, no armor plating, and no interior heating. Its outer skin was painted dead matte black.

In the aft fuselage, the smell of 100-octane aviation fuel and hydraulic fluid hung thick in the uninsulated cold. Mayer, Wijnberg, and Weber sat on the metal ribbed floor, their backs wedged against the bulkhead. Each man was bundled into heavy canvas winter jump smocks worn over layers of wool, with thick gloves, jump boots, and chest-mounted reserve chutes strapped tight over their main packs.

At eighteen thousand feet, the air was thin enough to make their lungs ache around the rubber rims of their oxygen masks.

Up in the cockpit, Captain John Billings flew with the cockpit lights blacked out, watching the pale luminescence of the instrument needles. He had flown supply drops into Yugoslavia and northern Italy, but threading a four-engine bomber through the high granite corridors of the Tyrol in dead winter was a different matter. To stay below German radar stations at Verona and Innsbruck, he had crossed the Adriatic at low altitude, then climbed steeply over the Venetian plain into the teeth of an alpine crosswind.

The intercom crackled in the crew chief’s headset. The sergeant leaned down to Mayer, pulling his mask back for a second to shout over the deafening roar of the four Pratt & Whitney radial engines.

"Ten minutes to initial point! Get hooked up!"

Mayer tapped Weber and Hans on their shoulders. They dragged themselves to their feet in the cramped, pitching fuselage. The vibration came up through the soles of their boots, rattling their teeth.

Hans checked his quick-release box with gloved fingers. His face was chalky under the flight goggles, but his eyes were steady. Weber moved with practiced alpine economy, checking the straps on the supply canisters lashed near the drop hatch—the "Joe hole," a round exit cut into the floor where the belly turret had once been.

Down through the dark opening, the world was a jagged monochrome nightmare.

Moonlight caught the ridgelines—immense razorbacks of black rock slicing through fields of brilliant, blinding snow. Billings was flying below the peaks now, weaving through the valleys, banking the heavy bomber so close to the cliff faces that the shadow of the wings seemed to scrape the crags. Downdrafts slammed the aircraft downward by fifty feet in a stomach-dropping lurch, then kicked it back up into the slipstream.

A bell rang once. A sharp, mechanical hammer against brass.

Above the hole, the red indicator lamp flicked on.

The crew chief dropped to one knee by the rim, sliding back the protective canvas cover. A gale of minus-twenty-degree air tore into the fuselage, instantly freezing the moisture on the men's goggles and cutting through three layers of canvas.

"Static lines!" the sergeant roared.

Mayer snapped his bronze carabiner onto the overhead cable running the length of the ceiling, yanking it back twice to test the lock. Weber hooked in second. Hans hooked in third.

The sergeant gripped the release lanyard on the first supply canister—the one holding the food, the dry clothes, and the two pairs of cross-country skis.

Billings leveled the wings. Down below, the dark serrations of rock opened up into an immense, unbroken white sheet tilted at a terrifying angle: the frozen expanse of the Sulztalferner glacier. There were no lights, no roads, no trees. Only the dead pale glow of ice under the stars.

The clock on the bulkhead read 01:05.

The red lamp cut out.

The green lamp hit them like a flashbulb.

"Go! Go! Go!" the crew chief screamed, kicking the equipment container down into the dark.

Weber lunged first. He dropped through the hole feet-first, his arms clamped across his reserve pack, sucked instantly into the black wash of the slipstream.

Mayer didn't pause. He grabbed both sides of the circular rim, threw his legs down into the open sky, and let the wind tear him from the plane.

The blast of four propellers hammered his ribs, spinning him blind into the freezing air before the static line snatched the pack off his back. There was a violent, upward crack that rattled his spine, and the white sheet of the glacier rushed up to meet him.

Chapter Five

The canopy cracked open, snapping Mayer’s head back against the nylon risers with the force of a hammer blow.

The deafening howl of the B-24’s four radial engines shrank into a faint, dying whine, swallowed by the immense granite basin. For several seconds, there was absolute, breathless stillness. The air was thin, pure, and so bitterly cold it burned his throat. Beneath his dangling boots, the vast white slope of the glacier tilted sideways in the moonlight, expanding until the texture of the wind-scoured crust resolved into jagged drifts and shadowy terraces of blue ice.

Mayer hit feet-first, drove straight through the crust, sank to his chest in powdery snow, and pitched forward. The silk canopy drifted down on top of him, smothering him in yards of suffocating white fabric.

He fought his way out, spitting ice crystals, fumbled with numb, stiffening fingers at his chest harness, and punched the center release disc. The straps whipped free. He struggled to heave himself up, but the powder gave way under his elbows; he sank to his waist, his jump boots finding no bottom in the flour-like drifts. The temperature hung near ten below zero, with a cutting thermal downdraft shrieking off the knife-edge crest of the Schrankogel.

"Hans!" Mayer yelled, his voice flat, deadened by the immense expanse of snow. "Franz!"

Fifty yards uphill, a dark shape dragged itself violently out of a snowbank. It was Hans Wijnberg. His canopy had dragged him thirty yards across a wind-swept patch of blue ice before snagging on a jagged granite horn.

"I'm here!" Hans called out, his breathing ragged and shallow. He was clutching the steel box of the SSTR-1 transceiver against his ribs; its padded container had landed four yards away, anchored by its small auxiliary chute. "The set is intact! I have the crystals!"

Through the darkness below, Franz Weber clawed his way toward them on his hands and knees. He had no skis under his boots. He was floundering through the drifts like a drowning swimmer, his face white, his jump smock torn at the shoulder where he had scraped across a boulder.

"The equipment crate!" Weber shouted, his teeth chattering so hard the words slurred. "Where is the cargo chute?"

They scanned the pale blue expanse of the Sulztalferner. Sixty yards downhill, a deep, furrowed drag mark cut through the untouched powder, curving around a wind-sculpted dome of ice before vanishing over a sheer break in the topography.

Mayer and Weber crawled to the edge on their bellies, dragging themselves through the drifts.

The snow shelf ended in an ink-black slit four feet wide. Below lay a vertical crevasse, its icy blue walls plunging hundreds of feet down into the bowels of the glacier. Far down in the blue shadow, wedged irrevocably into a narrow bottleneck of sheer ice, lay the crumpled, mangled wreck of the primary supply container.

"All three pairs of skis," Weber breathed, staring down into the abyss. His voice was drained of blood. "The heavy mountain boots. The dry woolens. The spare rations."

None of them had skis.

Mayer stared down into the crack, the bitter wind whipping his hair across his forehead. He spat onto the ice. "We aren't climbing down three hundred feet of dead ice to get them. We leave it."

He pushed himself up and attempted to take a stride toward the lower slope. The crust gave way instantly with a dull, hollow whoosh. Mayer dropped to his ribs, his legs pinned by the suffocating weight of five feet of powder. When he tried to lever his weight on his forearms, the snow acted like dry sand, sliding and caving in around his chest.

Hans tried to push forward to join them and sank to his hips, gasping as freezing powder forced its way down into the tops of his jump boots.

"We can't walk through this," Hans whispered, clutching the seventy-pound radio unit against his stomach with trembling hands. "We'll burn every calorie we have before we cover half a mile."

Weber looked down the vast, rolling expanse of the Sulztalferner. Below them lay three miles of treacherous, crevassed glacier dipping toward the treeline. Beyond that lay the locked mountain huts of the Gries valley.

"If we stay in this drift until sunrise," Weber said, "our feet will freeze. By morning, gangrene sets in and you cannot stand."

"Then don't let us stay in it," Mayer said, his jaw locked, dragging his torso forward out of the hole with his elbows. "You're the mountain trooper, Franz. Pick the ridge lines. We break the snow together."

Weber did not argue. He moved to the front. Without skis, he had to throw his entire body into the drifts, using his thighs and hips to batter down a crude trench in the snow.

Mayer fell into the furrow behind him, leaning his broad shoulders into the drifts to widen the channel, while Hans trudged in their wake, cradling the heavy steel transceiver against his chest.

One agonizing, lung-searing stride at a time, their boots soaked and numb, their breath freezing into icicles on their collars, the three men began the descent on foot through the arctic dark.

Chapter Six

↑ Back to top

The timber walls of the Amberger Hütte emerged from the gray predawn gloom like a stranded barge in an ocean of snow.

Drifts reached halfway up the ground-floor shutters. The mountain refuge had been locked since October, its chimney capped against winter storms.

Mayer stumbled against the heavy pine door, his legs shaking with exhaustion. His jump trousers were frozen solid from the knees down, stiff as sheet metal, scraping against his raw, bleeding shins with every step. Hans collapsed onto the snow-covered porch, his head slumped against the radio container, his breath rattling in his throat like broken glass. Weber’s face was bloodless, his lips purple.

"Is the door barred?" Mayer asked, his voice a hoarse rasp.

Weber didn't answer. He drew his heavy trench knife, rammed the steel blade into the gap between the heavy oak jamb and the iron lock-hasp, and threw his entire body weight against the hilt. The frozen wood groaned, splintered, and gave with a sharp, gunshot crack.

Weber kicked the door inward.

Inside, the hut smelled of dry pine, cold soot, and wool mothballs. The air was freezing, but it was dead out of the wind.

Weber went straight to the cast-iron stove, stripping dry shavings from an iron basket with trembling hands. He struck a match; the sulfur flared, catching shavings of dry larch. Within minutes, a dull orange glow flickered through the stove's draft door, casting long shadows across the empty tables.

Hans pulled his gloves off with his teeth. His fingertips were waxy white and hard as wood. Mayer grabbed Hans’s hands and shoved them under his own wool sweater, pressing them against the warmth of his bare armpits. Hans let out a ragged hiss as the blood began to needle into the deadened tissue.

Weber set a dented copper pot full of clean snow on the stove lid. He looked at Mayer.

Here, safe from Allied carpet bombing, the Reich had dispersed the manufacturing lines for the Messerschmitt Me 262—the world’s first operational jet fighter.

Allied command in Caserta was convinced that hundreds of these twin-engine jets were being secretly assembled in alpine caverns, ready to surge into the sky and wipe the Fifteenth Air Force from the air.

Mayer pulled a test-meter from his box, climbed an aluminum ladder near a central power distribution panel, and began unscrewing a junction plate. From eight feet off the floor, he had an unobstructed view down the entire length of Gallery 4.

The shark-nosed fuselages sat in rows on steel assembly jigs. He began counting.

One. Two. Three. Twenty-four airframes in various stages of completion.

He climbed down, moved to an overhead lighting run twenty yards further down the track, and reset his ladder near the wing-fitting station. He looked inside the engine nacelles slung beneath the swept wings.

"We have nineteen airframes ready for pre-flight installation," the sergeant said, pointing his pencil at a row of unpainted wings. "Where are the turbines from Regensburg?"

"Blown to scrap at the Salzburg junction," the foreman spat, not slowing down. "The train never crossed the river. We have no turbine rotors, no hydraulic fluid, and the night shift from the labor barracks stole forty meters of rubber fuel line to make shoe soles. If Berlin wants twenty jets this week, they'll have to push them out the tunnel by hand."

Mayer adjusted his screwdriver against a dead bolt, committing the words to memory.

KEMATEN PRODUCTION NEGLIGIBLE. TWENTY-FOUR AIRFRAMES INCOMPLETE. ZERO JET ENGINES AVAILABLE. FACTORY GROUNDED BY LACK OF RAIL LOGISTICS. DISMISS THREAT.

Bari received it without a repeat request. Across Italy, the strategic calculators at Allied Air Command crossed the Kematen jet threat off their target ledgers.
