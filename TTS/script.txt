The Fall of Lorkh

When Rome was hamstrung in 233 AD


 Chapter 1. — The Lion.

 Sand kicked up under bronze-shod feet as Tahna and To'ria closed the distance at a dead sprint, blades already rising, the crowd's roar loud enough to drown whichever of them screamed first.
 Then a second roar cut under it — deeper, guttural, rolling in off the far side of the arena — and both women broke stride at once, blades still up, eyes cutting toward the sound instead of each other.
 A lion came through the gate, mane low, claws tearing sand, and every eye in the stands went with it.
 Servius had owned and trained more than one gladiatrix in his years running the Koo'neggy'on, but the Greek woman, Tahna, was the only one who had ever really riled him. He had grown tired of her arrogance. Tired of the smirk she wore into every bout, the one that said she already knew how the other woman would die before the first blow landed. Eleven fights, eleven corpses that weren't hers, and still that same insufferable calm.
 This day, Servius had decided to pair her against the Hispanic firebrand To'ria.
 He had never matched two of his own gladiatrices against each other before. It wasn't done, not when both women drew a crowd and both crowds paid. But the sun-bronzed Iberian was fast in a way Tahna wasn't, vicious in a way the Greek's cold precision had never had to answer for, and he told himself the novelty alone would cover the loss. Two gladiatrices, one blade each, armour same as any other bout. Let the smirk answer that.
 What he hadn't accounted for was the third woman.
 Tasset'uh was difficult to manage in ways that had nothing to do with defiance. She never argued, never resisted, never gave the guards a reason to raise a hand. She moved through the compound quietly, often somewhere she wasn't supposed to be. She had been there three years, and not once had Servius heard her utter a word, or even make a sound. In that time she had also learned to move silently, which shouldn't have been possible in bronze armour. There was something uncanny about her he could never put a finger on.
 While Tahna and To'ria were led into the tunnel that opened onto the sand, Tasset'uh was already gone, slipping into the low stone corridor where the compound kept its beasts.
 She had a plan, and it was not a subtle one.
 Back in the arena, the lion paused momentarily, sizing up the two women, neither of whom had been primed for fighting a beast this round. But they had both participated in paired animal kills before, and their tactics switched immediately. They needed to divide the lion's attention to gain the advantage. Without a word, and not even so much as a second glance towards each other, they peeled apart and approached from two different angles. The lion turned its focus on Tahna and in a moment, To'ria's falcata tore into the beast's emaciated frame. It was over in under a minute — messy, efficient. The crowd didn't see a jailbreak. They saw a miracle. They saw two gladiatrices turn a death-match into a shared triumph, and they roared for it, and Tahna and To'ria walked out of that arena arm in arm.
 In the tunnels beneath the arena, the animal handlers were already unraveling. A lion loose mid-bout wasn't bad luck. It was a failure, and they knew exactly whose neck it would land on. Word went out fast — voices raised — calling down every man they could pull from elsewhere in the compound to help account for what had gone wrong. Guards who should have been at their posts were suddenly needed somewhere else entirely.
 When Tahna and To'ria came back in off the sand, still catching their breath, they found Tasset'uh waiting for them in the passage.
 Tasset'uh simply beckoned and they followed. They knew whose hand had released the lion, and they knew, too, who had cleared the passage while the crowd was busy cheering a miracle.


 Chapter 2. — Out of By-zantium.

 The tunnels beneath the arena were built for animals and the men who managed them, low stone and narrow turns, and in the confusion nobody had thought to watch the three women who didn't belong there.
 Tahna and To'ria still carried what they'd fought with. Disarming them hadn't occurred to anyone in the scramble to explain a loose lion — and so a falcata and a xiphos came with them, unremarked, into the dark.
 Tasset'uh already had a blade of her own, taken quietly from the beast cages.
 She led them without hesitating, through turns she seemed to already know. Handlers' voices carried from side passages, sharp with panic, none of it aimed at them. Tasset'uh moved through the noise without slowing.
 They came out through a low service door into the cool dark beyond the compound walls, the city's noise different out here — market close, temple bells, someone singing badly two streets over.
 They had perhaps a hundred paces before the cry went up behind them.
 A headcount, a door found unbarred — it didn't matter which. What mattered was the shout that followed, and the sound of hobnailed sandals breaking into a run on stone.
 "Move," Tahna said, and they moved.
 By-zantium at that time was narrow lanes and blind corners, walls close enough on both sides to touch. She cut left down a street that looked like a dead end, ducked under a hanging line of drying fish, vaulted a low wall into a courtyard that put them three streets further than any pursuer would guess.
 It wasn't enough. By-zantium's watch knew their own city too.
 Two men came out of a side alley ahead of them, blades already drawn, shouting for them to halt — and To'ria didn't slow down to consider it. The falcata came up and around in the same motion as her stride, catching the first man across the throat before he'd finished the word, and she was past him and into the second before he'd registered what had happened to his partner. He didn't get his blade up in time. Neither of them would be standing watch again, that night or any other.
 Behind them, a third pursuer rounded the corner at a dead run and met Tahna instead. She didn't have To'ria's speed, but she'd never needed it. She stepped inside his reach, took the strike on her forearm instead of her chest, and put the xiphos in under his ribs, quick and clean.
 He went down without a sound. She was already moving again before he finished falling.
 More voices behind them now, closer than before, torchlight starting to bounce off the walls at the mouth of the lane. Tasset'uh caught To'ria's sleeve and hauled her sideways through a gap between two buildings barely wide enough for a shoulder, and Tahna followed. There was only time to run and trust that the woman who'd gotten a lion loose in a guarded arena knew how to get three women out of the city.
 They lost the torches in the tanners' quarter, where the smell was bad enough that even a determined watch didn't want to linger, cutting through standing water and worse until the shouting behind them thinned into confusion, then nothing.
 By the time By-zantium's outer wall rose up ahead of them, the pursuit had scattered into search parties combing the wrong streets, calling to each other across distances that told all three women exactly how lost their hunters already were.
 They avoided the gate. Tasset'uh found a stretch of wall where the stonework had crumbled enough for a foothold, and they went over it instead, dropping into the scrub beyond breathing hard, blood on two blades that wasn't theirs.
 They stayed close to the wall that night, watching the gates, listening for a search party to emerge.
 The night was quiet, but at first light, a search party came out from the city gate, four men, unhurried, assuming the fugitives to be miles from the city by now.
 Tasset'uh spotted them first. She watched them long enough to learn their spacing, then went back for the other two.
 What happened after was deliberate. To'ria and Tahna had killed men in the dark of a city, fast, out of necessity. This time there was no need for speed. Four men went into that stretch of scrub looking for three fugitives. None emerged.
 By the time anyone in By-zantium worked out that a search party had gone missing, the three gladiatrices were already miles to the north, leaving no trace.


 Chapter 3. — The Wrong Wind.

 Tig'rah-ta'vaa had spent three winters alone by choice, and intended to continue that way.
 She had been born into the confederation of the Roxolani, one of the great Sarmatian peoples. Her family group had ridden as auxiliary cavalry for Rome for eleven years, forty riders under her father, holding a stretch of frontier that Rome didn't have the men to hold itself. They were paid in grain, in land rights, in a governor's word that the arrangement would last as long as they kept the ground quiet. It lasted until a Roman patrol raided a village north of the line and the governor needed someone to answer for it. The blame fell on her father's riders, and they were called to a muster, disarmed, and killed by the same officers they'd ridden beside for eleven years. The governor's treaty with the tribes further north closed within days.
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa had not been there. She was further east, moving the herd to summer pasture. She came back to a burned camp and forty bodies. Since that time, she had known what a Roman promise was worth.
 A woman alone on the steppe would not have been expected to survive, but Tig'rah-ta'vaa had made a study of defying expectations.
 She tracked and pillaged Roman patrols now, work she understood and that Rome kept supplying regardless of clan. She travelled on horseback; her mare was as much a part of how she read the ground as her eyes were. This patrol, six men and a junior officer, she'd been shadowing for two days, learning their rhythm, waiting until the time was right.
 That was when she spotted the other three.
 They were tracking the same patrol. Badly.
 She watched them for the better part of a day before she made up her mind about them. Three women, plainly fighters, with no instinct for the wind at all: one carried herself like a blade in motion, one seemed certain of the outcome already, and the third hadn't said a word all day, yet somehow conveyed the most.
 She came down off the ridge, leading the mare, and didn't bother with a greeting.
 "You're standing in the wrong wind," she said. "They'll smell you before they see you."
 Three heads turned, but none of them flinched. These weren't women who startled.
 "You've been tracking us," the sharp-eyed one said.
 "I've been tracking them," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said, with a tilt of her head toward the distant patrol. "You've been getting in my way doing it badly. Figured I'd fix that before it got one of you killed."
 She didn't explain further — she'd watched them, decided they were worth the trouble of correcting, and that judgement was enough. Whatever came next between the four of them, it would be built on that: a hunter's plain accounting of what something was worth.


 Chapter 4. — Remembering.

 Weeks on the steppe had taught four women to move as a unit. Tig'rah-ta'vaa still scouted alone, still vanished for hours at a stretch and came back with information about the richest pickings, but the other three had also learned to read her silences almost as well as they read each other.
 They needed supplies. Winter was close and four women moving through open country couldn't easily carry what they'd need to outlast it. Tig'rah-ta'vaa had made that plain enough without saying it outright: fewer arrows spent, more grain taken.
 This time she'd spotted a small supplies convoy, consisting of five soldiers and one young officer, accompanying three wagons of supplies.
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa opened the attack from a distance, unseen. An arrow into the throat of the officer was all it took to throw them all into confusion. The column broke formation instantly. Tahna was already moving before the officer slid from his horse, xiphos flying. Tasset'uh moved behind her, silencing the struggling.
 That left To'ria the wagons, and the two men still standing between her and them.
 The falcata came up and she was already past the first man, already turning into the second, and somewhere in the space between them, something in the depth of the fight — the dust, the shouting in a language that wasn't familiar, the particular quality of fear on a soldier's face — took her mind, for only a brief flash, back in time to a hillside and smoke that didn't belong there. Her mother's voice, cut off mid-word.
 She had been small enough, then, that she couldn't understand what the men who came through her village were doing. Rome did not explain the putting down of trouble in Iberia to the daughters of the people who'd caused it; Rome simply took what was useful and left the rest behind. To the Romans, To'ria had been useful — young, quick, worth more alive than dead.
 She didn't remember the ship. She remembered the smoke, and her mother's voice, and then nothing at all until years later, in a different country, when a wooden sword was put into her hands by a man who called it training.
 The second soldier's blade caught her across the forearm — not deep, but enough to snap her back to the present.
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa found her a moment later, blood on her hands and a brief, lost look in her eye. She recognised immediately the particular stillness that came over a person reliving a flash of the past.
 "You're bleeding," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said.
 "It's nothing," To'ria said, which was true of the wound.
 They took what the wagons carried — grain, salted meat, a cache of coin. They took the officer's horse as well, the only mount worth the trouble among the column's tired draft animals. Winter would be survivable now, and the horse would let them cover ground faster than four sets of tired feet ever had.
 Nobody asked To'ria what she'd seen in that half-second she'd gone somewhere else. Tasset'uh, who understood better than any of them what it cost to carry something silently, rode with her on the way back to camp — just near enough to convey an understanding.


 Chapter 5. — The Falx.

 Dacia in winter was bleak. It had been a Roman province for over a hundred and thirty years, but the imperial infrastructure hadn't altered the wild landscape.
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa sighted the woman from a rise, downwind, unseen. She watched for longer than usual, then came back to the others with her report.
 "One woman. A local, not Roman — the falx makes that plain enough, and the fact that she's standing over a dead surveyor and isn't hiding the body." She paused, turning something over in her mind. "She's not hiding herself either. Looks like she wants to be found. By the right people."
 "And are we the right people?" Tahna asked, amused by her own question.
 "I think," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said, "she will know the answer to that better than we do."
 They found her just as Tig'rah-ta'vaa had described, quite unbothered by their arrival, her grip on the falx unaltered.
 To'ria spoke first. "That's a strange place to leave a dead Roman."
 "It's not for want of caring," the woman said. "I want the next one to see him."
 It was a bold enough answer that To'ria's mouth twitched toward something sharper — an edge she rarely bothered to leash when a stranger tested her — and it might have gone further than words if Tasset'uh hadn't simply looked at her. To'ria exhaled through her nose and let it go.
 "I'm To'ria," she said instead, and if her voice was a shade cooler than it had been a moment ago, the stranger didn't seem to notice. "We're headed north. You're welcome to ride with us, if you've a mind to."
 "I have a camp a half hour from here," the woman said, with a glance that took in the two tired horses and the four women. "You'll want a rest before going too much further." She turned toward a picket line half-hidden in the brush, where a horse of her own stood already saddled, and swung up onto it without waiting to see if they'd follow.
 Her name was Tee'aahta, and her camp was better kept than four fugitives expected from one woman alone — dry wood stacked, water close, the fire built low and smokeless in a hollow. She moved through her own space with the same flat certainty she'd shown standing over the body, and soon the five of them were settled around the fire.
 It was Tahna who broke the quiet first, because something about Tee'aahta's self-assurance needled her.
 "You kill Roman surveyors for sport, or is there a cause behind it somewhere?"
 "There's a province behind it," Tee'aahta said softly. "A hundred and thirty years of one. You'd know something about causes, I'd think, given how loudly you don't have one."
 Tahna's smile didn't move, but her eyes narrowed. "I have plenty of cause. I simply don't feel the need to announce it to strangers with strange swords."
 "Falx," Tee'aahta said.
 "I know what it is."
 "Then you'll know it's not for show either." Tee'aahta turned the blade slightly where it lay across her knees. "You wear your skill like it's an entitlement, rather than having earned it. I've known many Roman officers like that, and they tend not to survive contact with people who earn what they carry."
 The fire cracked. Nobody else spoke.
 Tahna's jaw tightened, and her voice was thin. "You don't know anything about what I earned."
 "Then tell me," Tee'aahta said flatly.
 Tahna looked at the fire for a long moment before she answered, and when she did, her face had become sober.
 "I wasn't born to a cage," she said. "My family had standing — not wealth, but standing, in a town on the coast that many thought too small for pirates to bother with. We were wrong about that." She turned a piece of kindling over in her hands. "I was fourteen. They didn't take the town. A dozen men came ashore at night, took what they could carry that breathed, and were gone before anyone could raise an alarm."
 "The market at Delos, or somewhere smaller?" Tee'aahta asked, not unkindly.
 "Smaller. It doesn't matter. What matters is the man who bought me didn't buy me for my arms or my back. He watched me for one day and he told his overseer I'd last longer than the fighters twice my size, because I wasn't going to make the mistake of fighting fair." Something almost like the old smirk flickered back, thin and bitter now. "He was right. Eleven bouts. Eleven women who thought strength was the only thing that mattered in that sand, and eleven times I let them believe it right up until it killed them."
 "And the arrogance?" Tee'aahta asked.
 "It's not arrogance," Tahna said. "It's arithmetic. I already know how most fights end before they start. I've simply never found it useful to pretend otherwise."
 Tee'aahta studied her for a moment longer, then nodded once. "Good," she said. "I can use arithmetic."
 It wasn't exactly warmth that passed between them after that, but it was something neither of them had offered a stranger before. To'ria let the last of her earlier temper go now that she understood there was nothing in the woman's bluntness aimed at any of them personally.
 They sat around the fire long after the wood had burned down to nothing, and for the first time since By-zantium, the silence between them was truly comfortable.


 Chapter 6. — A Leader.

 Tee'aahta was the first awake. She had the fire stoked and food prepared before the others stirred. Soon all five were ready to take on the day.
 "Where are you headed?" asked Tee'aahta.
 "We don't entirely know," To'ria admitted. "We started out running. That's simple enough to understand — By-zantium wasn't going to let three escaped gladiatrices stay as guests." She glanced at Tahna.
 "But running can only be a beginning," Tahna said. "Away isn't a destination. We've killed enough patrols. Taken enough supply. Put enough dead officers back on Roman desks that someone has to explain them. We're not avoiding the empire anymore. We're hurting it, but it's only small cuts. Nothing it can't absorb."
 "Cuts do add up," To'ria said, her passion rising. "But there are garrisons out past where anyone bothers to look — frontier postings, undermanned, half-forgotten by whoever's supposed to be minding them. Hit the right one, hard enough, in the right place, and it isn't a supply raid anymore. It's a message that travels further than we do. Rome doesn't fear losing men. It fears looking like it's losing control of what it already holds."
 "You mean to make the empire flinch," Tee'aahta said.
 "I mean to make it bleed somewhere it can't hide the woond," To'ria said. "Whether that's the same thing or not, I haven't decided yet."
 Tee'aahta was quiet for a while after that, turning the falx over where it lay across her knees.
 "That's not a plan," she said. "That's an appetite with a direction. Which isn't nothing — appetite is more than most people manage. But it isn't a plan yet either."
 She paused, but no one had an answer.
 "I'm not ready to move on this morning," Tee'aahta went on, standing, banking the last of the fire with the flat of her boot. "There's a matter or two I haven't finished settling in this province, and I'd rather not leave it half done. But I wouldn't mind the company while I finish it."
 It wasn't an offer, exactly. But all four of them understood it for what it was — that whatever this had been until now, a coincidence of four women who happened to move well together, was becoming something with structure. Something that would need more than appetite and a direction to survive contact with an empire that could afford to lose entire legions and barely notice.
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa caught To'ria's eye across the dying fire, and To'ria caught Tahna's, and even Tasset'uh, watching all of it with her usual unreadable stillness, allowed something in her expression to show resolve.
 They had been four women surviving by their own separate wits, working together and benefiting from the companionship. What stood in front of them now, banking a fire with unhurried certainty, was the first among them who didn't survive by wits alone. She survived by strategy. And strategy was the one thing that would forge their unique skills into a deadly weapon.


 Chapter 7. — Old Ground.

 The matter Tee'aahta hadn't finished turned out not to be any particular individual, but a process.
 "A survey team," she told them, as they broke camp. "Six men, an escort of four, working their way along the old boundary stones. They're not measuring the land for its own sake. They're measuring it so it can be given away — parceled out to men who've never set foot on it, veterans owed land for service, officials owed favours. Once the stones are moved and the record's made in Rome, it stops being anyone's but theirs. That's how a province disappears. Not all at once. Stone by stone, until one day the name on the map is the only Dacian thing left."
 They moved together — all five, for the first time since Tee'aahta had joined them, rather than splitting off in pairs the way old habit still sometimes pulled them. It was Tee'aahta who called for it, and it was Tee'aahta, scouting the ground an hour ahead of the survey party's expected line, who turned to Tig'rah-ta'vaa rather than to any of the others when the terrain finally opened up in front of them.
 "You reed ground better than anyone I've fought beside," Tee'aahta said. "Where would you put us?"
 It wasn't flattery. Tee'aahta didn't deal in flattery, any more than she dealt in comfort, and Tig'rah-ta'vaa understood the question for what it was — a genuine ceding of judgement on the one thing Tig'rah-ta'vaa had always done better than anyone else.
 She studied the tree line, the fall of the ground, the place where the old boundary stones sat half-swallowed by grass.
 "There," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said, pointing to a shallow rise choked with scrub, upwind of the stones and low enough to stay unseen. "I would open from here. The escort will break toward the noise instead of toward the surveyors, which buys you the six that actually matter. Tahna and I can pin the escort from two angles once they scatter. Tasset'uh takes whoever's left standing when it's done. You and To'ria go straight for the stones and the men measuring them, before anyone thinks to run with the record they've already made."
 Tee'aahta looked at the ground Tig'rah-ta'vaa had marked out for a long moment, turning the plan over, and nodded once. "That'll do."
 The escort broke exactly as Tig'rah-ta'vaa had said it would, chasing the noise instead of the threat that had actually hit them, and by the time they understood which direction the real danger had come from, three were already down and the fourth was running in a direction that wouldn't save him. Tahna finished what Tig'rah-ta'vaa started with cold economy, and Tasset'uh moved through the confusion leaving no one alive.
 To'ria and Tee'aahta came on the surveyors from the tree line together, and the falx and the falcata did their work.
 One of the surveyors — older, grey at the temple, a man who'd clearly done this work in many provinces — simply stood over the half-driven stake he'd been setting, hands empty, and said something in Latin before To'ria's blade ended it.
 She stood over him afterward longer than required.
 "You understood him," Tee'aahta said.
 "Enough of it." To'ria didn't look up. "He said it wasn't personal. That he was only doing the measuring — someone else decides where the lines go, he just walks them." Her jaw tightened. "Someone said something very like that to me once before. Different words. Same meaning."
 Tee'aahta didn't press. She simply waited.
 "I was small when Rome came through my village," To'ria said, at last. "Small enough that nobody explained anything to me. There'd been trouble — I never learned what kind, or whether it was even trouble my own people had caused. Rome doesn't bother finding out before it burns something down. It just puts down what's in front of it and calls the job finished." She finally looked up. "I remember smoke. I remember my mother's voice stopping in the middle of a word. I don't remember the ship, or the market, or most of the first year after. Just that I was young, and quick, and worth more living than dead, and that was the whole of the mercy anyone showed me."
 "And the surveyor," Tee'aahta said quietly.
 "Was measuring the same kind of thing that was done to me," To'ria said. "Somebody's already decided where this land goes. He's just walking the lines so Rome doesn't have to admit that's what happened. I know that walk. I've been the thing at the end of it."
 Tee'aahta was quiet for a moment, then reached down and pulled the half-driven stake free of the ground herself, tossing it into the brush.
 "Then let's make sure this stone stays exactly where it was," she said. "Whatever else changes."
 It wasn't comfort, not the way Tasset'uh's silences offered comfort, or the way Tahna's bluntness sometimes did despite itself. But To'ria understood it all the same — the first time since her mother's voice had stopped mid-word that anyone had looked at what Rome had done to her and offered something like restitution.


 Chapter 8. — The Cost of Silence.

 They returned to Tee'aahta's camp, and Tasset'uh built the fire, quickly and without wasted motion. She had always been the quietest of them, and by now the others had simply accepted her as she was, but Tee'aahta noticed it differently.
 "You haven't spoken since I joined you," she said, once the fire had caught and they had all settled. It was a flat, exact statement, and somehow more disarming than sympathy would have been.
 Tasset'uh didn't look up from the fire.
 "You either don't speak because you can't, or because you've decided against it," Tee'aahta said, "You're not the first kind."
 Tasset'uh still remained silent, but her shoulders had begun to relax.
 Tahna shifted, about to step in, but Tee'aahta lifted one hand, and Tahna settled again.
 "I don't ask to be cruel," Tee'aahta said. "I ask because I've noticed you're the one among all of us who never once has to be corrected. Tig'rah-ta'vaa reads people. Tahna reads fights. To'ria acts before she thinks and is usually right anyway. But you—" she paused, choosing her words carefully, "—you never do anything that needs undoing. That's not nature. That's discipline. And discipline like that is built, not born, whatever quiet you started with."
 The fire crackled, and one of the horses whinnied.
 After another long silence, Tasset'uh spoke. "I was always quiet."
 The words came out low. It was the first full sentence any of them had heard from her.
 Tee'aahta simply nodded. "I imagine you were. But quiet isn't silence. Not this kind." She paused a moment before going on, more gently. "What made the difference?"
 Tasset'uh was quiet again for a long while. She was still looking at the fire when she finally spoke again, each word costing her great effort.
 "My father," she said. "Owed a debt he could not pay." She paused. "He found a way to pay it that did not cost him anything he valued."
 "Rome bought what he sold," she said. "That is all there is."
 Tee'aahta simply nodded.
 "That's enough," she said, a note of respect creeping into her voice.
 Tasset'uh went back to the fire, but a stillness stole over the little group. They had all witnessed a change in Tasset'uh. She had opened up in a way she had never done before, and only those who understood her could appreciate what it had cost her.


 Chapter 9. — The First Target.

 They broke camp before sunrise, and Tee'aahta had already decided how the morning's riding would be spent.
 "You asked me for a plan," she said, bringing her horse alongside To'ria's. "I have the start of one."
 "There's an outpost northwest of here — a watch-station, the kind Rome scatters along the frontier. A dozen men, perhaps fewer by now, forgotten by whoever's supposed to be rotating them out. That's not an accident. Rome doesn't forget things by mistake. It forgets them because remembering costs coin it would rather spend elsewhere."
 "And that makes it worth our time because—" Tahna prompted.
 "Because a message needs a place to land," Tee'aahta said. "You've been cutting patrols and supply trains for months. Rome absorbs that — it doesn't like it, but it barely slows down. A watch-station is different. It's a fixed point. It reports in on a schedule. When it stops reporting, someone has to ask why, and whoever answers that question has to explain it to someone above them, and so on, up a chain that doesn't like admitting a dozen of its own simply vanished off a frontier it swore was secure."
 "You want them to notice," To'ria said.
 "I want them to have to explain it," Tee'aahta said. "There's a difference. A raided supply train is bad luck. A watch-station gone silent, with nobody left to say what happened to it, is a question nobody in command wants asked — because someone has to answer."
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa turned that over without comment for a while before she spoke. "How many men have actually manned it, that you know of?"
 "I don't know precisely. That's the first thing we need to find out before anything else." Tee'aahta glanced her way. "You'll want eyes on it before we're anywhere near close enough to be seen."
 "I shall," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said.
 "And after?" To'ria asked. "Once it's fallen?"
 "After, Rome sends someone to ask questions nobody can answer," Tee'aahta said. "And somewhere far south of here, in a room none of us will ever see, someone with far too much authority and far too little information starts wondering whether the frontier he's been assured is secure actually is." She paused. "That's the plan for now. Not to win a war — just to make sure the people who think they're winning one start doubting it."
 They rode on into country that grew wilder with every mile — the road thinning to a track, trees closing in from both sides until the sky narrowed to a strip overhead. Before long the ground grew too broken and close-set with timber for the horses to press further, and they went the last stretch on foot, leading the animals into cover thick enough to hide both.


 Chapter 10. — A Silent Station.

 Tig'rah-ta'vaa found it two days before the others reached it — a timber watch-tower and a low palisade wall, set on a rise where the old frontier road bent north, manned by fewer men than even Tee'aahta had guessed.
 "Wood, not stone," she reported, back at the tree line where the others waited. "One gate. The watchtower at the northeast corner, one man in it most hours, sometimes none. They've gone soft here."
 "Good," Tee'aahta said. "Soft is what we came for."
 This was not a patrol, strung out and vulnerable on open ground. A watch-station had walls and a gate, and men inside it who could bar a door and buy time even in their panic.
 "No one escapes," Tee'aahta said. "Not a rider, not a runner, not a shout carried far enough to matter. This isn't a message we're sending with a survivor. This is a message we're sending with silence. Rome doesn't get an account of what happened here. It gets an absence."
 It was Tig'rah-ta'vaa who took the tower, going up and over the palisade in the dead hour before dawn. The man on watch never called out. He simply stopped being a threat, and the tower's height became theirs instead of his.
 Tahna and Tasset'uh took the gate. To'ria and Tee'aahta went through the barracks door together, and what followed inside wasn't the kind of fighting any of them had done in open ground — no ambush to spring, no formation to shatter, just close, fast, brutal work in cramped quarters, men waking to their own deaths.
 It was over before the sky had properly lightened. Nine men dead, and not one of them had gotten a shout past the walls.
 They left it standing — gate shut again, palisade intact, everything in its place except the men who should have been walking it. From a distance, riding past on the frontier road, it would look like nothing was wrong at all. That delay was the whole point. The longer it looked normal, the longer before anyone thought to ask why it had stopped answering.
 They took two horses from the station's small paddock before they left — sturdy, unremarkable frontier stock. To'ria claimed one, and Tahna took the other, muttering something about finally not having to share a saddle with anyone's elbows.
 "How long before someone notices?" To'ria asked.
 "Depends how badly Rome wants to admit it's stopped watching its own frontier," Tee'aahta said. "Days, if we're fortunate. Longer, if the men meant to check on it are as soft as the men we just met." She looked out over the quiet palisade, the closed gate, the ordinary morning settling over the station. "Either way, by the time anyone works out what happened here..." she trailed off.
 They struck north again into the deep timber, leaving the station standing silent behind them.


 Chapter 11. — Out of Place.

 The country north of Dacia thickened into forest by degrees, as they rode between the old Iazyges pastures to the west and the Hasdingi holdings to the east. It was Gothic country now and none of them expected to find much this deep in it beyond game trails and old grudges.
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa lay along a fallen trunk for the better part of an hour before she came back to report her find, which was rather strange — Tig'rah-ta'vaa didn't usually need an hour to decide what she was looking at.
 "There's a woman half a mile ahead, dressing a boar over a low fire," she said. "Alone. No camp markers, no signs of kin nearby, nothing that says she's expecting company or minding a herd for someone else." She paused. "She doesn't belong here."
 "Meaning what?" Tee'aahta asked.
 "Meaning I've seen Gothic dress and Gothic weapons the whole way through this stretch of forest, and hers isn't either. Fair enough hair for it, but the cut of her leather, the way she's built her fire, the way she's carrying herself — none of it is local. She's a long way from wherever she started."
 Tee'aahta considered for a moment. "You think she's dangerous."
 "I think she's competent," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said. "There's a difference, though it doesn't always work in our favour. She hasn't looked up once in an hour, which either means she hasn't noticed me — unlikely — or she has, and doesn't consider one watcher in the trees worth spoiling a meal over." A faint, private note of respect crept into that last part. "I'd want your read on her before we get any closer. This isn't ground I know as well as you do."
 It was, in a way, its own kind of compliment — Tig'rah-ta'vaa conceding a judgement to someone else's territory rather than trusting her own instinct.
 Tee'aahta studied the tree line for a long moment, though there was nothing to see from here but green and shadow. "If she's been taken from somewhere and dragged this far south, there are only a few ways that happens," she said, half to Tig'rah-ta'vaa, half to herself. "Gothic raiding parties push north against the tribes along the Baltic coast more than Rome ever bothers to. If she's not Gothic and not local, that's the likeliest story — captured somewhere up past where any of us have ever been, brought south, and somewhere along the way, she stopped being anyone's prisoner."
 "You've seen this before," To'ria said.
 "I've seen enough of it," Tee'aahta said. "Rome isn't the only power on this continent that profits from moving people who don't belong to it." She straightened up. "Let's go and find out for ourselves, rather than guess at it from a hundred paces out."
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa led them in, quiet as ever, and the woman by the fire didn't so much as glance up until they were within a dozen paces of her — at which point she looked up, entirely unsurprised, as though she'd been waiting for them.
 She was tall and broad, an axe within easy reach of one hand and a boar half-dressed in front of her.
 "You've been standing in my tree line for over an hour," she said, in accented but serviceable Latin, nodding once toward where Tig'rah-ta'vaa had been. "I was starting to wonder if you were ever going to come down and say something, or if I'd have to finish this boar alone."
 "I'm Tov-aeh," the woman added, turning back to her work without waiting for a reply. "You're all welcome to join me, because there's more boar here than I can eat."


 Chapter 12. — Rugii.

 The boar was good, and Tov-aeh made sure everyone said so before she let the conversation move on.
 She fed the fire generously, yet without waste, tearing off the better cuts of meat and passing them along before anyone asked for more, laughing easily at Tahna's dry remarks in a way that made even Tahna's smirk soften into something like genuine amusement. By the time the meal was down to bones and embers, she'd made herself the most immediately likable person any of them had met.
 It was Tee'aahta who finally set the warmth aside long enough to ask what the rest of them had been too well-mannered to ask outright.
 "You're not Gothic," she said. "You're not local to any tribe within a hundred miles of this fire. And you speak Latin better than most of the Romans I've killed for the privilege of practising mine." She said it with the same flat directness she always used. "I'd like to know how you came to be sitting alone in this forest, that far from wherever you started."
 Tov-aeh didn't flinch. If anything she seemed almost relieved to finally have the question asked.
 "Rugii," she said. "Though I doubt the name means much to any of you this far south. My people held the coast a long way north of here — lovely cold water, bitterly cold winters. The kind of place Rome's never once bothered sending a legion, because there's nothing there Rome wants badly enough to freeze for it." A faint, wry smile touched the corner of her mouth. "The Goths wanted it badly enough, though."
 "Gothic raiders took you," Tee'aahta said.
 "Took quite a few of us, over a few bad years. I wasn't the only one, and I wouldn't have been the last." Tov-aeh turned a piece of bone over in her hands, not quite looking at any of them now. "They didn't keep me for themselves, not for long. I was traded on. Somewhere in all that moving, I ended up in the keeping of Vandal traders. Hasdingi, if the distinction matters to you."
 "It does," Tee'aahta said, quietly.
 "Then you'll know they'd made themselves useful to Rome by then. Land for loyalty, or something close enough to it — I never heard the whole of the arrangement, only enough to understand that whatever I was worth to them, some part of that worth was owed, one way or another, to keeping Rome content." The wryness in her voice had hardened into a touch of bitterness.
 "How did you get free of them?" To'ria asked.
 "Badly, and slowly, and mostly by being underestimated," Tov-aeh said, the warmth creeping back into her voice, threaded now with real satisfaction. "People who move captives for profit get careless about which of us they think capable of anything beyond carrying what we're told to carry. I waited until careless turned into an actual mistake, and then I made sure it cost them more than they'd bargained for." She shrugged, as if the details weren't worth the breath. "I've been on my own since. Long enough now that I'd stopped expecting company worth keeping — until an hour of watching five strangers not quite manage to sneak up on me."
 That drew something close to a real laugh around the fire, Tig'rah-ta'vaa included, which was itself no small thing.
 "Any interest in trading one kind of alone for a different kind?" To'ria asked, when the laughter had settled.
 Tov-aeh looked at each of them in turn.
 "I've been waiting for something worth my time since I stopped being anyone's captive and started being nobody's anything," she said. "I don't know yet if that's you. But I know I'd rather find out than spend another season alone in this forest wondering." She picked her axe back up, resting it easy across her knees the way Tee'aahta rested her falx. "So. Where are we headed?"


 Chapter 13. — West.

 Nobody answered right away. It was Tasset'uh, in the end, who did.
 She rose from where she'd been sitting and drew her sword. She held it out, arm level, blade steady, and turned until it settled due west.
 That was the whole of her answer. She didn't sheath the blade again right away, and she didn't look to Tee'aahta, or to anyone, for confirmation.
 Tov-aeh studied the line of the blade for a moment, then looked back at the fire, at all of them, with something that might have been the beginning of real respect. "West," she said. "Away from the Hasdingi. Away from the people who sold me."
 "Away from what took you," Tee'aahta said, "but not away from what it means. West is only a direction, not a destination."
 They were moving before the fire had fully died, west and further west. Tov-aeh fell into the line easily.
 It was Tig'rah-ta'vaa, again, who found the trouble before the trouble found them.
 "Party ahead," she reported, dropping back from a rise just wide enough to see through the trees. "Six, maybe seven. Moving east — toward us. Loaded down, from the look of their pace. Coming home from somewhere."
 Tov-aeh's jaw tightened at the word. "Hasdingi carry themselves a particular way when they're heavy with plunder," she said. "That's them."
 "Then they're a long way from home to be careless," Tee'aahta said. "Tov-aeh—" she paused, "—this one's yours to lead, if you want to."
 Tov-aeh's answer wasn't in words. She simply hefted the axe, already moving toward the tree line that would put her closest to whatever came first.
 The Hasdingi party walked into the ambush carelessly — strung out, talking too loud. Tig'rah-ta'vaa's arrow took the first, and the line broke into chaos.
 Tov-aeh didn't wait for the formation to fully collapse. She went in low and fast, her axe finding the gap between one man's shield and his ribs before he'd registered the threat. There was nothing showy in it, just a raw, focused violence that hadn't been visible at all around the fire where they'd found her, and To'ria, working the opposite flank, found herself admiring the passion of their new companion.
 Tahna and Tasset'uh closed the rest of it — fast, economical, no wasted motion — and within minutes the party that had walked out so carelessly wasn't walking anywhere again.
 Tov-aeh stood over the last of them a moment longer, breathing hard, something unreadable in her eyes.
 "Feel any different?" To'ria asked, not unkindly.
 "No," Tov-aeh said, after a moment. "I thought it might. It doesn't." She wiped the axe clean, matter-of-fact again, the warmth already creeping back into her voice. "Turns out revenge isn't really what I wanted."
 They took what the party carried and pushed on until the light had nearly gone, making camp in a hollow Tig'rah-ta'vaa chose.
 The fire that night burned easy. Tov-aeh told the story of the boar and even the corners of Tasset'uh's mouth resembled a smile.


 Chapter 14. — Along the Frontier.

 They moved west for the better part of a month. One territory gave way to the next.
 Through Iazyges country the going was easiest, open plain and old grazing ground, and Tig'rah-ta'vaa moved through it easily, familiar with this kind of ground. Tov-aeh rode behind Tee'aahta, whose horse was easily the sturdiest of the five and bore the both of them without complaint. They encountered Iazyges riders a couple of times, and both times the riders simply watched them go. Six women on five horses was a curiosity rather than a threat.
 Where the plain gave out, they picked up the line of the frontier itself and followed it west — close enough to see the watchtowers on a clear day, far enough that no patrol ever crossed their path. It was strange country to ride, Roman order on one hand and everything else on the other. More than once Tee'aahta pointed out some fresh scar on the land — a burned granary, a hastily rebuilt stretch of palisade — evidence of a frontier under more pressure than Rome would admit.
 Quadi territory took longer to pass than expected. The Quadi kept close to Rome by treaty, client kings and Roman garrisons sitting near enough to each other that the band spent most of that stretch moving at night, sleeping through the daylight in whatever cover they could find. Twice they came near enough to a Roman supply column to have taken it, and twice Tee'aahta judged the risk not worth taking. "We're not here to bleed every vein we cross," she said. "We're here to reach the one that matters."
 Marcomaani land came next, rougher and less watched. There was a skirmish, one grey morning in the Marcomaani hills. A hunting party stumbled onto their camp before dawn. They were more startled than hostile, but it ended fast and ugly. Three of the hunters died before either side understood what was happening. The rest scattered back into the tree line rather than press a fight they hadn't wanted in the first place. Nobody in the band was hurt, but it left all of them quieter than usual for a day afterward.
 By the time the ground softened into the river valleys of Alemaani territory, autumn had turned the whole frontier brown and gold.
 The Alemaani themselves were newer to this ground than almost anyone else along the frontier — a confederation still finding its feet, drawn together out of smaller peoples.
 "They're young, as peoples go," Tee'aahta said. "Still deciding what they are. That makes them dangerous in ways older tribes aren't — nothing to lose yet that they haven't already lost once in getting here."


 Chapter 15. — In Full View.

 Thilda had been walking north for three days when she found the smoke.
 It wasn't battle-smoke — she knew that smell too well by now to mistake it for anything else — but a cook-fire, banked low and careful, the kind of fire built by people who understood exactly how far its scent would carry. That alone was enough to slow her step. Careless fires got built by careless people, and careless people didn't survive long in the country between Raetia and Upper Germania, not with Rome pressing from the south and the Alemaani raiding parties coming down from the north.
 She had been walking this particular stretch of the border for several years now. Her own people held ground that had not interested any invaders — no gold, no grain worth an emperor's attention, just forest and hard winters and a stubborn refusal to disappear quietly the way so much of this frontier had already been persuaded to. That refusal had cost them. It had cost her, specifically, more than she cared to consider. She scouted for herself now.
 The fire, when she finally worked close enough to study it properly from a fold in the ridge above, belonged to six women — all of them armed, their weapons close at hand. She watched the way they sat, the way their eyes never all rested in the same direction at once, the easy economy in how one of them fed the fire without looking away from the tree line.
 These were women who had survived together long enough to trust each other.
 She watched for the better part of an hour, before she made her decision.
 She stood, in full view, and walked down toward the fire.
 They were on their feet before she'd covered half the distance — fast, controlled, six weapons raised. She stopped well short of striking range and simply waited, both hands visible, spear planted upright in the dirt beside her.
 "I'm alone," she said, in workable if accented Latin. "I've been alone a long while. I saw your fire three ridges back and decided to investigate."
 A tall woman with a falx stepped forward from among them — the one, Thilda judged, was their leader. "That's a long way to walk on a guess."
 "I've walked further on less," Thilda said.
 They didn't lower their weapons yet, and after a long, measuring moment, the falx-bearer nodded once.
 "Sit, if you're going to," she said. "We'll decide the rest after."
 Thilda sat. She didn't tell them everything that had brought her to this ridge, this fire, this particular late point in a war she'd been fighting alone. That would keep. Some of it would keep for a long time yet.
 But she told them enough — that Upper Germania was her home and had been her people's for centuries, that the losing of ground here had been slow and then sudden, that she had buried more of her own than she could remember.
 It was enough for now.
 Someone passed her a share of the evening's meal — a smaller woman, quick-moving, who set it down beside her without ceremony and went back to watching the tree line. Thilda ate without hurrying, the first meal in a long time that she hadn't had to eat standing, weapon in hand, watching her own back.
 "You've fought Rome directly," the falx-bearer said, at length.
 "More than I've fought anyone else, this past year," Thilda said. "Though the tribes pressing from the north give me nearly as much work. Rome, at least, I understand. It wants land, and it's patient about taking it. The others just want what's left when Rome's finished, and they're not patient at all."
 "That's most of this frontier, in one sentence," the falx-bearer said.
 "You're not from here," Thilda said.
 "None of us are from any one place," said the one with the axe, older sounding than she looked, amusement threaded through it. "That's rather the point."
 Thilda looked around the fire once more, slower this time — the falx, the twin blades resting easy against one woman's leg, the round shield with its worn boss propped near another's shoulder, the stillness of the one who hadn't spoken a single word since she'd walked into the light. Six women who by every reasonable account should have had nothing holding them together.


 Chapter 16. — Roxolani.

 Morning came grey and mild, and Thilda woke to find the camp already stirring around her.
 It was Tee'aahta who made the introductions, moving around the fire with the same flat economy that marked her every action.
 "To'ria," she said, nodding to the one with the falcata. "Iberian-born. Taken as a girl when Rome put down trouble in her province. Gladiatrix out of By-zantium before any of the rest of this started."
 To'ria didn't look up from the blade, but the twitch in her shoulders acknowledged it all the same.
 "Tahna," Tee'aahta went on, nodding to the one with the easy, sharp smile. "Greek. Taken by pirates off her own coast at fourteen, sold into the same arena. Eleven bouts survived on nothing but reading a fight faster than her opponent."
 "Flattering, when you say it like that," Tahna said, though she didn't disagree.
 "Tasset'uh." A level glance from the quiet one was the whole of her greeting. "The breakout that got the three of them out of By-zantium was hers, start to finish. She usually lets her gladius speak for her."
 Tasset'uh's only reply was the faintest incline of her head — confirmation, offered and withdrawn in the same breath.
 "Tov-aeh," she said, nodding to the broad-shouldered one with the axe, who grinned outright and passed over the last of the previous night's meat. "Rugii, from a long way north of anywhere on this frontier. Taken by Gothic raiders, traded on to Hasdingi hands, freed herself in the same patient way she does everything else."
 "And Tig'rah-ta'vaa," Tee'aahta finished, nodding to the one already half a distance apart from the rest, watching the tree line.
 Tee'aahta paused there. "Which is, I realise, a name I've often spoken, but have never heard the story behind," she said, half to Thilda, half to herself, an admission that seemed to genuinely surprise her.
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa's mouth curved, faint and dry. "You've never asked."
 "That's not an answer."
 "No," Tig'rah-ta'vaa agreed. "It isn't."
 She was quiet a moment, and when she finally spoke it was brief, exact, and nothing wasted.
 "Roxolani," she said. "One of the Sarmatian peoples. My family rode as auxiliary cavalry for Rome for eleven years. My father led forty riders. Paid in grain and little else. When a Roman patrol raided a village to the north, the governor needed someone else to blame. My father and his riders paid with their lives for something they didn't do." She paused. "I was moving the herd east at the time. Came back to a burned-out camp and forty unburied dead." She drew in breath and looked out into the distance.
 Nobody spoke.
 After a few moments, Tee'aahta said, "And you'd been alone since."
 "Alone by choice. There were other family groups I could have joined, but all were confederates of Rome." This last she said with obvious distaste, before brightening a bit and going on. "I watched these three for the better part of a day before I ever spoke to them. Decided they were worth the trouble before they knew I existed. That's simply how I do things. It served me well enough on my own. It's served well since."
 Tee'aahta studied her for a long moment, the same weighing look she gave everyone, and nodded once — the closest thing to a verdict she ever handed down.
 Thilda, watching all of it unfold around a fire she'd been sitting at for less than a full day, found herself, for the first time in years, in no particular hurry to be anywhere else.


 Chapter 17. — South.

 They didn't break camp with sunrise.
 They sat with the fire still smoking between them. Tee'aahta, falx across her knees, looked across at Thilda.
 "You know this ground better than any of us," she said. "Before we decide where we're riding next, I'd rather hear where you'd take us."
 It wasn't a small thing, and Thilda understood it. She'd spent a year listening to men who gave orders without ever once asking what the ground actually permitted, and the difference impressed her.
 "North along the border, the way you've been moving, you'll keep finding what you've already found," she said. "Watch-posts, patrols, the occasional supply line worth bleeding. Useful. Steady. It won't ever be more than steady, though — Rome expects losses along that stretch, budgets for them, barely blinks." She nodded, slowly, westward, away from the frontier entirely. "West, the ground changes. Fewer eyes, less discipline. Harder country to move through safely. To the south, about a day's journey, is the fort of Lorkh, which is right at the point where the frontier turns north. All the other forts have the frontier east and west. Lorkh has the frontier north and east, so they don't think they need to watch west or south. I've spent a year learning exactly how much Rome doesn't watch what it doesn't think it needs to. It's more than you'd guess."
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa, listening from the edge of the fire, spoke up. "If we go south, I'll need a couple of days ahead of the rest of you to read it properly."
 "Take what you need," Tee'aahta said, without hesitation.
 Tov-aeh leaned back, arms folded, something considering in her expression. "South also puts more distance between us and the nasty cold," she offered. "No harm in that."
 "And less distance from Hispania," To'ria said, almost wistfully.
 Tee'aahta let all of it settle before she spoke again, each voice folded into the decision.
 "South, then," she said, at last. "Not blindly. Tig'rah-ta'vaa goes ahead alone, same as always — she'll move faster and quieter without the rest of us slowing her. The rest of us hold somewhere safe until she's read enough of it to bring us in behind her."
 Nobody argued — not because Tee'aahta's word was law, but because everyone at that fire had just watched her actually listen before she decided.
 "There's a hollow half a day south of here," Thilda said, into the pause that followed. "Good water, better cover, nothing on the approach that isn't visible a long way off. If Tig'rah-ta'vaa needs a few days to read what's further south, that's where I'd sit and wait for her word, rather than push the rest of us blind into ground even she hasn't seen yet."
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa nodded once, already turning it over. "That works. I'll move faster alone than any of you could keep pace with regardless."
 "Then that's settled," Tee'aahta said.
 They broke camp within the hour — south, toward the hollow Thilda had named, seven of them moving together while Tig'rah-ta'vaa struck out alone ahead, into ground none of the rest of them, apart from Thilda, had seen before.


 Chapter 18. — The Fort.

 Tig'rah-ta'vaa left before the others had finished breaking camp. She took the mare and nothing that could rattle or catch light.
 She passed Alemaani farmsteads first, scattered and new, the wood still pale and unweathered. Further on the land roughened into forest. She slowed the mare to a walk and read the ground at that pace instead.
 She found a trail by midmorning: old but used, running south, worn by feet rather than hooves. People without horses to spare. Poorer country, and likely the loose, unwatched ground Thilda had promised.
 It wasn't long before the trail crossed a ford — shallow and worn smooth by long use, with nothing standing guard over it. No palisade. No watchtower. Every Roman crossing she'd ever scouted had one, even a poorly manned one. This had none. She sat above it a while, working out what Thilda had meant back at the fire — not ground Rome had forgotten, ground Rome had never bothered learning about in the first place.
 That alone would have been enough for the day, but she followed the far bank west a while instead, out of habit, and that was when she saw it — straight lines against the tree line, where nothing straight grew on its own.
 The fort. Stone footings, from what she could make out, and enough of a footprint to house a real garrison. She spent the rest of the afternoon working the tree line in a slow half-circle, getting close enough to be sure without being seen.
 It sat at the exact point where the frontier turned north and stopped watching west, just as Thilda had said. The walls faced east and north, back the way the frontier came from. On the west side, behind the fort itself, a supply track ran wide and open — wagons coming and going on a plain schedule, because nobody expected trouble from that direction.
 She counted what she could see. Enough men to matter, not a skeleton crew. That supply road was undefended, and the whole fort was built facing away from the road.
 This was the target To'ria had imagined, back before any of this had a plan behind it — a fixed point, not a scratch Rome could shrug off. Nobody had known then where to find one. Now there was one, sitting exposed on its blind side.
 She marked the road, the blind approach, and the two places a rider could cross the ford unseen, then turned back before the light went.
 It was full dusk by the time she reached the hollow, later than she'd said she'd be. Tee'aahta was on her feet before Tig'rah-ta'vaa had cleared the tree line.
 "You found it," Tee'aahta said.
 "I found the garrison," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said.
 The others gathered without being called. Tig'rah-ta'vaa laid it out plainly — the ford, the fort, the supply road nobody was watching.
 "Stone-built. Real numbers, not a skeleton crew. We're not walking in and clearing it the way we cleared the watch-station." She looked at To'ria. "But it's the one you asked for. Back before Tee'aahta, before any of this had a plan. That road is wide open. We can't take the fort, but we can make the men inside it stop trusting it."
 To'ria was quiet a moment. "How long to plan it properly?"
 "Longer than tonight," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said. "But it can be done. I saw how."
 Tee'aahta looked around the fire once — To'ria still, Tahna already turning it over, Tasset'uh watching, Tov-aeh's hand resting on the axe across her knees, Thilda leaning in.
 "Then that's where we're going," Tee'aahta said.


 Chapter 19. — Released.

 They crossed the ford at first light, and said nothing until the fort's shape rose clear through the trees ahead of them.
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa split them to their work. She and Tahna went to ground on the west side, within sight of the supply road and the blind stretch of wall behind it — the side Tig'rah-ta'vaa had already read as open, and the side that mattered most to confirm before anything else. Tasset'uh and To'ria worked around to the east, where the wall stood higher and better watched, reading what a real approach from that direction would cost, in case it was ever needed. Tov-aeh and Thilda ranged the ground behind the western tree line, marking cover, water, and a line the horses could hold without giving anyone away.
 What the west side gave up matched everything Tig'rah-ta'vaa had reported. Wagons rolled through the gate on that side on a loose, trusting schedule, waved through without a challenge called down from the wall. No patrol crossed that ground all morning. Every patrol that did go out swung east and north instead, walking the line the fort had always watched.
 "They still don't look this way," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said, low, watching a wagon roll through unchallenged. "Not once, all morning."
 Tov-aeh found ground worth using not far back from the road, on the blind side — a hollow, water close, cover thick on three sides. Thilda walked its edges herself before she'd trust it.
 "There's a patrol runs somewhere near this ground," she said, once she'd finished. "Small. Four men, maybe five. Not part of the northern loop — a stray from it, most likely. If we hold here quiet, we take it clean tomorrow, well clear of where we're sitting."
 Tee'aahta turned that over, the falx across her knees. "Tomorrow, then. Tig'rah-ta'vaa first light, eyes on their—"
 She didn't finish it.
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa was already on her feet, one hand raised flat for quiet, head turned toward the trees south of the hollow. A moment later the others heard what she'd caught first — voices, unhurried and very close.
 "That's them," she said, low. "The same patrol. Come further off their line than they should have, and headed straight toward us." She looked to Thilda. "This was meant to be clear ground."
 "It should have been," Thilda said, already moving. "They shouldn't stray this far from the northern loop."
 Tee'aahta was up, falx in hand.
 "Positions," she said. "The ones we marked for tomorrow. We use them now."
 They moved fast and silent, each of them already knowing where Thilda's ground had placed her, where Tov-aeh's strength would matter, where Tig'rah-ta'vaa's bow would open it. The patrol walked into it exactly as Tig'rah-ta'vaa had read them — unhurried, unaware — and the ambush took four of them down within a dozen heartbeats of the first arrow.
 The fifth was young, and when To'ria's blade came level with his throat he didn't raise his own to meet it. He dropped it instead, both hands open, shaking hard.
 "Hold," Tee'aahta said, before To'ria's arm had finished its swing.
 To'ria held.
 Tee'aahta studied the boy a long moment.
 "How long have you served here?" she asked, in workable Latin.
 "Six months," he managed. "Less."
 "Then you're young enough to still remember what fear feels like," Tee'aahta said. "That's useful to me today." She nodded to To'ria, who lowered the blade. "You're going to walk back to that fort. Not run — walk, so you have time to remember all of it. Tell them four of your patrol are dead and you're the one left to say so. Tell them seven women did it, in less time than it takes to explain, on the side of the fort nobody thought needed watching."
 The boy said nothing. He seemed to have lost the power of speech altogether.
 "Tell them exactly that," Tee'aahta said. "Don't soften it for your own sake, and don't guess at anything you didn't see. The truth, plain, is worth more to me than anything you could invent to shrink it."
 They stripped him of blade and shield, anything that might let him claim afterward he'd fought rather than cowed, and let him go.
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa watched him go until the trees took him, then turned to the others.
 "That wasn't silence," she said. "That's the opposite of the watch-station."
 "It was meant to be," Tee'aahta said. "A forgotten station gets erased quietly, and nobody above it ever has to explain the gap. A garrison this size, with men left who'll ask questions when four of their own don't come back — that fort needs a story it can't control, told by someone who was there and can't be accused of exaggerating it." She looked toward the fort, still standing exactly as it had that morning, not yet knowing what it had lost. "Let them wonder what's coming next. Wondering costs more than four men ever could."
 They left the ambush site and moved deeper into cover before the fort could send anyone out looking. Behind them, a boy walked alone toward a garrison that would soon be a great deal less certain of what stood outside it.


 Chapter 20. — Movement in the Dark.

 They moved twice more before dark, each time a short distance — always toward better cover, never far enough to lose sight of the fort itself.
 The fort sat low against the last light, and they made no fire that night.
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa took the vigil during the hours around first light — the hours most difficult to remain alert, yet most likely to be fruitful.
 She set herself further out than the previous watch, out past the edge of camp where a sleeper turning or a horse shifting its footing couldn't reach her. She wanted nothing to distract her.
 For a long while there was nothing. The fort held its shape against a sky just beginning to turn grey. Not a single torch moved along it. Nothing.
 Then something changed — she couldn't have said what.
 She froze and strained every sense into pinpoint focus on the base of the wall.
 There it was. A movement, then nothing.
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa held her focus.
 Again, slightly north — a flicker at the tree line. Then stillness — complete, absolute.
 That was enough.
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa came back toward the sleepers, and touched Tee'aahta's shoulder once.
 "Something came off that wall," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said. "One movement, then a second further north, moving into the trees."
 "Down," she said. "All of you. Now."
 The other five were awake instantly, low against the ground, weapons ready.


 Chapter 21. — The Tigress of Lorkh.

 Tahran had learned, over the last three years, that captivity didn't need bars to be a cage.
 She awoke that morning, as always, in the small chamber off the Pryfectus's own quarters, dressed in the clothes required by the household, her red hair loose and flowing, as required.
 Breakfast she took standing, near the door, as required. She didn't mind it. Standing let her see the room.
 The morning brought two officers up from Raetia, minor men on some minor errand, and the Pryfectus did what he always did when new eyes came through his gate — found a reason to have her brought into the hall and, in the particular tone he used only for this, mentioned that she'd been taken in Britannia three years past, that she'd fought three of his own men to a standstill, that even now, three years tamed, she could probably still put a blade through a man's throat if she were given the opportunity.
 "Show them," he said, as he held out the practice blade.
 She showed them. It cost her nothing to move well for an audience that thought they understood what it meant — proof of Rome's reach, proof that even the wildest thing dragged out of the furthest edge of the map could be brought to heel and kept, years from home, still red-haired, still dangerous-looking. She'd learned to give them exactly that and nothing more. The part of her that actually knew how to kill a man, she kept for herself, and these shows helped to keep her hand in.
 The two officers left before midday, suitably impressed, and the household settled back into its ordinary rhythm — one she'd had three years to learn in every particular. She knew which guards drank in the evening. She knew the armoury was checked at dawn and rarely again before dusk. She knew the Pryfectus's moods better than the rest of the household. She knew when he wanted her silent, when he wanted her shown off, when a bad week made him crueler and a good one made him softer.
 She had spent three years watching all of it. She had no plan yet, but had made herself patient instead. She knew that she would recognise the moment when it came.
 Dusk came grey and early that day, cook-fires already lit, and she was standing where she always stood at that hour — near the Pryfectus, in the long room where he took his reports.
 A lad was brought in still shaking — stripped of blade and armour, dust and worse on him, eyes full of fear.
 "Speak," the Pryfectus said.
 The boy spoke.
 Four of his patrol were dead. They were killed inside a dozen heartbeats, on the western ground nobody watched, by seven women who'd laid an ambush. They'd let him go. They'd told him to walk, not run, so he'd remember it right. They'd told him to tell it plain.
 The room went very still.
 Tahran watched the Pryfectus's face — the flush, the anger, the fear. He shouted something at an aide. He demanded numbers, demanded a search, demanded answers. Nobody moved with any real purpose. Everyone in that room had spent so long believing the western ground didn't matter that not one of them had the first idea what to actually do now that it had proven otherwise.
 She stood very still herself, near the wall and felt a cold certainty settle in her chest that she hadn't felt in three years.
 Seven women were on the western ground, waiting, watching, moving exactly when the moment offered itself and not a heartbeat before.
 She had no idea who they could be, but she knew that whoever they were, they were the same type of woman as her — women who had been taken and measured and put on display for what other people needed them to be, who had decided, at some point, to stop being anyone's trophy and start being something with teeth of its own.
 The Pryfectus was still shouting when she slipped out of the hall. She took the passage that led to the armoury that nobody would check again before morning.


 Chapter 22. — Between the Sentries.

 She took a pair of matching blades — a little larger than daggers — and a couple of other items from the armoury. Getting them out of the praetorium was the easy part.
 The fort itself was another matter. The praetorium sat at its centre, walls in every direction, and centre-to-wall was ground she'd only ever crossed under guard.
 Once out of the praetorium, she found a gap between the granary and the wall it backed onto, and waited.
 The garrison took its time settling. Voices carried a while longer than she'd have liked, and lights moved behind the praetorium's shutters long after they should have gone dark.
 By the deep hours all was quiet. She moved to the outer wall, counting the intervals between the sentries on the rampart above her.
 She found her place at the wall's lowest stretch, where old repair work had left the stone rougher, and went up it hand over hand, pausing with every hold to listen.
 She stopped short of the top and hung there, flat against the stone, and waited for the two sets of footsteps above her to meet.
 She'd chosen her spot for exactly this — the point on the rampart where the two sentries' paths crossed, where both men would be furthest from her at the other end of their beat. Their footsteps met and passed, and she waited until they were distant, then she went up and over, flat on the walkway for less than a breath, and dropped to the outer face.
 She hit the ground at the base of the wall and went still, folded small against the stone, and did not move again until both rhythms had passed a second time.
 Then she slid along the base of the wall, keeping its shadow, until she stood opposite a stretch of forest to the north.
 She waited once more. The footsteps met and passed again. She crossed the open ground, reached the tree line, and froze, listening.
 She held there a moment longer, Fort Lorkh behind her for the first time in three years.


 Chapter 23. — Unfamiliar Ground.

 For three years she had been an object of curiosity in a household of military men. She had never set foot outside the walls of the fort. She had no idea what to expect here, apart from what one frightened soldier's account had told her: seven women, and four Roman dead, west of the fort.
 When she judged she was deep enough into the cover of the trees, she turned west, and held that line.
 The sky was beginning to turn from black to grey behind her, the outline of the fort falling further behind, to her left.
 After several minutes, she turned south, keeping the fort to her left.
 She hadn't gone far on the new bearing when she was stopped in her tracks by a blade at her throat.
 She went perfectly still.
 Silence.
 Then a form began to materialise out of the dark ahead of her, large and foreboding, closing without a sound, carrying a weapon she'd never seen the like of, a curved blade nearly the length of her own arm.
 "Tasset'uh. Thank you." The voice was that of a woman.
 The blade left her throat as quietly as it had arrived.
 Tahran didn't move. Whoever this was had not lowered her guard, the curved weapon held loose but ready.
 "You came from the fort," the woman said.
 "I did."
 "Alone."
 "Yes."
 "Why."
 "One of the Pryfectus's men came back to Lorkh tonight talking about seven women and four dead men. I was in the room when he told it. I've spent three years there, taken from Caledonia, kept as something to be shown off at dinner. I didn't wait to hear it twice."
 "Anyone see you leave?"
 "No."
 "You're certain."
 "If they'd seen me, half the province would know it by now."
 The woman studied her a long moment. "You're telling me you walked out from under the Pryfectus's nose, scaled the rampart, and picked your way in the dead of night through a forest you'd never set foot in, to look for a band of killers — on the strength of a report from some deranged young upstart."
 "That sums it up well."
 "That was either very brave or very stupid."
 "I've had three years there to learn that the difference doesn't always matter. Only the outcome does."
 The woman moved in closer. "Tee'aahta is my name," she said. "You'll answer for yourself properly once we're back with the others. Until then you walk between us, and you keep both hands where Tasset'uh can see them."
 "That's fair," Tahran said.
 "It isn't meant to be fair," Tee'aahta said. "It's meant to keep you alive long enough to prove you're worth trusting."


 Chapter 24. — The Eighth.

 Four of them stayed at the camp. To'ria took the near ground, Tahna beside her, both watching the fort's dark wall. Tov-aeh and Thilda held the far edge, facing the trees — because whatever Tee'aahta, Tasset'uh, and Tig'rah-ta'vaa had gone out into the dark to find could just as easily come back out.
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa reappeared first, moving low and fast between the trunks. "A woman from Lorkh. Tee'aahta's bringing her."
 Tee'aahta walked the stranger out of the trees, Tasset'uh a pace behind, and stopped in the open ground at the centre of the camp. "This is Tahran," Tee'aahta said. She looked at Tahran. "Tell them your tale. See if they believe you."
 Tahran didn't hesitate.
 "Three years ago they took me out of Caledonia. Since then I've belonged to the Pryfectus at Lorkh — paraded in front of his guests as proof of what Rome does to things it can't kill outright. Tonight a soldier limped home with a story about seven women and four dead men on ground to the west of the fort. I heard him tell it before the household. I decided this was my moment. Securing some weapons, I slipped out of the praetorium and waited until just before first light before scaling the wall. Nobody in that fort knows I'm gone yet."
 Tahna spoke first. "A woman doesn't cross ground like that on a soldier's rumor unless she's either got nothing left to lose, or she's very good at making people believe she doesn't."
 "Both have kept me alive this long," Tahran said. "Which one I am is yours to judge."
 "That shouldn't be difficult," To'ria said. "We seven aren't still alive through luck alone."
 Tov-aeh didn't speak. Her eyes held the look of someone who remembered exactly how it felt to be measured this way.
 Tee'aahta turned to Thilda. "No movement from the fort?"
 Thilda shook her head. "Nothing yet."
 "Good. But it won't be long."
 The grey in the eastern sky was already beginning to give way to shades of deep purple and burnt orange.
 Tee'aahta looked at the eight of them gathered in the half-light — seven who'd earned their place, and one other.
 To'ria was already moving, passing what little they had left between hands. Tov-aeh checked the horses, running a hand down each foreleg in turn. Tahna kept her eyes on the fort. Tahran ate what was passed to her gratefully.
 Dawn broke and the fort remained silent — no riders, no alarm, no patrol. Whatever else was unproven about her, that much of her story was true.
 Tov-aeh was the first to draw alongside her. She said something too quiet for the others to catch. Tahran answered her, just as quiet. After a brief silence, Tov-aeh reached out an arm and drew Tahran into a gentle embrace. For the first time since Caledonia, Tahran allowed herself to relax.


 Chapter 25. — Not Possible.

 The Pryfectus was clearly rattled.
 "Numbers," he said to his aide, slower this time. "I want to know exactly who went out on that patrol, by name, and I want it before I decide anything else."
 The aide went, and he turned again to the lad.
 "The western ground, you say? Tell me again."
 The lad told his account again. Nothing in the second telling made any more sense than the first.
 The Pryfectus doubted him — not the lad's honesty, exactly, but his sense. A recruit six months into his service, sent out onto ground he'd never patrolled before, could mistake a great many things in bad light.
 "And you say it was women."
 "Seven of them."
 Women didn't do that. They just didn't — well, Tahran had put three trained men to shame—
 He glanced toward the wall where she usually stood, and found the space empty. Gone to the kitchens, likely, or sent for water. It didn't matter. She wouldn't have gone far.
 But Tahran was Caledonian. Whatever the wilds out past the northern sea bred into its women, it wasn't bred into — not into Roman women, who were subdued and quiet and knew their households, not even into the Germanic women along this frontier, tractable enough once a firm hand had been laid on them. Tahran herself had proven it. Three years, and she stood by a wall and said nothing unless spoken to. Even the wildest of them came to heel eventually.
 "There were seven, you said."
 "Yes."
 "It couldn't be right! Not even seven women from these parts could stand against one patrol of my men. If we were in Caledonia, I might give it credence, but not Germania."
 "They didn't fight the patrol so much as end it," the lad said.
 "And they sent you back," the Pryfectus said. "Say that part again. What exactly did they tell you?"
 "To walk. Not run. So I'd remember it right."
 Not possible. He'd fought Caledonians — men who killed everything in reach and left nothing behind to carry a message. Whoever had done this had thought about him specifically, had decided what he should be told and how he should be made to hear it, and that was a cruelty he did not think possible.
 The gate captain's voice carried from the passage outside, and behind it the tramp of the sentries being gathered in.
 "Ah, here is the watch! So, this lad is of the belief that he is the sole survivor of this evening's western patrol. It's a fabrication, isn't it!"
 Nobody could answer him straight.
 One man had stood a rotation two nights past and sworn the gate hinge had complained without a hand on it, and put it down at the time to wind. Another had heard nothing at all, and said so emphatically. A third began an account of a shape at the tree line that unraveled halfway through into something he could no longer swear he'd actually seen, and stopped, red to the ears, rather than finish it. The gate captain tried twice to bring some order to the accounts, but voices climbed over each other, each man's fragment both feeding the fear in the room and refusing to amount to anything tangible.
 The Pryfectus felt the room slipping out from under him.
 "Enough!"
 The room went quiet.
 "Whatever is out there," he said, "will keep till morning. Go to your posts. All of you."


 Chapter 26. — Vanished.

 The villicus was up before the household.
 He worked through the morning's accounting — stores, staff, bread, water, the kitchen fires.
 Tahran should have been at her door by now.
 He didn't think much of it at first. The whole household was off its rhythm this morning; she'd be a few minutes behind, that was all.
 He went back to the stores, and a while later he checked again. No sign of her near the Pryfectus's rooms, nor in the kitchens either.
 He went to her chamber, and eased the door open. The room beyond it was bare. The bed hadn't been slept in, and what few things were hers were gone.
 He stood in the doorway a moment, then headed straight for the Pryfectus's chamber.
 The Pryfectus was already in discussions with his centurions, arguing over what to do about the western ground. An aide took one look at the villicus and turned him away.
 "Not now," he said. "He's occupied. I'll call you."
 So the villicus went back to the stores and waited. It was close to an hour before he was called.
 By then the sun was well up, and the household had moved on without him. Men were mustering in the yard — shields were being checked, horses were coming up from the stable fast.
 He found the Pryfectus in the middle of it, half into his armour, giving orders.
 "Speak quickly," the Pryfectus said, not looking at him. "I've a garrison to move."
 The villicus told him.
 The Pryfectus's hands stopped on the strap he'd been buckling. First seven women destroy a patrol. Now Tahran was gone from her chamber.
 "Gone," he said. "Where? Since when?"
 "I don't know. Her chamber was empty this morning."
 The Pryfectus raised a hand, and the yard went still.
 "Halt it," he said. "All of it. Nobody rides west until I know what happened inside my own walls."
 He sent men to search — the praetorium, the outbuildings, the granary, the stable, the cellar. He called the night watch in a second time and made them account, one by one, for every hour of dark.
 The search found nothing. The gate had been barred all night. The night watch had not seen anything.


 Chapter 27. — The Ambush.

 Markellus had known his cousin since they were boys, and he had never seen him the way he'd looked that morning — armour half-fastened, giving conflicting orders.
 He didn't argue when the command fell to him. Someone with a clear head needed to ride out west.
 "Take what you need," the Pryfectus told him, gripping his forearm. "Bring back the dead. Bring back the truth, whatever it turns out to be. I'd rather know."
 Markellus rode out with a full century, foot and horse both, and they soon found the site of the previous night's ambush.
 There were four bodies, roughly where the lad had said. Markellus dismounted and knelt by the nearest of them.
 The wounds were clean. Whoever had done this was skilled, and they hadn't been afraid.
 "Recover them," he said, straightening. "Gently. They rode out as my men. They come home as my men."
 The century went to work. The horses stood patiently and weapons were laid aside.
 He walked the ground while they worked — churned earth where more than four had clearly stood and fought, and then nothing. No trail leading off in any direction. Whoever had done this had left no trace.
 A shout went up from the tree line to the north.
 Markellus was moving, and four infantrymen fell in around him. "Hold the recovery," he called back to his optio. "Cavalry stays with the dead. I'll see what this is."
 He didn't get thirty paces into the trees when the man leading his file went down — an arrow found his throat, buried to the fletching.
 "Down! Cover!" The infantrymen scattered.
 There was no second shot.
 Then from the south a second shout went up, followed by the sound of another man falling.
 Markellus understood that this wasn't the same threat. The distance between the two was too great to be the same archer.
 He turned back toward the horses.
 "Cavalry, mount!" he shouted, as he reached his own horse.
 The other men were already swinging into saddles around him, horses shifting, unsettled by the shouting and the smell of blood already thick from the four dead being loaded. Markellus had one hand on the pommel, and was swinging his foot over, when the horse reared beneath him.
 It gave him no warning, and he was thrown off before he'd gotten his weight over the saddle, hitting the ground in a daze.
 A woman broke from the tree line to his left, a spear leveled, and put it into the chest of the nearest mount. Another came from the right with an axe, swinging low and taking a second horse's legs out from under it — the animal going down screaming, the rider caught under it.
 Behind him, at the rear of the line, two more forms emerged, blades already out. Markellus understood. This attack wasn't aimed at his men. It was aimed at the horses. Every single one of them.
 He rolled over and got a hand under himself, lifting himself up, when the next woman approached.
 She came unhurriedly through the chaos, a massive curved blade held with two hands, and went straight for his own horse where it still reared and screamed above him — one swing, low and hard, and the animal's legs buckled under it, sending it down in a heap. She was already moving on to the next.
 He tried to get to his feet, but a hand closed over his shoulder from behind, and a blade found his throat.
 "Don't," a voice said, close to his ear.
 A second woman appeared at his other side, matching pace as he was hauled to his feet and moved away from the wreckage of his century. She gripped his arm with a power he would never have expected from a woman, and a faint smirk lifted one corner of her mouth.
 "You shaved well this morning, centurion," she said. "Don't make us do it again now."
 The blade against his throat remained steady while his mind reeled, unable to comprehend where his discipline had failed.
 As he was escorted from the scene, the rest of the assault dissolved back into the trees, leaving a heaving, dying mass of horseflesh and men.
 Amidst the screaming of dying horses, and the cries of wounded men, not one man of his own century had seen their commander being taken.


 Chapter 28. — Captive Ignored.

 Tasset'uh and Tahna walked the centurion into the clearing, blade still at his throat.
 The others had also slipped away from the scene of chaos, and they all converged on the campsite.
 Tee'aahta suggested they take Markellus to a lone birch at the edge of the clearing, where the firelight thinned into shadow, and to bind his wrists behind it.
 "We should get him talking," Tahna said. "See what we can find out about how that fort actually runs. See if he'll tell us anything useful."
 "He won't be able to tell you much," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said. "My father's men lived under it eleven years — Rome calls it Diskepleena, a man drilled to hold his place and take his orders, nothing more. He's just a spoke in the big Roman wheel. He won't know much more than his own patrols. He'd have no idea what's happening further up the line."
 "He deserves a good thrashing anyway," To'ria said. "He rode out this morning thinking he was in command. Let's give him something else to think about instead."
 "He was in command, as far as he's concerned," Tov-aeh said. "Trouble is, if we knock him around, he'll tell himself he took it like a soldier, faithful to his Emperor. It'll just give him more to crow about."
 "He and his cousin treated me like a cur," Tahran said. "He deserves no better himself."
 Tee'aahta was quiet a moment, before turning to Tahran.
 "You know, Tahran," she said, "you're right. He doesn't deserve another moment of our time."
 She turned back to Tahna and Tasset'uh. "He might as well enjoy the birch for company tonight, and we'll see to him in the morning."
 Tasset'uh had held the blade steady at his throat all this time, and they now escorted him to the birch, where they sat him down and bound him. They both stood and walked away, Tahna casting a single sneer back in his direction before completely ignoring him.
 Tov-aeh had built the fire up higher than usual, feeding it with her usual generosity. Thilda sat close to the flame with her spear across her knees, unhurriedly working a nick out of the blade with a whetstone. Tahna sat down and stretched her legs toward the fire, letting her head fall back, eyes half-closed.
 "Four horses down before any of them understood what was happening," To'ria said. "I've never seen a line come apart that fast."
 "They never had a chance to hold anything," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said, not looking up from the arrow in her hands. "They split their own line chasing two shouts that weren't where the real danger was. By the time they went back for the horses, half the century was still in the trees looking the wrong way."
 Tee'aahta sat a little apart, the falx laid flat across her knees. She'd already heard the whole of it once, walking back — every angle, every kill, exactly how the ambush had folded itself back into the trees.
 It was Tahna who turned the talk toward Tahran.
 "And our newest terror," Tahna said, tipping her chin at Tahran, the old sharpness softened. "South side, drawing off half their foot before any of them knew there was a fight. First real work with us, and she puts an arrow through a man's throat before he's gone ten paces into the trees. I'd call that a fair morning's introduction."
 "I had the angle Tig'rah-ta'vaa gave me," Tahran said. "She read the ground. I only had to be where she said."
 "You only had to hold and not flinch," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said. "Most people flinch the first time it's real. You didn't."
 Tov-aeh reached over and knocked a shoulder against Tahran, warm and solid. "First time with us, and already better than half the recruits I've ever seen a Roman drillmaster claim credit for."
 "I've held a bow longer than I've held anything else," Tahran said. She turned the yew stave over in her lap, the wood worn smooth in places. "This very one. Just not for three years."
 Even Tee'aahta leaned in at this.
 "I went into the armoury for the blades," Tahran said. "I never knew what he'd done with my bow — never really thought about it."
 She paused.
 "It was hanging above his own armoury rack. Something for his guests to admire and be told about — the wild woman's bow, same as the wild woman herself." Her jaw set, hard, before she went on. "I never knew he'd kept it. Not until I saw it hanging there last night."
 She ran a thumb lovingly along the grain.
 "I took it down with my own two hands," she said, with a hint of satisfaction. "It still remembers my draw."
 Tov-aeh's hand found her shoulder again.
 "Then it's yours again," Tee'aahta said, at last.
 The fire crackled. The centurion sat too far from it to feel its warmth, but close enough that every word had reached him.
 Thilda finished with the whetstone and laid the spear aside, testing the edge once against her thumb before she seemed satisfied. "First watch," she said, already rising. "I'll take it."
 "I'll spell you at the turn," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said, and that was the evening finished.


 Chapter 29. — Watching.

 The Pryfectus stood at the tower rail, the sentry beside him.
 He had told himself that a commander belonged where he could see the ground his men were on. Below, in the yard, were the men who had watched him flailing for control. Up here there was only the western ground in front of him, the sentry beside him, and the wooden rail beneath both hands.
 The sun had gone and the sky was darkening. He had been up there since midafternoon, watching the same stretch of trees for hours.
 "Sir."
 The sentry was peering into the dark. A moment passed before his hand came up.
 "There," the sentry said. "Coming out of the tree line. Low ground, past the old ford marker."
 The Pryfectus followed the line pointed out by the sentry, and could just make out several figures moving in a loose scatter, coming on foot.
 A century had ridden out with horse and foot both, yet all he could see was on foot.
 "How many," he said.
 "Can't say yet, sir. Too far. Could be a dozen. Could be less."
 His hands closed harder on the timber rail as the line came on slow and uneven, some of the figures leaning on others.
 "Send down," he said. "Gate captain. Torches on the wall. I want every man who can carry a light on that approach."
 The sentry went down, but the Pryfectus stayed.
 He peered into the darkness again, but he could not see a single horse anywhere in the broken line.
 Torches were lit along the wall below him, one after another, and the approaching line of figures stumbled in. He watched for the unmistakable gait of his cousin, but did not find it.
 The gate opened below him, and the column began to file through, into the ring of torchlight in the yard.
 The Pryfectus did not go down to meet them yet. He stood at the rail a moment longer, watching from above, peering at every face in the torchlight.
 His cousin's face was not among them.


 Chapter 30. — Markellus Is Missing.

 The Pryfectus went down from the tower and out into the yard, where the gate stood open and the wreckage of a century was still filing through.
 "Form them," he said, to the gate captain. "Get water into them. Then bring me whoever can still speak plainly."
 A man near the gate, blood dried black down one side of his face, told it start to finish twice in a row, and the two tellings didn't agree with each other. Another, further in, hadn't heard any shout at all — he'd been at the horse lines the whole time, he said, and the horses had simply started dying around him, one after another. A third began an account of the centurion going down and stopped halfway through it, unable to say with any certainty whether he'd seen Markellus fall or not.
 None of it agreed. The Pryfectus had heard that particular kind of disagreement for two nights running now.
 He went from man to man himself rather than wait for the reports to be gathered. He wanted the accounts raw.
 Slowly and unevenly, he began to piece together the story.
 The recovery had been underway — four dead, his cousin's own hand on the work of bringing them in gently. Then a shout from the north, and a man down before he'd gone ten paces past the tree line, an arrow in his throat from somewhere out of sight. Then a second shout from the south and another man down — too far to be the same archer. Two separate threats, deliberately placed and deliberately timed. This had been a well-planned ambush.
 And then there was the horses.
 It was clear from the accounts that the cavalrymen had not been the target of the attack. It was the horses that had been targeted, which was a tactic he could not fathom. Blades to the legs, a spear into a chest, one animal after another went down, its rider thrown or trapped beneath it.
 In the middle of a heaving mass of screaming horseflesh and struggling cavalrymen, not one of them spared a moment to see who was where. It was only back inside his own gate, under the light of the torches, that any of them realised that the centurion was not there.
 They all now assumed that Markellus was dead, yet not one of them would say so.
 "At first light," he said. "A party goes out. Full strength, foot and horse both — what horse we have left. We bring back our dead. All of them. And we do not come back inside these walls until we know that every man is accounted for."


 Chapter 31. — Cold Captive.

 Tig'rah-ta'vaa caught the movement at first light.
 Torches were lit at the gate, but there were more of them than a normal muster needed. She watched long enough to assess: horse and foot both, more men than yesterday, nobody riding out ahead of the rest. She then went back for Tee'aahta and Thilda without waking anyone else.
 "They're moving again," she said. "Same ground as yesterday, by the look of the column. More men this time."
 Tee'aahta was on her feet in an instant. "How many more."
 "They're not expecting an easy morning, I'll put it that way."
 They went together, low along the same ridge Tig'rah-ta'vaa had used the night before, and settled in the trees.
 The column came on slower than yesterday's had. Men rode close together, foot soldiers keeping pace with the horses rather than falling into their own line, eyes going to the tree line every dozen paces.
 "They think we're still out there," Thilda said.
 "We are still out there," Tee'aahta said.
 The recovery went slowly, every man working with a hand on his weapon. A shout went up at one point, and the whole column went still for a full half-minute.
 "They're jumping at nothing," Tig'rah-ta'vaa murmured.
 "That's the point," Tee'aahta said. "Yesterday cost them a centurion and however many of that column they're still counting, in less time than it takes to load a cart. Today they don't know if it's going to happen again, or when, or from where."
 They watched the whole of it — the wounded horses put down where they lay, the dead loaded with more speed than care, a count taken and confirmed, men calling names back and forth across the field.
 They watched the column go, then returned to the camp.
 By the time they were back, the morning at their own fire had already moved on without them.
 Markellus looked like he hadn't slept at all.
 Tee'aahta went up to him and, crouching down, cut the binding at his wrists and ankles.
 "The armour comes off," she said. "The tunic under it, the greaves, the braccae, the boots, the cloak. All of it."
 "But, I'm a Roman officer—"
 "You were," Tee'aahta said, and waited.
 He gave it up piece by piece, hands shaking. When it was done there was a pile at his feet, and a man kneeling on the ground in an under-tunic, shivering in the cold breeze coming in from the east.
 Tahna looked him over once, head to foot.
 "The little man looks cold," she said, and went back to what she'd been doing.
 Tov-aeh dropped a bundle of grey wool on the ground in front of him. "That's the choice," she said. "Wear it, or don't. I don't care either way, but you might."
 It was a peasant girl's dress, plain, worn soft at the seams. He looked away.
 "I'll manage," he said.
 Tahna gave a snort. "That's what he thinks now."
 They rebound his bare ankles, and bound his wrists in front of him this time. He was left at the birch while the camp went on exactly as it would have without him.
 Tahran strung the bow herself that morning, turning it in the light to check the tiller, and slung it across her back when she was satisfied. She sat when she wanted to sit and stood when she wanted to stand. She laughed and she joked. She crossed in front of him a dozen times through the morning, each time ignoring him.
 By midday full cloud cover had blocked out the sun, and the wind was coming from the north.
 "The Pryfectus's silver boy is beginning to tarnish," Tahna said, passing with an armful of wood. "He's going purple around the edges."
 He was shivering uncontrollably now, but said nothing.
 The light went early under the trees, and before long, he was shivering so hard his teeth were chattering.
 "I'll take it," he stammered, when Tov-aeh walked past.
 Tov-aeh nodded once and picked the wool up off the ground. "Can't have you catching cold on top of everything else, can we now."
 "Tahran," she called. "Help me dress the sweet lad, if you don't mind. I think he took his virtus off with his armour and he wants his dress on now."
 Tahran laughed as she came across to help, bringing a blade to cut the binding from Markellus's wrists. He watched her hands as she worked the blade. He knew those hands well — three years hanging idle at a wall, offered a practice blade only when his cousin wanted sport. Now he was watching them deftly handling a blade he must have recognised as belonging to his cousin.
 "Arms in the air," said Tahran, as she slid the dress down over his head.
 "There we are," said Tov-aeh, chucking him under the chin. "That's much warmer than virtus, isn't it."
 "What's virtus," asked To'ria.
 "Virtus? It's a joke," said Tov-aeh.
 To'ria looked baffled. "I don't get it."
 "It's the Roman's idea of what makes a man," said Tig'rah-ta'vaa. "They think only men have the ability to act decisively under pressure."
 To'ria burst into laughter. "He made a decision all right."
 "Vir!" said Tahna, between snorts of laughter, "looks more like a vir-gin now!"
 They were all laughing now.
 "Better tie it to the birch again," said Tahna, wiping her eyes. "It'll shiver itself out of its sack, otherwise."
 Tov-aeh helped Tahran rebind his wrists behind the birch, and they both turned away to the fireside.


 Chapter 32. — Phenomenon.

 The party had ridden out at first light, and the Pryfectus had stayed in the yard through the whole of the morning, until they returned.
 Four rode in, and the rest walked beside litters, ten of them, with canvas drawn over them.
 The Pryfectus approached the nearest rider.
 "Well?" he said. "Well?"
 The rider was young and it took him a moment to understand he was the one being addressed.
 "Sir, we recovered six more of the fallen, as well as the four fallen the previous evening. The ground was—"
 "Is he among them."
 "No, sir."
 "Then where."
 "We don't know, sir. We searched as far as—"
 "You searched." He bellowed. "You searched and you come back and tell me you don't know."
 "Sir, every man who rode out yesterday is accounted for now. Six dead. Fifty-four returned. Centurion Markellus is the only one who isn't."
 "Then go out again," he said. "Now. Every man who can still sit a horse."
 "Sir, the light—"
 "I did not ask you the hour." He had hold of the rider's arm now. "You will turn this province inside out before you tell me a man of mine isn't anywhere. Men don't simply—"
 "Pryfectus."
 Centurion Rufeenus had come up behind him, and touched his arm.
 "Let him go, sir. He's told you what there is."
 "There is more. There has to be more."
 "There isn't. Not today. Not from him."
 The yard had gone quiet. The litters were coming through. A horse shifted somewhere behind him and whinnied.
 "Sir," Rufeenus said. "Not here."
 "I am not finished—"
 "You are finished here."
 Centurion Drahco took the other arm, and between the two of them, they took him into the record room off the praetorium and shut the door.
 It was several minutes before he was calm enough to speak rationally.
 "They saw that," he said.
 "They saw a man who's lost more in two days than most commanders lose in a year," Drahco said. "It will pass."
 "It will not pass. Four men. Then a woman gone from my own house without a trace. Then a full century sent out and come back in pieces. Now this. My cousin. Gone. Not found. Just not there."
 Neither centurion had an answer to this.
 "You need to send word up the line," Rufeenus said. "Tonight. Before it's a week old and looks like something you sat on."
 "And say what." He sounded exhausted. "Say that seven women ended a patrol and left a witness on purpose. Say that a woman kept three years under guard walked out of a secure fort leaving no trace. Say a centurion, my own cousin, vanished off a battlefield, right under the noses of his own century. Say that the same century returned in total disarray, having been shattered by the same hands — by seven women. Say that the entire cavalry division of that century returned on foot, having had — every man of them — his steed cut down beneath him. And I have no consistent account of any of it because not one man among them saw it clearly enough to give account." He looked at them both. "Tell me which part of that I write down, so whoever reads it doesn't think I've lost my mind."
 "Write what happened," Drahco said. "Not what it means. Numbers. Names. What was seen, and by how many. Let them draw their own conclusions."
 "And if their conclusion is that I imagined it?"
 "Then it's their error, not yours," Rufeenus said. "But you don't get to decide that by staying silent instead."
 The Pryfectus sat a long while at the table without touching the wax. Twice he started a line and stopped, reading back what he'd written.
 In the end, he wrote it all. Numbers. Names. What was seen, and by how many. He left out the word "phenomenon", though it was the only word that could rightly describe what was happening. Written down, addressed to a man who'd never stood in his yard, it didn't make any of it easier to believe.


 Chapter 33. — Nobody There.

 Six days passed and no patrol had crossed the western ground.
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa reported it each evening, back from whichever ridge had held her that day. Twice to the north, three times to the east, and once to the south — a foraging party more than a patrol. To the west, nothing.
 "They've stopped believing that ground is theirs," Tee'aahta said, the third evening.
 They made sure the fort knew they were still there.
 An arrow went into the gatepost on the fourth night, about two inches from the sentry's shoulder. On the fifth day, a figure stood at the tree line in full daylight long enough for three separate men on the wall to swear to it.

 The first day, he'd flinched at every shadow that crossed near the birch.
 By the fourth, he'd stopped noticing anything around him.
 He ate what was placed in front of him — bread, a strip of dried meat, whatever was left over — without looking up. He drank when the waterskin appeared near his knee. His wrists had been unbound since the second day.
 To'ria passed within a foot of him twice a day, on her way to the horses, and he'd stopped turning his head to follow her.
 Tahna had stopped needling him somewhere around the fifth night, only because she no longer got a response.
 He answered Tee'aahta once, on the sixth morning, when she crouched in front of him and asked whether he was still capable of hearing her. He said something none of them could understand.
 He had simply become a fixture of the camp — tethered, fed, and otherwise unremarked.

 Tig'rah-ta'vaa caught the muster leaving the fort before it had cleared the gate, and was back at camp with the word before the column had covered a third of the ground.
 "They're coming," she said. "Full strength again, and the Pryfectus riding at the head of it this time. Not a patrol. A rescue."
 There was no panic — the horses stayed tethered exactly where they stood, saddled and waiting.
 They all slid silently into the tree line and watched the column approach. Tasset'uh stayed at the birch until the column was almost on them.
 She cut the tether at Markellus's ankles with one stroke, and vanished into the foliage.

 Drahco had argued against the Pryfectus riding out himself, but Rufeenus had only asked how many men he wanted at his own back, and set about arranging them.
 They approached with extreme caution, every man watching the trees. None of them had expected an easy day, in fact, many of them expected not to come back to the fort alive.
 They found the camp — a fire still smoking where it had been kicked over rather than banked, bedding laid out around it in a rough circle, tack and gear stacked with the particular care of people who intended to come back to it. Horses stood tethered at the tree line's edge, saddled and waiting.
 A form lay near the birch, half-curled on the ground. The grey wool dress Tov-aeh had thrown at his feet days before hung off his shoulders. He had no rope on his wrists, nor at his ankles. There was nothing holding him there at all.
 The Pryfectus was first into the clearing, and first to the form on the ground. He went down on both knees beside it and turned the face up out of the dirt — the same man whose forearm he'd gripped at his own gate, the same Markellus he'd grown up alongside.
 Markellus's eyes found the Pryfectus's and held there a moment, flat and still — without recognition. He said nothing. He made no sound at all when the Pryfectus said his name.
 Rufeenus bent down and took the Pryfectus by the arm. "Come, sir."
 He rose quietly, and the men behind him stooped and, lifting Markellus between them, carried him out of the clearing.

 Nothing came at them.
 Every man in that clearing had spent the walk braced for an arrow out of the trees, a dart from somewhere unseen. They had come prepared to do whatever was necessary to secure Markellus — to fight for him, to buy him, to beg for him.
 The gear was real, stacked with the care of people who intended to return. So were the horses — tethered, saddled and waiting.
 They didn't linger to search the tree line. They left the way they'd come, every man among them certain that eight pairs of merciless eyes were watching their every move.


 Chapter 34. — Rome on Its Head.

 The last frightened Roman was barely out of earshot before the clearing rang with laughter, and those eight pairs of merciless eyes finally let the amusement show.
 "I keep thinking about the gate," Tov-aeh said, turning a piece of kindling in her hands without feeding it to the fire yet. "Not tonight's gate. Tomorrow's. The next watchman who has to stand it, knowing what stood outside it last week and walked away without anyone catching a glimpse."
 "He won't sleep," Tahna said. "None of them will. We didn't have to kill the whole garrison to ruin their sleep for the season — we only had to send one man back to remind the rest what waiting for us feels like." She stretched toward the fire. "I've fought plenty who feared dying. This is the first time I've built one that fears living."
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa nodded, not looking up from the arrow she was fletching. "A man afraid of dying is done costing you anything the moment he's dead. A man afraid of living carries it into every room he sits in, for as long as he lasts. That's worth more to us than half a garrison dead."
 Tahna laughed. "I won't argue that."
 To'ria had been quiet longer than usual. "I keep thinking about tonight's report," she said. "The one the Pryfectus has to write now, once his hand stops shaking enough to hold the stylus." She looked into the fire rather than at any of them. "What does a man like that even say. I lost a patrol. I lost a woman I kept three years and never once thought could leave. I lost a century, and my own cousin's mind besides, and none of it to an enemy I can name to a superior without sounding mad. I'd almost pity the wax."
 They all laughed.
 "It won't stay at Lorkh," Thilda said. "Whatever he writes goes up the line to someone who's never smelled that fort's air. That man reads numbers he can't reconcile, and he has two choices. Believe it, and admit the frontier's rotting. Or doubt it, and hope the next report proves him right." She fed the fire. "Either way, he won't sleep well either. Fear moves up a chain slower than it moves down one. But it does move up."
 "That's the part I like best," Tov-aeh said, leaning forward. "Not just Lorkh. Whoever sits above the man who sits above the Pryfectus. Some official who's never thought about a frontier fort by name, holding a dispatch that says eight women undid a century and walked a Roman officer home in a dress, and nobody can say how it was done." She shook her head. "That's not a bad week for one Pryfectus anymore. That's the whole of Rome on its head."
 "You're imagining a very large collapse from one very small dress," Tahna said.
 "Am I? Rome's whole trick is convincing everyone that who stands over whom can never change. Man over woman. Captor over captive. Citizen over everyone who isn't." Tov-aeh nodded toward Tahran. "Dress one man exactly the way he'd have dressed one of us, tether him exactly the way he tethered her, and you haven't just humiliated a centurion. You've shown every man who believed in that order that it isn't fixed. It only looked fixed because nobody had yet had the nerve to turn it around on them."
 Tahna let her head fall back against the log and gave a dry snort. "Maybe not on its head just yet," she said, the old smirk returning. "But we've got it on its knees, at least."
 Thilda looked up from testing the edge of her spear, glancing south through the dark toward where Lorkh lay hidden behind the ridges. "Right at the knee of their own frontier," she said.
 She turned to Tahran. "You probably heard them talking about the knee, did you?"
 "That's exactly what they called it," Tahran said. "Genu. The Knee. The officers cursed it every muster. Said a wall with a crook in its leg was twice the trouble to guard."
 Tahna let out a short laugh. "They were too busy watching the knee to see us coming up behind the hamstring."
 Tahran was sitting apart from the fire's full light, the bow across her knees, thumb moving along the grain.
 "I only care what it's done to a brute who used to hand me a practice blade and call it generous," she said, finally, eyes narrowing. "Three years, he stood there and let his cousin show me off like a hound that had learned to heel. Now half a garrison lies awake wondering if the thing they can't see is standing at their own door." She smiled a thin smile with no warmth in it. "Let the empire crack if it wants to. I only wanted the one house to know it isn't as safe as it thought."
 Tov-aeh's hand found her shoulder, but no further words were exchanged.
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa let the quiet hold a moment. "I'd be careful how satisfied we let ourselves get, all the same. A frightened system doesn't only despair. Sometimes it hunts harder, because it can't afford to be seen doubting itself. The bigger the crack we imagine we've made, the more men Rome will decide it can spend proving the crack isn't there." She set the finished arrow down. "Tonight, we've earned the fire. Tomorrow, we should assume they're already deciding how much they're willing to lose finding out we exist."
 "They already know we exist," To'ria said.
 "They know a story exists," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said. "There's a difference, and it's the difference that's kept us alive this long. Let's not spend it early because it felt good to imagine the size of what we'd done."
 Tee'aahta had let the whole of it run without steering it. Only now did she weigh in.
 "All of that is true, and exactly how we meant it to be," Tee'aahta said. She let the falx settle across her knees. "But Rome doesn't come apart because of one dead century, or one broken man, or one report a Pryfectus can't bring himself to write plainly. It comes apart from years of it, done over and over, by people it never thought worth watching. Tonight was one weight added to the pile. There'll be others before we're finished." She looked around the fire. "To'ria said as much, the first morning I rode with any of you. Not to beat Rome in open ground. To make the men running it start doubting the ground they're already standing on."
 Tasset'uh hadn't spoken. She fed the fire, reached over once and set her hand against Tahran's arm, and went back to the fire.


 Chapter 35. — Mogoon-tee-aahkoom.

 Priscus had been cornicularius for six years.
 He worked through the week's dispatches at his table — a requisition for tent leather, a complaint about the grain measure at Nida, two decurions arguing in writing over a horse neither of them had paid for. Under those sat a thicker stack, tied with cord, that he'd been putting off since morning. Reports off the limes filled it, fort after fort, Alemaani raiders attacking and destroying at will.
 He cut the cord and read them in order. Men dead, numbers given exactly. Ground lost, or held at a cost the writer wanted noted. Some asked for grain. Some asked for men. Some asked for neither, wanting only the loss set down on record.
 The dispatch from Lorkh was near the bottom, its wax seal already broken by whoever had carried it this far.
 A patrol destroyed west of the fort, one man released alive to carry word of it back. A captive Caledonian woman gone from the Pryfectus's own household that same night. A century sent out to recover the dead from that patrol, ambushed in turn, every horse under its mounted division cut down. And the centurion who'd led that century — the Pryfectus's own cousin, the report made a point of saying so — gone missing in the same ambush, not found, though the search had covered every foot of ground his men could reach.
 Priscus read it a second time, tracing the lines with one finger.
 Cassius had the next table, a tribune six months into the posting, a stack of wax tablets in front of him from the morning's work.
 "Cassius. Read this one."
 He came over without putting his stylus down, and read it standing, lips moving slightly.
 "Peculiar," he said.
 "That's the word I had for it."
 "The patrol, the century, the missing centurion — that's one enemy, one fight, spread over a few bad days. He writes it plainly enough." Cassius tapped a line partway down the sheet. "A captive escaping in the night is unlikely to be tied to two ambushes and a missing man, yet he seems to think it's all one and the same."
 "Why would Alemaani raiders abduct a single Caledonian woman?" Priscus said.
 "He's lost his cousin," Cassius said. "More than a cousin, from the way he writes about him. Grief does strange things to a man."
 "So it goes on the pile with the rest?"
 Cassius shrugged, already turning back to his own table. "We've had worse than this off that stretch this month, from men who kept steadier hands while writing it."
 Priscus set the Lorkh dispatch down with the others — Dalkingen, Abusina, a granary burned near the Nida road — and reached for the next in the stack.

 More than a week passed before the second dispatch from Lorkh came.
 It came in with a courier at dusk — a single sheet of papyrus, shorter than the first had been, the wax seal still soft enough that Priscus's thumb left a print in it when he broke it. He read it standing at the window, the last of the light going grey outside.
 The centurion had been found alive. The first recovery, sent for four dead, had cost the fort the centurion himself. The second, sent the next morning, brought back ten bodies and confirmed he was the only man still missing. Then six days had passed with no patrol sent out at all, before the Pryfectus rode out himself with every man who could still sit a horse. They found a camp — fire still smoking, horses saddled — and the centurion lying near it, alive, stripped of his armour, and out of his mind. He hadn't spoken since. The physician at Lorkh had no explanation for his condition.
 Priscus carried the dispatch to Cassius's table himself.
 "The Lorkh report. From a fortnight back."
 "I remember it."
 "The cousin's turned up."
 Cassius set down his stylus. "Alive?"
 "Alive. Not right in the head." Priscus held the dispatch out. "Read it."
 Cassius took it to the lamp and read it slowly.
 "That's not grief," he said, when he'd finished.
 "No."
 "He writes that six days passed with nobody willing to cross that ground at all. Then he writes that when they finally did, they found a camp — fire still smoking, horses saddled, gear stacked as if whoever left it meant to come back for it." Cassius set the dispatch down. "Six days of nothing, and then a camp that is obviously still inhabited, but no inhabitants."
 "He's held that fort three years. Longer than most out there."
 "A young man losing his nerve after a bad week is nothing," Cassius said. "This one served on the Caledonian frontier before Lorkh. He's no novice, and every other report off that stretch reads like the rest of them. This is something different. We need to send a Benefikiahrius out to Lorkh to see what's happening."


 Chapter 36. — The Benefikiahrius.

 Ligahrius had been Benefikiahrius for nine years, on the governor's staff at Mogoon-tee-aahkoom. His work fell under no centurion. He answered directly to the governor's office, and took whatever posting they gave him. Some months he had none at all. When a report needed checking in person, he went and checked it, and wrote down what he found. He had done this work in three provinces before Germania Superior, and had been recalled to testify at two courts-martial on the strength of his reports.
 Most of his postings were unremarkable. He had settled a dispute over a quartermaster's numbers, and once found that a death on the road blamed on bandits was a quarrel between two soldiers. Generally, that was about as far as his role took him.
 Priscus came to his table one morning in early autumn, two sheets of papyrus in hand.
 "Lorkh," he said. "You'll want to read both of these before I explain any of it."
 Ligahrius read the first, then the second, taking his time over both.
 "What else do you know?" Ligahrius asked.
 "The Pryfectus there has held the post three years, and served on the Caledonian frontier before it," Priscus said. "He's no novice. The tribune thought it was grief. I let it go on that understanding, the first time. I'm not willing to let it go a second."
 "What changed?"
 "The second dispatch." Priscus leaned over and tapped the sheet in Ligahrius's hand. "A found man should be a relief. Instead he's a fresh problem. He can't explain the six days nobody moved, the camp still warm with nobody in it, or what's wrong with the man he found." He paused. "A man losing his composure over a death, I understand. Losing it over a rescue is different."
 "You want me to go."
 "I want someone at Lorkh who isn't the Pryfectus, asking questions the Pryfectus doesn't seem to be able to answer."
 Ligahrius took the dispatches to carry with him. A cornicularius had no authority to send a Benefikiahrius out on a case, and both men knew it. Neither raised the point. The report needed to go further up the chain, whether or not Priscus had the authority to send it there himself.
 He left within the week.
 The road southeast ran well for the first day, then narrowed and roughened as it left Rome's settled country behind. Ligahrius rode alone, a single packhorse behind him, and crossed in four days without incident.
 Lorkh didn't look like a fort under siege. The walls stood intact. The gate was manned. Nothing about it explained two ambushes, a missing woman, and a centurion out of his mind.
 It was the men who told him something was wrong before he'd exchanged a word with any of them.
 The sentry who took his name at the gate skipped the routine questions — travelling alone, what business, how long he meant to stay. He waved him through and kept his eyes on the tree line west of the fort.
 Inside, nobody crossed near the western wall without glancing at it first. Two soldiers stood talking near the granary, and their talk stopped the moment they noticed him.
 The Pryfectus received him immediately and didn't try to hide his relief. Most men in his position treated an investigator's arrival as an accusation before a word had passed between them.
 "You've read the dispatches," the Pryfectus said.
 "Both."
 "Then you already know most of it." He said it without shame. "Ask what you need. I've nothing left to hide."


 Chapter 37. — The Rider.

 Tig'rah-ta'vaa had watched the fort from the same ridge for eleven days, taking the dawn hours because that was when a gate crew was most likely to be tired and careless. Most of what she'd seen in that time hadn't changed: a sentry walking the rampart, smoke starting from the kitchen fires, no patrol willing to cross the western ground. This morning a rider came down the road from the northwest.
 He rode alone. A packhorse followed him on a lead, loaded with two hide cases and a bedroll. No escort came with him, and his pace didn't match a courier's — couriers rode fast and pushed their mounts hard; this man came on at a walk.
 His horse was better fed and better shod than anything she'd seen a common soldier ride. He wore a plain travelling cloak, no armour visible under it, and carried a leather document case slung across his back.
 The gate opened for him before he reached it. She had watched that gate for weeks. Every other arrival — patrol, courier, muster — had been made to stop and give a name before the gate moved. This one wasn't stopped at all.
 She went back and told Tee'aahta what she'd seen: one rider, one packhorse, a document case, no escort, admitted without being stopped.
 "Was he questioned at the gate," Tee'aahta asked.
 "No. It opened for him before he'd reached it."
 "Any second rider. Held back out of sight."
 "None that I saw."
 "How was he dressed."
 "A travelling cloak. No armour showing under it. He carried a case for documents on his back."
 Tee'aahta sat with this for a while before she spoke again, the falx flat across her knees.
 "A courier gets stopped at that gate the same as anyone else," she said. "Every man who's come down that road since we've been watching it has been made to give his name first. This one wasn't. That means someone at Lorkh already knew he was coming, and had told the gate to let him straight through."
 "Either that, or they recognised him," To'ria said. "Perhaps he's an officer sent from another fort."
 "An officer travels with at least one man at his back, usually more," Tee'aahta said. "This one came alone, with nothing but supplies and papers. That rules out an officer. It could still be a man the gate crew knows by sight — but a familiar face doesn't explain the case of documents, or why he came alone instead of with whatever business usually brings him to Lorkh. I'd rather read him as someone sent to look at what happened here and write it down — someone who answers to an office above the Pryfectus, not under him."
 "So he's here to ask questions," Thilda said.
 "That's my reading of it," Tee'aahta said. "And if he's been sent from outside Lorkh's own chain, it means whatever the Pryfectus already reported was thought serious enough for someone further up to check it in person, rather than take his word for it."
 To'ria said nothing for a moment. "Tig'rah-ta'vaa, you said something like this yourself, the night after we let the Pryfectus find Markellus. That a frightened system doesn't only lose its nerve. Sometimes it looks harder instead."
 "That certainly seems to be what's happening," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said.
 "This is what that looks like," Tee'aahta said. "It's also what we wanted. To'ria, I recall you saying yourself, the first morning I rode with any of you — not to beat Rome in open ground, but to make the men running it start doubting the ground they're standing on. A man like this doesn't get sent unless someone above the Pryfectus already doubts him. That's exactly the doubt we set out to cause. It doesn't mean we've won." She paused, then added, "Yet."
 She looked slowly around the group before continuing. "It simply means the stakes are higher, and that's exactly what we want."


 Chapter 38. — Seven and Eight.

 Ligahrius spent the first day on testimony, which included spending time with Markellus himself.
 He had asked to see the centurion and was taken to him — a man sitting upright, watched over, silent, eyes open and fixed on nothing. The physician had nothing to add beyond what the second dispatch had already said, but Ligahrius was certain he could see a spark of knowledge in the otherwise vacant stare. He asked if he could be alone with the centurion, and the guard withdrew to the outer chamber, closing the door behind him.
 Ligahrius sat opposite Markellus for a time, saying nothing. He watched his eyes, and he could tell Markellus was not entirely catatonic, yet there was something strange about his stare. He tried to reason with him, asking what he remembered of the ambush. Markellus lifted his gaze momentarily, then his eyes dropped again.
 He asked him what he remembered of the six days in the camp of the attackers. For a moment, he thought he had broken through. He was sure he saw Markellus forming the word "attackers" with his lips, but there was nothing more.
 Ligahrius decided to change tack. He stood over Markellus now and raised his voice.
 "Does Diskepleena mean nothing to you?" he shouted. "How can you claim to be a centurion when you allow your men to walk back to camp in disarray and without you? A Roman soldier holds his order even in captivity. How much more a centurion?"
 Markellus faintly shook his head, but there was no other response.
 "Where is your Virtus? I've seen the dress. How could you have allowed such a thing?" He leaned down and spoke directly into Markellus's ear. "And your Fama is destroyed. Do you have no regard for your father's house? Have you not thought of the man who put his confidence in you? You have brought shame on yourself, and your cousin, the Pryfectus, is a ruined man. Do you not care about how your men will remember you?"
 He sat down again, opposite Markellus, and watched him. For a few moments there appeared to be no response. Then Markellus lifted his head and looked Ligahrius in the eye.
 "Feminitas outweighs Diskepleena," he said. "I have seen Virtus in women beyond anything you or I will ever have. Fama has no more substance than the morning mist."
 Then his chin dropped to his chest, and he said no more.
 Ligahrius sat watching him for a few moments longer, turning the words over in his mind, but he couldn't make any sense of them. Whatever fever had produced them, it wasn't anything he'd encountered before. He rose silently, and left the chamber.

 The second day he asked for an escort to take him out to the western ground, but the Pryfectus was unable to raise one. The men simply refused, one excuse after another, until the Pryfectus ordered Rufeenus to select four men he could trust, and give them the option of forming the escort or immediate dishonourable discharge. Rufeenus was also ordered to accompany the escort.
 The clearing where the ambushes had taken place was exactly as described in both dispatches — ground churned wide by more than one fight, and drag marks all running the same direction, toward where the recovery party's carts must have stood. All of this was as he expected, but there was absolutely no clue as to the identity of the assailants.
 After he had combed the ambush site, he asked the escort to take him to the place where they had found Markellus. The escort brought him within sight of the camp and refused to go any further. They simply would not take another step closer. Rufeenus reiterated the threat of immediate dishonourable discharge, but all four said they would rather dishonourable discharge than face what Markellus had faced. Ligahrius had never witnessed terror like this in seasoned soldiers before. Even Rufeenus insisted that he needed to stay with the men to prevent them running. So Ligahrius approached the camp alone.
 He stepped cautiously out of the tree line, into the clearing that was clearly a lived-in camp, laid out exactly as the second dispatch had described it — the fire was burning low, eight bedrolls arranged around it, five horses tethered nearby, calm and well-cared-for.
 He looked again — eight bedrolls.
 The lad had said seven. Ligahrius had gone back over that testimony more carefully than any other he'd taken, because it was the only account not given in the middle of a fight, yet here were eight bedrolls. Either the lad had been in error, or an eighth had joined them. Could this be the Caledonian captive who vanished?
 He knew the woman only by report: three years a captive, taken from Caledonia, kept for display rather than labour. He had built his working picture of this band from the only material available to him — local, frontier-born women, if they were indeed women, who shared a grudge against the local Roman fort, but a Caledonian would have no place among them.
 She had no kin on this frontier. No tribe here that he knew of had connections with Caledonia. By every account, she had stood three years at a wall in that fort and said nothing to anyone beyond what was required of her. He couldn't fathom how a foreigner could join a band of locals, who would then trust her enough to fight beside her the very next day. In his experience, that kind of loyalty took years to build.
 Even granting the eight — granting Tahran, granting whatever local grievance had raised the rest — he could not make the numbers answer for what had been done to Markellus's century. Two ambush points had worked in coordination, timed apart just far enough to split the response. Every horse in a mounted division had gone down inside minutes, in a fight fifty men had not been able to hold together long enough to protect their own mounts. A centurion had been taken from under the noses of his own escort, and not one man among fifty had seen it happen. This was command — someone reading the ground, dividing the labour, timing the strikes to land when the confusion was thickest. He had questioned officers, in three provinces, who could not have managed that with a century of trained men under them.
 He walked back to the fort as the light went, putting the report into its final order in his head. He would write what he had, plainly, the way a report should be written. Seven confirmed at the first engagement, on the word of a released witness he judged reliable. A camp, found after, showing eight. A captive missing from the Pryfectus's own household that same night — the timing noted, the connection unproven. Coordination in the second engagement beyond what he would expect of an undisciplined local band, suggesting direction from a source not yet identified. He would ask, before he left Lorkh, that a wider search be made west and south of the fort, and that Markellus be questioned again the day he found his voice, if he ever did.
 He would leave one thing out of the report: that eight women alone might have done all of it. He turned the sentence over as he walked and could not make himself set it to wax. The ground had told him plainly enough. What he lacked was a place in his own mind built to hold it. A man writes down what he has a place for. He had none for this.


 Chapter 39. — Watched in Turn.

 Thilda held the ridge above the fort's east gate, the morning after the outsider had arrived.
 It was about mid-morning when the escort came out, four men on foot, one centurion and the rider Tig'rah-ta'vaa had seen the previous morning. They were keeping a steady walking pace, and were heading directly for the ambush site.
 She was back at camp within the half hour.
 "There's an escort heading this way," she said. "Four foot soldiers, one centurion and the stranger."
 Tee'aahta was already on her feet. "Not a search."
 "Not a search," Thilda said. "They are headed directly for the ambush site."
 They left the camp standing and moved to the ground above the clearing, keeping to the tree line the whole way. Below them, Ligahrius worked the churned earth on foot, crouching at the drag marks, following them out to where the recovery carts had stood. He worked the site methodically, noting all his findings on a wax tablet he kept in his document pouch.
 "He's thorough," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said.
 "None of the other five have spoken to him since they left the gate," Tee'aahta said.
 When Ligahrius straightened and turned to the escort, asking them to take him to the camp, the band followed, staying out of earshot.
 The escort's nerve broke within sight of the camp.
 Rufeenus appeared to be ordering the four soldiers to continue on, but none of them moved. Ligahrius waited, saying nothing, while the argument played out in front of him. In the end they watched Ligahrius continue alone.
 Tee'aahta turned to alert Tig'rah-ta'vaa to prepare her bow, and found her with bow half-drawn, and arrow tracking Ligahrius across the open ground. Tee'aahta nodded and said nothing.
 He stood at the edge of the clearing a while before he moved further in, taking in the fire, the horses, the bedrolls, without touching any of it. He crouched by the fire once, close enough to feel its heat, and didn't disturb the ash. They watched him counting the bedrolls, moving his lips.
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa's bow didn't waver the whole time.
 He left the way he'd come, unhurried, and joined the escort to return to the fort.
 "The eight bedrolls seemed to interest him," Tov-aeh said, with a chuckle in her voice.
 "That's it," Tahna said. "The boy from the first patrol would have reported seven, and nobody in that second attack would have been able to count anything."
 Tahran's jaw was tight. "He'll figure out I'm here, won't he?"
 "You've left Lorkh, that much he knows," Tee'aahta said. "But for him to believe that you're still this close, he'll be thinking either he's mad, or you are." She held Tahran's gaze a moment. "Rome won't attack what it doesn't believe in."


 Chapter 40. — Three Sides.

 "I think it's time we paid our new friend a visit," Tov-aeh said. "He came all this way. Be rude not to return the courtesy."
 "Gatecrashing seems impolite," To'ria said. "We should send word ahead. Let them know we're coming."
 "We could have sent it with those courier boys who just left," Tahna said.
 Tov-aeh grinned at that. "I must say, it would be nice to have a warm welcome when we visit."
 Tahran had been quiet, working a whetstone along one blade. She looked up.
 "Tig'rah-ta'vaa and I could send messages from both north and south," she said. "The same as we did with the century."
 Thilda turned that over for a moment. "That would work. Better still if we follow it with a patrol strike." She looked at Tee'aahta. "What do you say, Tee'aahta?"
 "I think we could make it work," Tee'aahta said. She turned to Tasset'uh. "Where would you put the strike?"
 Tasset'uh lifted her gladius and pointed it due east.
 "I like that idea," Tee'aahta said. "A patrol strike on the east would really upset the balance if we accompanied it with the shots on either flank. We'll want to get the timing right, though."
 "There's an eastern patrol that usually returns to the fort just on sundown," Thilda said. "I've already got a good ambush site in mind."
 "I reckon I know a couple of good vantage points, north and south, where the sentries on the ramparts are easily within striking distance," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said. "The wind around sundown is usually from the north-west, so the north shot will probably be the easier of the two. Do you have any preference, Tahran?"
 Tahran thought for a moment. "I'd like to try the south shot."
 "OK," Tee'aahta said, "sounds like you two have that part under control."
 She looked around the group. "The plan will be: strike the patrol, release a witness, split north and south." She paused. "We need to synchronise these two shots, so I'm thinking Tasset'uh could tail the witness to the fort, then signal the two of you the moment he enters the eastern gate."
 Tasset'uh looked across at Tee'aahta. She nodded once, and the corner of her mouth twitched.
 Tov-aeh had been quiet through the planning, turning something over. "There's a second part to this, if you want it," she said. "They've been in this camp twice now. If we accompany tonight's strike with a camp shift, they won't know where to look for us next."
 Tee'aahta considered that. "You might be onto something there. Any suggestions of where to go?"
 "Well, yes actually," Tov-aeh said. "There's a hollow to the south of the fort with good water, thick cover, and a rise behind it giving a clear line down onto the fort below."
 "I think I know the spot you mean," Tahran said. "I was looking at it yesterday."
 Tee'aahta stood up. "We'd better be packing up, then."

 They broke camp within the hour. Tov-aeh led them south, to the new camp site. It was mid-afternoon by the time the last of the gear was settled and the horses picketed.
 From there Thilda led them east, toward the ground she'd already walked twice. They reached it with the light beginning to slant low through the trees, and took the positions she'd chosen.
 The patrol came round the bend to find their path blocked. Thilda's shield filled half the width of the track, Tahna's oval shield locking in beside it. The patrol paused before raising their own shields.
 Tahran dropped from the branches overhead with a scream that even caught Tee'aahta off guard. She landed in the middle of their formation with her twin blades flying. Tov-aeh came in low on one flank, axe swinging, and To'ria appeared on the other side, falcata leveled.
 One man at the rear suddenly felt cold steel at his throat and a harsh rasp in his ear. "Stay."
 Tee'aahta and Tig'rah-ta'vaa stepped up and relieved him of his blade and shield, and began to strip him of his armour, leaving him in only his under-tunic.
 "We haven't gone anywhere, have we?" said Tee'aahta. "Off you go now, and tell your visitor we look forward to meeting him. Walk, don't run, and if you value your life, don't look behind you. Now, go."
 She turned him to face the fort and let him go.
 He began walking immediately, and Tasset'uh silently fell in behind him.
 To'ria's forearm had taken a blade, deeper than she'd first thought. Tov-aeh noticed it first, and immediately attended to her.
 "You'll live," Tov-aeh said, already binding the arm. "But you're done for today."
 Tov-aeh and Tahna walked with her back toward the new camp, To'ria leaning on Tov-aeh's shoulder.

 The rest split without discussion. Tee'aahta went north with Tig'rah-ta'vaa. Thilda went south with Tahran. Both pairs had marked their ground already, and both had a clear line to the same point east, where the returning soldier would have to pass on his way home.
 Tasset'uh went alone, following the young man's track at a distance.
 She watched him all the way to the gate. The moment he was admitted, she raised the gladius and held it there until she saw the response from both directions.

 Tahran had the sentry on the southern rampart in sight before the signal came. She loosed her arrow the moment she saw Tasset'uh raise her gladius, and the man folded without a sound.
 To the north, Tig'rah-ta'vaa's arrow found its mark in the same breath.
 Both women were already moving before either man had collapsed, gone before a shout could be raised on either wall.

 They came into the new camp after dark, Tasset'uh first, followed by the other two pairs. To'ria sat close to the fire, her arm bound and resting across her lap, watching the others settle.
 "Four on the road," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said, dropping to sit. "One north, one south. All in less time than it takes to walk a survivor through a gate."
 "He'll have barely finished passing on our regards," Tahna said.
 Tee'aahta looked around the fire at all of them, and let the quiet hold before she spoke.
 "I wonder how soon their visitor will be leaving," she said. "There isn't a side of that fort he can trust."


 Chapter 41. — Every Side.

 Ligahrius had sent his report north that afternoon, sealed and given to a courier bound for Mogoon-tee-aahkoom. He meant to follow within the week. His part at Lorkh was done, and he had told the Pryfectus so over the evening meal.
 The Pryfectus had ordered the best wine he'd yet put in front of him. The talk ran easier than it had since Ligahrius arrived — old postings, a mutual acquaintance at Mogoon-tee-aahkoom, nothing that touched the western ground.
 Suddenly, shouts rose from every side — north, east, south.
 The Pryfectus was on his feet and moving toward the yard with Ligahrius close behind him, out through the praetorium and into the evening air.
 Men were running toward the north rampart stair. Others were running toward the south. A knot of soldiers stood crowding around the eastern gate, and a runner was crossing the open ground at a sprint, heading directly for the Pryfectus.
 "Sir." The runner reached them out of breath. "North wall. A sentry's down. Arrow. From the north-east ridge."
 A second man arrived before the first had finished speaking. "South wall the same, sir. Both of them, struck at the same moment."
 The Pryfectus was visibly shaken. He turned toward the gate, where the knot of soldiers had begun to move, and Ligahrius went with him.
 They parted to let the Pryfectus through. In the middle of them stood a soldier stripped to his under-tunic, skin raised in gooseflesh in the night chill.
 The Pryfectus had seen this before. It was the same pattern as the first strike, several weeks earlier.
 "Report," the Pryfectus said.
 "Five of us went out on the eastern loop." The soldier's voice was halting. "We didn't come back on time. They were waiting for us on the bend before the ford. I'm the one they sent back with a message."
 "How many," the Pryfectus said.
 "I never saw more than four. Could have been more I didn't see."
 "Weapons."
 "I don't know, sir. It was fast."
 Ligahrius stepped in before the Pryfectus could ask the next question, and the Pryfectus let him.
 "You said they sent you back with a message," Ligahrius said. "What was the message?"
 "The big one said to tell your visitor we look forward to meeting him."
 Ligahrius paused at this. "Is that all they said?"
 "No, sir," said the soldier. "They said 'We haven't gone anywhere, have we?'"
 "And the north wall," Ligahrius said. "The south. Did they say anything of those."
 "No. Nothing."
 Ligahrius looked first to the north stair, then to the south, then to the gate where the soldier stood. He said nothing for a moment.
 "Bring me the men who saw the wall shots," Ligahrius said. "Whoever was nearest each one. I want them separately, and I want them now."
 The order went out through Rufeenus. Within a quarter hour Ligahrius had two men in front of him, in the record room off the praetorium, the Pryfectus standing by while Ligahrius asked the questions.
 Both accounts agreed on the key points. Neither man had seen anything at the tree line, or heard a sound before the sentry beside him fell. Both had the timing to within moments of each other. Neither could say from what distance the shot had come, only that it came from deep within in the tree line.
 Ligahrius set down his stylus and closed his eyes for a moment before he spoke again.
 "Two dead on the wall," he said. "Struck within the same breath, on two sides of this fort that face away from each other. A patrol taken on a third side, the same evening, by the same method that was used on the first attack. One survivor released, stripped, sent back walking." He looked up at the Pryfectus. "I've spent the last two days telling myself this was contained to the ground west your walls. I don't know what to think now."
 "Then it's not seven women in a forest," the Pryfectus said. "It's something that can stand at my own walls and put arrows through my sentries."
 "It's still seven women," Ligahrius said. "Or eight. I haven't changed my count. What's changed is where I believed the edge of their reach to be." He stood. "I know where their camp is. I've stood in it myself. And by every report I've gathered since I arrived, it hasn't moved in over two weeks."
 "You think that carelessness."
 "I think it confidence, possibly overconfidence," Ligahrius said. "This garrison has refused to set foot on that ground since well before I arrived. They've had two proofs now that nobody from this fort will follow them home. A band that bold, sitting that long in one place, isn't hiding from you. It's decided it doesn't need to." He paused. "Tonight, while every man in this yard is looking at his own dead, that camp is very likely unwatched. They've had no reason yet to think we'd dare attack."
 The Pryfectus looked at him for a long moment.
 "You're proposing we go to them."
 "Before sunrise tomorrow," Ligahrius said.


 Chapter 42. — Torches.

 Tov-aeh had the last watch before dawn the following morning, and it was still over an hour till first light when the torches in the fort began to be lit.
 The fort usually held no torches through the night, not since the first week Tig'rah-ta'vaa had watched it. Tonight the wall was lit end to end, and men were moving in the yard.
 She went back to alert Tee'aahta.
 "Something's happening at the fort," she said. "Torches lit along the whole wall. The yard is alive with men on the move."
 Tee'aahta was on her feet without a word, and the rest followed, watching in silence as a column formed in the pre-dawn dark — foot and horse both, more men than either recovery party had mustered.
 They watched the column pass through the gate and take its bearing.
 "They're headed for the old site," Tahna said.
 "They are indeed," Tee'aahta said.
 She turned to Tov-aeh. "You had us move camp yesterday. What did you see that I didn't?"
 "I really don't know," Tov-aeh paused. "I just didn't like how quiet it had been."
 "Keep not liking it," Tee'aahta said. "It's useful."
 To'ria had been watching the gate rather than the departing column. "I reckon more than half that garrison just walked out its own front door," she said. "That's an opportunity we shouldn't miss."
 "We can't take the fort," Tahran said.
 "Nobody's suggesting that," said To'ria. "But we can give them a surprise to come home to."
 They talked through it standing where they were — the gate, the granary, the stables. It was Tig'rah-ta'vaa who finally had an idea that might work.
 "There are two ridges to the north that look straight down into the yard," she said. "I've marked them before. Those torches they've lit for the muster — I can put an arrow into more than one from up there. If enough come down in the right place, that yard is going to catch fire."
 "You're certain of the range," Tee'aahta said.
 "I am."
 "Of course you are," Tee'aahta said. "I needn't have asked."
 "No harm in certainty," said Tig'rah-ta'vaa.
 She turned to Tahran. "I reckon you could knock some torches, too."
 "Gladly," said Tahran, as she began to collect her gear.
 They went east first, keeping the fort's bulk between themselves and the column, then cut north and climbed the ridge. From their vantage point, the yard sat open beneath them, torches burning along the wall and a scatter more further in, near the stores.
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa took the first. The torch left the bracket and rolled, still burning, into what looked like stacked fodder. Tahran matched her a moment later, and then again, until five had come down. One went out on the packed earth, but the others found fuel.
 They made their way around to the north-west ridge, and once again, the yard opened out beneath them.
 Three more torches found a resting place among flammable material, and it wasn't long before the stables began to burn.
 They found a rise close enough to watch both the fort and the road the column had taken, and waited.
 The fire took hold slowly, at first. Figures ran along the wall with buckets, but the flames were soon showing over the rampart.
 The column came back into view shortly after sunrise, all semblance of order gone.


 Chapter 43. — Seven Fires.

 Drahco had watched the column go, the gate barred behind it. The morning should be simple enough: hold the walls and wait for word.
 The yard settled quickly once the dust from the column's departure cleared. What remained of the garrison went back to its rhythms — the ovens, the stores. Drahco walked the wall himself once, start to finish. The column was in the field now, chasing down whatever had been haunting them. By evening, or tomorrow at the latest, it would be finished.
 A short time later, he was in the record room, going through the week's requisitions with the clerk, when the first shout was raised.
 "Fire!"
 Within moments another shout was raised, then a third. Drahco reached the yard, already filling with running men, as a fourth shout echoed from the walls.
 Smoke was rising from the granary roof, and even as he watched, a fresh plume rose from further along, near the stores shed.
 "Water!" he shouted. "Get the cistern open. Buckets, anything that holds water, move!"
 Men ran for the cistern. By the time the first buckets reached the granary, another fire had caught in a stack of tent leather near the south wall.
 The first fire was almost smothered when a fifth fire caught in a supply cart near the east wall, flame running up its canvas cover.
 Then a sixth caught near the armoury door.
 Then the heap of fodder behind the stables went up.
 The stables themselves were past saving. The roof was gone to flame end to end. Horses screamed somewhere inside it, and the men who had gone in there to save them soon came out choking on the smoke.
 Seven fires, and not one man in that yard had seen a single one start.
 That was when the shouting reached him from beyond the wall.
 It couldn't be the column. They'd barely been gone two hours — nowhere near long enough to have found the western band, destroyed them all, and ridden back. Whoever was out there at the gates weren't friendly.
 The Alemaani had been pressing this whole stretch of frontier for a season now. Other forts along the line had gone the same way — overrun in an hour, garrisons that should have held for at least a day were gone before help could reach them. Drahco had seen some of the reports. Now, with more than half the garrison outside the walls and the remnant fighting fires, it looked exactly like the kind of attacks those reports described.
 "Archers!" he shouted, already moving for the rampart stair himself. "Get me archers on that wall, now!"
 Several men peeled off from the fire lines and ran for the stair behind him, bows snatched up on the way.
 He reached the rampart just as the first volley went out.
 Through the smoke drifting low along the wall, the ground before the gate was thick with figures, milling and shouting.
 He gave the order again, and a second volley went out into the haze, aimed at the thickest of the movement below.
 A horse screamed somewhere in the murk beyond the gate. Then a man's voice, high and furious, cut through the noise — a voice he recognised.

 The Pryfectus had smelled the smoke before the fort came into sight — thin and acrid. He'd pushed the column hard, and by the time Lorkh's walls rose up ahead of them through the haze, he could see the smoke was coming from inside the fort.
 He approached the walls and called for the gate captain to open up, but there was no response. After a few more moments of men shouting at the gate, a hail of arrows came down out of the smoke.
 The man riding just ahead of him went down with a shaft through the shoulder, his horse bolting into the column and throwing two more into confusion.
 "Hold!" the Pryfectus shouted, though nobody heard him over the shouting — a second volley, closer to the mark this time, and another man screaming as his horse went down beneath him.
 "CEASE!" he roared, standing in his stirrups, both arms up. "It's the Pryfectus! Hold your arrows — it's the column, it's your own men—"
 His voice didn't carry far enough, and a third volley of arrows rained down on them.

 They watched from the ridge as confusion spread through the fort.
 "That's more than we lit," Thilda said.
 "It's spreading fast," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said. "One fire feeding the next and hardly a soul left inside to fight it."
 Tov-aeh said nothing for a while, just watched the stables collapse in on themselves, a column of sparks going up where the roof had been.
 It was Tee'aahta who caught the column first, and said nothing until the first volley came down out of the smoke along the wall.
 "They're firing on their own," she said.
 Below, a second volley found its mark, and even at this distance the sound of the column breaking apart in confusion carried up to them — horses screaming, men shouting.
 Tahna laughed. "We didn't do that part."
 "No," Tee'aahta said. "They managed that bit themselves." She watched a moment longer. "That's worth more to us than the fire."


 Chapter 44. — The Fall of Lorkh.

 It took another volley, and a fourth horse screaming down under its rider, before the Pryfectus's shouting finally cut through.
 By the time the gate opened, Drahco was there to meet him, soot-blackened and drenched.
 "Sir." Drahco kept his voice steady. "Seven fires. Nobody saw a one of them start. The stables are gone. The stores and the armoury are half destroyed."
 The Pryfectus rode through into the yard — smoke hanging low over everything, flames still visible along the stores shed and the stables, men dashing across with buckets of water.
 "How many dead," he said.
 "I haven't had time to check, sir," Drahco said. "I fear more were lost from the arrows than the fires. I gave the order myself, not knowing who was riding up out of the smoke. I hadn't expected you back so soon."
 The Pryfectus turned away without another word.
 He walked the yard alone — the granary roof was gone, the stores shed was still ablaze, and the stables were a pile of burning timber, the screaming from within beginning to die down.
 Rufeenus found him, near what was left of the armoury door.
 "Sir. What are your orders?"
 The Pryfectus said nothing. He was still looking at the stables, where the mangled bodies of horses were visible through the flames.
 It was Ligahrius who answered, coming up beside them, a streak of ash across one cheek, his document case still slung across his back.
 "There won't be another report from this desk, Pryfectus," he said. "I've seen enough here myself now. I'll write what I witnessed, which is a fort that can no longer hold its own wall, doesn't know its own enemy, and just put arrows into its own returning column. I have the authority to give this order. Lorkh is finished as a position. We're wasting our time trying to put these fires out — it's too far gone. I want the fort evacuated by nightfall."
 The Pryfectus turned to look at him.
 "You'd have me give up a Roman fort?"
 "This fort has already given up on itself," Ligahrius said. "You lost it the moment your own archers couldn't tell friend from foe through the smoke. And besides, there'll be nothing left to hold, even if we do get the fires out."
 The Pryfectus held Ligahrius's gaze a moment longer, then looked away, back toward the wreckage of the stables.
 "Orders," he said. "Let the fires burn. Begin packing the carts. Wounded first, then whatever stores we can salvage."

 They watched from the ridge through the better part of the afternoon.
 Below them, the fires still raged as carts were being loaded at the gate.
 "Well, they've decided they aren't staying," Thilda said.
 "So it appears," Tee'aahta said. "They're not wasting any time about it, either."
 To'ria watched a moment longer, her bound arm resting against her side. "That's the end of that garrison."
 "What's left of it," Tig'rah-ta'vaa said.
 Tahna folded her arms, a look of amusement on her face. "We didn't take that fort."
 "No," Tee'aahta said. "They gave it up."
 The column moved off just before sunset, and Tahran watched until it had shrunk to a thin dark line on the road east.
 "He's in there somewhere — riding out of the only place I've known for three years." She paused. "I wonder if he knows I'm watching."
 The group was quiet for a while before Tee'aahta spoke. "They'll write this up as something else," she said. "Alemaani, most likely — that's what's been eating this frontier all year, and it's an easier thing for Rome to believe than the truth of what actually happened here. Let them. It doesn't matter what name goes on it. All that matters is that Fort Lorkh is gone."
 Tov-aeh was shaking her head. "I can't believe they just let it burn."
 Then Tasset'uh spoke.
 "Let it burn," she said. "Let all of Rome burn."


 Author's Note.

 Fort Lorkh was a real Roman garrison. It sat at a point known today as the Limesknie — literally, the "limes-knee," the bend where the Upper Germanic limes turned sharply east to meet the Raetian limes on its way to the Danube. Genu is the Latin word for knee, and it's the word Tahran uses for it in this book.
 The site lies beneath the present-day town of Lorkh, which has prevented the same kind of full excavation carried out at other forts along the frontier, such as Zugmantel and the Limes Gate at Dalkingen. The only part of the fort still visible above ground is a single tower base at the west gate. The last significant excavation was carried out in the 1980s, and found the remains preserved under about 1.5 metres of hillside silt and more recent construction.
 The wider collapse of the frontier referenced in this book is also historical, known as the Limesfall. The worst of it came later than this book's setting, mainly in the 250s AD, but pressure from the Alemaani had already begun by 233. Several forts near Lorkh, including the Limes Gate at Dalkingen, were burned and abandoned around this same period.


 Character Index.


 Tahna.
 Origin: Greek.
 Meaning: "death".

 Tahna is the first of the eight introduced to us on the sand of the Koo'neggy'on arena in By-zantium. One of Servius's many gladiatrices, she had been taken by pirates from her hometown on the coast of Greece. She was fourteen at the time, from a family of good standing and modest means.
 As a child, she had excelled in her lessons, her exacting and analytical nature finding its true passion in arithmetica. This preternatural ability to solve problems instantly, using any means available, served her well in her role as gladiatrix, allowing her to walk victorious from eleven death matches. Over time, her ruthless calculating directness hardened into a smug, cynical outlook on life.
 In combat, she handles her xiphos with calculated dexterity, unafraid to step into close hand-to-hand bouts, prepared to fall back on her xiphidion if required. Even at a distance, she is deadly accurate with her akontia.


 To'ria.
 Origin: Iberian.
 Meaning: "conqueror".

 To'ria is the second of the eight we see, first shown to us as opponent of Tahna, set to battle to the death, before Tasset'uh intervenes. She has been under the brutal hand of Servius for longer than the others, having been taken as a small child when Roman soldiers were sent to quell unrest in her native province on the Iberian Peninsula. She remembers little more than the sound of her mother's voice being cut off mid-word, and the smoke that did not belong in that hillside village.
 Her outgoing, fun-loving nature was severely curtailed during her years at the Koo'neggy'on in By-zantium, forcing an underlying dominance to the fore. This served her well during those years, building in her a fiery spontaneity, a quick eye and a lethally fast hand.
 In combat, she will face down the toughest of enemies, her falcata leaving a trail of destruction. Through her hastiness, she is prone to put herself in harm's way, but rarely does she remain there long enough for harm to reach her. So ready is she to lead in close combat, that the spear she carries seldom serves any greater purpose than to simply be there if required.


 Tasset'uh.
 Origin: Roman.
 Meaning: "silent" or "secret".

 Tasset'uh is introduced in the Koo'neggy'on arena in By-zantium. Owned by Servius, she has been a gladiatrix there for three years, after being sold by her father to settle a debt.
 Although she was already a quiet girl by nature, the betrayal of paternal duty forced her to build an unbreakable resilience through silence. She has an unplumbed depth of emotion — a unique understanding of hardship that, though silent, does not refrain from offering her own peculiar form of compassion. She had always been steady and precise, but due to her past and her extreme self-discipline, these features have sharpened to lethality.
 Her role in combat is never on display. She is the silent steady hand holding the blade that doesn't so much appear as just suddenly exist at a throat. She acts as the silencer of the survivor, and the extractor of the hostage. Her gladius is more an extension of her presence than a weapon, when in close combat, and a handful of plumbatae carried at the belt are reserved for silencing from a distance.



 Tig'rah-ta'vaa.
 Origin: Sarmatian.
 Meaning: "with the strength of an arrow".

 We first see Tig'rah-ta'vaa as she tracks a Roman patrol on the Sarmatian steppes. Born into the Roxolani tribe, she is the sole survivor of her family, after her father and the forty men who rode with him were disarmed and killed by the Roman officers they had ridden with for the previous eleven years. The blame for a Roman raid on a village north of their territory was laid on them, and Tig'rah-ta'vaa, who had been moving herds to the east at the time, returned to a burned camp and forty unburied dead. From that time on, she had fended for herself, surviving by pillaging passing Roman patrols and supply trains.
 Her natural analytical precision served her well through those lonely years, and her patient observation allowed her to choose her battles well. This same reserved caution has made her the ideal scout for the band.
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa is often the one to open combat, precisely placing one of her trilobate arrow heads through the throat of an enemy, sending it from her composite recurve bow, and usually from concealment. When called upon to assist in close combat, she is no less adept at wielding her longsword.


 Tee'aahta.
 Origin: Dacian.
 Meaning: "daughter of the strong".

 Tee'aahta is a larger-than-life mystery, first seen openly displaying her latest victim. The mystery is her past, which she has never cared to share, and has never been asked to reveal, however, hints of it are visible in her unmitigated vendetta against the Roman surveyor — any Roman surveyor. It hints at a past that has built in her a controlled yet bitter resentment of the Roman partitioning of the Dacian territory.
 Her self-sufficiency is predominant when we are introduced to her personal campsite, where everything is arranged decisively to serve its most practical use. She wears her dominance openly, yet her natural conscientiousness leads her to seek the opinions of others before making her final decisions. This habit of leading by listening softens her otherwise superior bearing, making of her a trusted and trusting unranked commander.
 In combat, her initial role is in planning. Her direct and decisive manner ensures everyone plays their part correctly. When it comes to the battlefield, her size and her two-handed falx leave nothing standing before her. The sica sheathed at her hip is mostly there for domestic use, and rarely sees daylight when the falx is at hand.


 Tov-aeh.
 Origin: Scandinavian.
 Meaning: "beautiful thunder".

 Tov-aeh is first seen in the forest between the Iazyges pastures and the Hasdingi holdings, unbothered by an hour of being watched from the tree line. Rugii by birth, from a cold coastal homeland far to the north, she was taken by Gothic raiders and traded onward to Hasdingi hands. She allowed her captors to underestimate her until the opportunity presented itself for her to flee, and had been alone in the forest since.
 Tov-aeh's warmth and affability are her most disarming traits, inviting the band to join her on their first meeting and willingly sharing her meal. She's the first to welcome a newcomer, and the first to offer help to the wounded.
 In combat, Tov-aeh is willing to lead the strike, her raw focus and the brutality of her axe carrying the weight of her past into every battle. She discovered that revenge alone offered no relief to the pain of her past, and even her affability can be weaponised by using it as a patronising corrosion to an enemy iron soul.


 Thilda.
 Origin: Germanic.
 Meaning: "strength in battle".

 Thilda comes onto view scouting along a stretch of border, when she comes across the campfire of the band of six women. Born to a people who held relatively resource-poor ground in Upper Germania, she fought first under self-important commanders, and later alone, coming to know both Rome's patient encroachment from the south and the raiding tribes pressing down from the north. We are not told what happened to her people other than that she had buried more of her own than she could remember.
 Whatever the hardships were that she faced during her early years, they had only served to strengthen and stabilise a quiet confidence in her own ability to read the landscape. The caution first seen in her covert observation of the others around their campfire was no less present in her bold approach, after deciding they were worth investigating.
 In combat, Thilda will holds the line, her tower shield planted as solidly as herself, an immovable object against which seemingly unstoppable forces break themselves. She is unafraid to use her framea in direct attacks and will turn to the spatha when close combat requires it.


 Tahran.
 Origin: Caledonian.
 Meaning: "thunder".

 Tahran is the last of the eight, first seen while still in captivity at Fort Lorkh, standing silent by a wall as the Pryfectus displays her to visiting officers as proof of Rome's control even beyond the frontiers. Taken from Caledonia as an exotic curiosity, she has spent three years treated, in her own words, like a hound that had learned to heel. She breaks her captivity the night a frightened soldier's account of an ambush reaches the household, lifting weapons from the Pryfectus's own armoury before scaling the fort wall in the dead of night.
 From the little we are told about her life in Caledonia, we can see that her speed and dexterity was more than a match for a Roman soldier. This fiery-tempered nature never burned out during her captivity, and even the practice blades she was proffered were used to keep her skills sharp. Over the three years of captivity, she learned a unique cold patience that now acts as a faucet through which she channels her frenzy, however, it never chilled her natural warmth which resurfaces the moment she allows herself to relax into Tov-aeh's embrace.
 In combat, Tahran appears to be the embodiment of a contradiction. She has the immense patience required to be a highly skilled archer, and other times, she's a screaming banshee who drops from overhead onto five armed men with her twin sgian already flying. The tuagh she has at her belt has not yet seen action since Caledonia, but would no doubt prove as deadly in her hands as her other weapons.
