 Chapter 16. — Roxolani.

 Morning came grey and mild, and Thilda woke to find the camp already stirring around her.
 It was Tee'aahta who made the introductions, moving around the fire with the same flat economy that marked her every action.
 "To'ria," she said, nodding to the one with the falcata. "Iberian-born. Taken as a girl when Rome put down trouble in her province. Gladiatrix out of By-zantium before any of the rest of this started."
 To'ria didn't look up from the blade, but the twitch in her shoulders acknowledged it all the same.
 "Tahna," Tee'aahta went on, nodding to the one with the easy, sharp smile. "Greek. Taken by pirates off her own coast at fourteen, sold into the same arena. Eleven bouts survived on nothing but reading a fight faster than her opponent."
 "Flattering, when you say it like that," Tahna said, though she didn't disagree.
 "Tasset'uh." A level glance from the quiet one was the whole of her greeting. "The breakout that got the three of them out of By-zantium was hers, start to finish. She usually lets her gladius speak for her."
 Tasset'uh's only reply was the faintest incline of her head — confirmation, offered and withdrawn in the same breath.
 "Tov-aeh," she said, nodding to the broad-shouldered one with the axe, who grinned outright and passed over the last of the previous night's meat. "Rugii, from a long way north of anywhere on this frontier. Taken by Gothic raiders, traded on to Hasdingi hands, freed herself in the same patient way she does everything else."
 "And Tig'rah-ta'vaa," Tee'aahta finished, nodding to the one already half a distance apart from the rest, watching the tree line.
 Tee'aahta paused there. "Which is, I realise, a name I've often spoken, but have never heard the story behind," she said, half to Thilda, half to herself, an admission that seemed to genuinely surprise her.
 Tig'rah-ta'vaa's mouth curved, faint and dry. "You've never asked."
 "That's not an answer."
 "No," Tig'rah-ta'vaa agreed. "It isn't."
 She was quiet a moment, and when she finally spoke it was brief, exact, and nothing wasted.
 "Roxolani," she said. "One of the Sarmatian peoples. My family rode as auxiliary cavalry for Rome for eleven years. My father led forty riders. Paid in grain and little else. When a Roman patrol raided a village to the north, the governor needed someone else to blame. My father and his riders paid with their lives for something they didn't do." She paused. "I was moving the herd east at the time. Came back to a burned-out camp and forty unburied dead." She drew in breath and looked out into the distance.
 Nobody spoke.
 After a few moments, Tee'aahta said, "And you'd been alone since."
 "Alone by choice. There were other family groups I could have joined, but all were confederates of Rome." This last she said with obvious distaste, before brightening a bit and going on. "I watched these three for the better part of a day before I ever spoke to them. Decided they were worth the trouble before they knew I existed. That's simply how I do things. It served me well enough on my own. It's served well since."
 Tee'aahta studied her for a long moment, the same weighing look she gave everyone, and nodded once — the closest thing to a verdict she ever handed down.
 Thilda, watching all of it unfold around a fire she'd been sitting at for less than a full day, found herself, for the first time in years, in no particular hurry to be anywhere else.
